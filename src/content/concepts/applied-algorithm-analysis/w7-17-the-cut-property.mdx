Prim and Kruskal make greedy choices, and greedy choices lost with coins. The reason they
are correct here comes down to one idea.

## A cut is a division

Put every vertex on one side or the other. Nothing is deleted. On the four-town graph, take
`{a, b}` on the left and `{c, d}` on the right.

| Edge | Weight | Crosses? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `(a, b)` | 1 | no: both on the left |
| `(b, d)` | 2 | **yes** |
| `(a, d)` | 3 | **yes** |
| `(a, c)` | 4 | **yes** |
| `(c, d)` | 5 | no: both on the right |

A **crossing edge** has one endpoint in each group.

## The cheapest way across is safe

**Every spanning tree must cross every cut at least once.** If none of its edges crossed,
nothing on the left could reach anything on the right, so it would not be spanning.

So the MST has to pay for at least one crossing edge. The **cut property** says the cheapest
one is never a mistake:

> **A cheapest edge crossing a cut belongs to at least one MST.**

Here the cheapest crossing edge is `(b, d)` at 2, and it is indeed in the MST of weight 7.

The comparison is only among **crossing edges**. `(a, b)` costs less than `(b, d)`, but it
does not cross this cut, so it plays no part. Each cut has its own cheapest crossing edge.

## The intuition behind it

Suppose some MST does not use the cheapest crossing edge `e`. That tree still crosses the
cut somewhere, so add `e` to it. Now `e` and the tree's own route between `e`'s endpoints form
a cycle, and that cycle crosses the cut a second time, through some edge `f`. Remove `f`.
The result is still a spanning tree, and since `e` is no more expensive than `f`, it costs no
more. So there is an MST containing `e`. The full proof is in DPV, *Algorithms*, chapter 5.

## Two conditions

**Keep the selected edges together.** When the algorithm has already chosen some edges, the
cut must keep each selected component **on one side**. If `(a, b)` has been chosen, `a` and
`b` go on the same side. Then the cheapest edge connecting that group to the rest is safe to
add *together with* the edges already chosen. A cut that separated `a` from `b` could pick an
edge that, together with `(a, b)`, closes a cycle.

**Ties are allowed.** If two crossing edges share the lowest weight, either can be chosen,
and each belongs to some MST. That is why ties can lead to different MSTs with the same total.

## Why this makes greedy work

The coin rule failed because a good-looking step could leave a remainder that was expensive
to finish. The cut property rules that out for MSTs: taking the cheapest edge across a cut
never excludes an optimal answer. The next concept shows that each step of Prim and of
Kruskal takes the cheapest edge across some cut.

> Every spanning tree must cross every cut. The cheapest crossing edge is always part of some
> MST, provided the cut keeps the components already selected in one piece.
