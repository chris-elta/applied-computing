Both LIS methods so far return a number. What if the question asks for the subsequence
itself? Pause on that for the table method before reading on: `dp[i]` is a length, and a
length does not say which elements produced it.

## One extra array

When the loop computes `dp[i]`, it tries every earlier `j` and keeps the best. At that
moment it knows *which* `j` was best — and then throws that away. Keep it instead:

> `prev[i]` = the index `j` that gave `dp[i]` its value. If no `j` was used,
> `prev[i] = −1`: `nums[i]` is the first number of its subsequence.

```
for i = 0 … n−1:
    dp[i] = 1; prev[i] = −1              ← new
    for j = 0 … i−1:
        if nums[j] < nums[i] and dp[j] + 1 > dp[i]:
            dp[i] = dp[j] + 1
            prev[i] = j                  ← new: write down the winner
```

Nothing about the `dp` values changes. The loops are the same, so the time is still `O(n²)`;
the extra array costs `O(n)` space.

## Walk back along it

| `i` | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 | 7 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `nums[i]` | 10 | 9 | 2 | 5 | 3 | 7 | 101 | 18 |
| `dp[i]` | 1 | 1 | 1 | 2 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 4 |
| `prev[i]` | −1 | −1 | −1 | 2 | 2 | 3 | 5 | 5 |

1. **Start where `dp` is largest**: `i = 6`, with `dp = 4`.
2. **Follow `prev`**: 6 → 5 → 3 → 2 → −1. The numbers met are 101, 7, 5, 2.
3. **Reverse**, because the walk went from the last number to the first: `[2, 5, 7, 101]`.

## More than one right answer

The previous table concept offered `[2, 3, 7, 101]` as the LIS; this walk gives
`[2, 5, 7, 101]`. Both have length 4 and both are correct. They differ because `dp[3]` and
`dp[4]` tie at 2 when the 7 is being computed, and the loop — which only updates on a strict
improvement — keeps the first one it meets, so `prev[5] = 3` rather than 4. Starting the
walk at `i = 7` instead gives `[2, 5, 7, 18]`, a third correct answer.

The problem asks for *a* longest increasing subsequence. Which one you get is decided by
tie-breaking details of the loop, not by anything in the mathematics.

> A table of costs forgets the decisions that produced it. To recover the solution, record
> each winning decision as it is made, then follow the records backwards from the answer.
> The same idea returns for edit distance.
