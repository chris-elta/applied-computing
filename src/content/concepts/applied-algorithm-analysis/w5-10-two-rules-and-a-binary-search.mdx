The previous concept said what `sub` should contain: for each length, the smallest tail seen
so far. This one says how to keep it that way as each new number `x` is read. There are two
cases.

## Rule 1 — `x` is larger than everything in `sub`

**Append `x`.**

The last entry of `sub` is the tail of the longest increasing subsequence found so far. `x`
is bigger than it, so `x` can follow that subsequence, and the longest length grows by 1.

## Rule 2 — otherwise

**Find the first entry ≥ `x` and replace it with `x`.**

Say that entry is at position `k`. The entry before it, `sub[k−1]`, is smaller than `x` —
otherwise the search would have stopped there. So `x` can follow the subsequence whose tail
is `sub[k−1]`, giving a subsequence of length `k + 1` with tail `x`. And `x` is no bigger
than the old `sub[k]`, so it is a tail at least as good. (If `k = 0`, `x` on its own has
length 1.)

**Why ≥ and not >.** The subsequence is strictly increasing, so a number equal to a tail
cannot follow it. If `sub = [3, 8]` and `x = 8`, searching with ≥ lands on the 8 and
replaces it with itself: nothing changes, length 2. Searching with > would find nothing and
append, claiming a length-3 subsequence ending `8, 8`.

## Step by step

`nums = [10, 5, 8, 3, 9]`

| Read | Rule | `sub` after | What it means |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 10 | append | `[10]` | length 1: smallest tail is 10 |
| 5 | replace 10 | `[5]` | length 1: `[5]` is better than `[10]` |
| 8 | append | `[5, 8]` | length 2 is now possible: `[5, 8]` |
| 3 | replace 5 | `[3, 8]` | length 1: `[3]` is better; length 2 is still `[5, 8]` |
| 9 | append | `[3, 8, 9]` | length 3 is now possible: `[5, 8, 9]` |

`sub` has three entries, so the LIS has length 3.

Look hard at the row for 3. After it, `sub[0] = 3` but the 8 in `sub[1]` is still the tail
of `[5, 8]` — not of `[3, 8]`, which is not even a subsequence. The entries of `sub` belong
to different subsequences. The next concept is about exactly that.

## Where the speed comes from

Both rules keep `sub` sorted from small to large: an append adds a new maximum, and a
replacement puts `x` between a smaller left neighbour and a right neighbour that was already
at least as big. In a sorted array, "the first entry ≥ `x`" can be found by binary search
in `O(log n)` steps. One search per number gives `O(n log n)` in total.

> Rule 1 makes the best subsequence longer. Rule 2 makes some shorter one easier to extend
> later. Neither ever makes `sub` shorter.
