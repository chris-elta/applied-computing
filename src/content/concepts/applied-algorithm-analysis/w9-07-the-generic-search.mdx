In an undirected graph in three pieces — `s, u, v, w` joined together, `x` and `y`
joined to each other, and `z` alone — which vertices can you get to from `s`? Every vertex
in its own piece, `s, u, v, w`, and none of the others.

Now put arrows on the same edges. If both of `w`'s edges point *away* from `w`, it can no
longer be reached: you can leave `w`, but never arrive. From `s`, only `s, u, v` remain.

A vertex is **reachable** from `s` if some sequence of edges leads there from `s`. The
question this section answers is: how do you find them all, mechanically?

## One loop

Divide the vertices into two sets — **explored** (found so far) and **unexplored** — and
keep moving one across the boundary.

```
mark s as explored, all other vertices as unexplored
while there is an edge from v to w with v explored and w unexplored do
    choose some such edge from v to w        // underspecified
    mark w as explored
```

The boundary between the two sets is the **frontier**, and an edge from an explored vertex
to an unexplored one is a **crossing edge**. Each pass of the loop picks one crossing edge
and pulls its far end across the frontier.

| edge | can it be chosen? |
| --- | --- |
| explored → unexplored | yes: it crosses the frontier |
| explored → explored | no: nothing new to mark |
| unexplored → anything | no: the search is not standing at its tail |

An undirected edge can be followed either way; a directed edge only from tail to head.

**Postcondition:** when the loop stops, a vertex is explored *if and only if* it is
reachable from `s`.

## What "explored" means here

Explored means **found and marked**. It does not mean finished. A vertex that has just been
marked may still have outgoing edges no one has looked at — and those are exactly the edges
that can cross next. Keeping "marked" and "finished with" apart becomes important in both
searches that follow.

## The deliberate gap

Look at the comment on the `choose` line: *underspecified*. The algorithm does not say
*which* crossing edge to take. That is not carelessness. The next page shows that the
answer does not affect which vertices are found — and the two famous searches, BFS and DFS,
are nothing more than two ways of filling in that one line.

> Graph search is one loop: while some edge crosses from explored to unexplored, take it
> and mark the far end. When no edge crosses, explored is exactly reachable.
