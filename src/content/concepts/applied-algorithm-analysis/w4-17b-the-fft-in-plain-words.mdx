**The job:** multiply two long polynomials without doing all `n²` little multiplications.

## 1 · Use points instead of coefficients

Two points fix a line. In the same way, `n` points fix a polynomial with `n` coefficients. And
points are easy to multiply: at each `x`, multiply the two heights. So:

1. plug the same `x`-values into both polynomials;
2. multiply the answers pair by pair;
3. turn the resulting points back into coefficients.

The catch is step 1. Plugging in `n` values at `n` steps each is `n²` again.

## 2 · Pick the points in ± pairs

`x` and `−x` have the same square. Sort the terms into even and odd powers:

```
A(x)  = E(x²) + x · O(x²)
A(−x) = E(x²) − x · O(x²)
```

Work out `E` and `O` once and you get **two** answers. So `n` points for one polynomial become
`n/2` points for two polynomials half as long.

## 3 · Keep halving

To do the trick again, the squares must also come in ± pairs. Ordinary numbers cannot manage
that, because squares are never negative. Points spaced evenly round a circle of complex
numbers can:

```
1, i, −1, −i   → square →   1, −1   → square →   1
```

Those points are the roots of unity, and this halving is the FFT.

## The price

The problem halves `log n` times and each level takes about `n` steps: `n log n` in total. For
a million coefficients that is about 20 million steps instead of a trillion.
