Make 6 out of coins worth 1, 3 and 4, using as few coins as possible. You can use each value
as often as you like.

The obvious method: take the biggest coin that fits, subtract it, and repeat.

| Step | Amount left | Biggest coin that fits | Take |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 6 | 4 | 4 |
| 2 | 2 | 1 | 1 |
| 3 | 1 | 1 | 1 |

Three coins: `4 + 1 + 1`. But `3 + 3` uses two, and no single coin is worth 6, so two is the
best possible. The method lost.

## What made it lose

The first step was the best-looking one: 4 gets closest to 6. What it left behind was a 2,
and the only way to make 2 from these coins is `1 + 1`. Starting with a 3 looks worse, since
it leaves 3 instead of 2, but that 3 can be paid with one coin.

The method judges each coin by how much it takes off *now*. It never asks what the remainder
will cost. This is the same mistake that value per pound made in the knapsack last week: a
score for one choice on its own cannot see how well the rest of the problem works out.

## Greedy algorithms

Biggest-coin-first is an example of a **greedy algorithm**. The lecture defines one by four
properties:

- it builds the solution **step by step**;
- at each step it takes the **best local option**, the one that looks best right now;
- it **never backtracks**: once a choice is made, it is final;
- it needs a **correctness proof** before you can say the result is globally optimal.

The fourth property follows from the first three. Committing for good to whatever looks best
right now is a gamble, and the coin example shows the gamble can lose. Greedy algorithms are
fast and easy to write. Whether one is *correct* depends on the problem, and you only know
that from a proof.

For coins, the fix is the table from last week. Let `M(a)` be the fewest coins that make
amount `a`, and try every coin as the last one. That compares whole answers instead of single
steps.

## This week

Most of this lecture is about a problem where greedy *does* work: connecting a network as
cheaply as possible. It has two greedy algorithms, Prim's and Kruskal's, and the cut property
explains why both are correct.

> A greedy rule is a claim: the best step now is part of a best answer overall. Sometimes the
> claim is true and sometimes it is false, so a greedy algorithm without a proof is only a
