Here is the trap, and it is worth sitting with before reading on. Halving the size of a
multiplication makes it **four times** cheaper — cost goes like the square, so half the size
is a quarter of the work.

So surely splitting into half-size products is a win?

## Count it directly

Take two 8-bit inputs and count single-bit products both ways:

| method | count |
| --- | --- |
| schoolbook — every bit of `x` meets every bit of `y` | `8 × 8 = 64` |
| four half-size products — each 4 bits by 4 bits | `4 × (4 × 4) = 64` |

Identical. And in general:

```
4 · (n/2)²  =  4 · n²/4  =  n²
```

The split bought **nothing**. `T(n) = 4T(n/2) + O(n)` solves to `O(n²)`, which is exactly
what schoolbook multiplication already cost in week 2.

## Why this is the important slide

Each subproblem really is a quarter of the cost. There really are four of them. A quarter,
four times, is one.

> Cutting the input in half is not by itself a saving. The shrinkage has to outrun the
> multiplication in the *number* of subproblems.

That reframes the goal completely. The aim is not to make the subproblems smaller — the
split already does that, for free. The aim is to end up with **fewer subproblems** than the
shrinkage pays for.

Four is one too many. Losing a single one of the four is what the next page does, and it is
why the section needed Gauss.
