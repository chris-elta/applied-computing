Divide and conquer is three steps:

1. **Divide** the input into smaller subproblems.
2. **Conquer** the subproblems recursively.
3. **Combine** the solutions into a solution for the original problem.

Step 3 is where the cost usually hides. The paradigm only wins when combining is cheap
relative to the work it saves — and most of this week is really about measuring that trade
precisely.

## The trade, in miniature

Multiplying two complex numbers looks like it needs four real multiplications:

```
(a + bi)(c + di) = ac − bd + (bc + ad)i
```

Count them: `ac`, `bd`, `bc`, `ad`. Four.

Gauss noticed that three are enough, because the awkward middle term can be recovered from
a single extra product:

```
bc + ad = (a + b)(c + d) − ac − bd
```

Look at what the right-hand side actually costs. `(a+b)(c+d)` is one multiplication. `ac` and
`bd` are two more — **but you needed both of those anyway** for the real part. So the whole
calculation takes three multiplications and two extra additions instead of four
multiplications.

## Why that matters

One multiplication traded for two additions. On its own that is a minor saving, and it is
worth being precise about why it is a saving at all: from week 2, addition on `n`-bit numbers
costs `O(n)` while multiplication costs `O(n²)`. The two sides of the trade are not the same
price.

> Additions are cheap. That is the trade, and the rest of the week is what happens when you
> make it **recursively**.

Doing it once saves a constant factor. Doing it at every level of a recursion changes the
exponent — and changing an exponent is the only kind of improvement that survives the input
getting large.
