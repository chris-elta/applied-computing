The definitions are behind you. This page is drill: the same three steps, applied seven
times, until the mechanics stop needing thought.

Keep the picture from *Big-O, Formally* in mind, because every proof below is the same move.
`T(n)` is just the wait. The claim is about its **shape**, and proving it means **building a
fence the growth never breaks through**:

- the **fence** is `c·f(n)` — you choose how steep,
- the **starting point** is `n₀` — you choose when to start caring,
- and then it must hold forever after.

## Which relation should you claim?

The symbol you pick encodes *which question you are answering*.

| you want to say | use | typically because |
| --- | --- | --- |
| never worse than this | `O` | you are giving a guarantee |
| at least this bad | `Ω` | you are showing it cannot be beaten |
| exactly this rate | `Θ` | you can prove both, so say the strongest true thing |
| negligible beside this | `o` | you are discarding a term, or proving strict separation |
| cannot be this fast | not `O` | you are proving two rates are genuinely different |

The rule of thumb: **claim `Θ` if you can prove it; `O` if a promise is all you need; `Ω`
when the whole point is that no cleverness will help.** `Θ` is strictly more informative than
either one-sided bound, which is why claiming only `O` when you know `Θ` throws information
away.

## The recipe

Having chosen the relation, every upper-bound proof is the same three steps. To prove
`T(n) = O(f(n))`:

1. **Pick the fence's shape** — the leading term, the fastest-growing one. That is `f(n)`.
2. **Pick how steep, and from when** — the witnesses `c` and `n₀`.
3. **Show the growth stays under it** — `T(n) ≤ c·f(n)` for every `n ≥ n₀`.

Step 1 is usually obvious by inspection. The next two sections are steps 2 and 3 in detail.

## Where do the witnesses come from?

Three facts make finding `c` and `n₀` mechanical rather than inspired.

**1 · They are not unique.** If `c = 10, n₀ = 1` works, so does `c = 10, n₀ = 500`, and so
does `c = 10000, n₀ = 1`. You need *one* working pair, never the best one. So aim for the
pair that is easiest to check, not the tightest.

**2 · Choose `n₀` first, and almost always choose 1.** You pick it in order to *license* the
next step: at `n ≥ 1` the powers line up, `1 ≤ n ≤ n² ≤ n³ ≤ …`. Take `n₀ > 1` only when a
term needs `n` to be large before it is dominated.

**3 · Then `c` falls out of the inflation.** Once every term has become a multiple of `f(n)`,
add the multipliers together. That sum is your `c`.

The direction of the move flips with the relation. For an **upper** bound you *inflate*
smaller terms up to the leading one; for a **lower** bound you *discard* them, because
dropping a positive term only makes the left side smaller.

### Why inflate at all?

Because the definition accepts only one shape of answer: `T(n) ≤ c·f(n)` — a **single**
multiple of `f(n)`. And `3n + 7` is two different kinds of thing added together. No fixed `c`
makes `3n + 7 = c·n`, because the `7` refuses to scale with `n`.

So replace each awkward term with something that is **(a)** at least as big and **(b)**
already the right shape. `7 ≤ 7n` is both. Now every term is a multiple of `n`, and multiples
of `n` collapse into one:

```
3n + 7  ≤  3n + 7n  =  10n
```

Over-estimating is safe because you are proving a **ceiling** — raising the right-hand side
can never turn a true ceiling into a false one. Lower bounds discard for the mirror-image
reason: lowering a floor cannot break it either.

And `7 ≤ 7n` is not free — it fails at `n = 0`. **The inflation is what fixes `n₀ = 1`.** The
threshold is not chosen arbitrarily; it is the price of the move you just made.

### 1 · `T(n) = 3n + 7` is `O(n)`

**Why `O`.** The question is "does this scale like a straight line?" — a promise about the
ceiling. (`Θ(n)` is also true here and is the stronger claim; `O` is what a guarantee needs.)

Inflate the constant into a multiple of `n`. For `n ≥ 1` we have `7 ≤ 7n`, so

```
T(n) = 3n + 7 ≤ 3n + 7n = 10n
```

Witnesses `c = 10`, `n₀ = 1`. ∎

**The witnesses.** `n₀ = 1` was chosen first, purely so that `7 ≤ 7n` holds; `c = 10` is then
just `3 + 7`. A completely different pair also works: `c = 4, n₀ = 7`, since `3n + 7 ≤ 4n`
exactly when `7 ≤ n`. Both are valid proofs of the same claim, and nothing in the definition
prefers one — but notice the trade. **Inflation buys a trivial threshold at the cost of a
loose constant**; the tighter `c = 4` costs you having to work out where two lines cross.
That is why inflating is the default: it always works, and it needs no thought.

### 2 · `T(n) = 5n³ + 4n + 3` is `O(n³)`

**Why `O`.** The everyday question about a polynomial: what shape is it? Again `Θ(n³)` holds,
but `O` is the convention when reporting a cost.

For `n ≥ 1`: `4n ≤ 4n³` and `3 ≤ 3n³`, so

```
T(n) ≤ 5n³ + 4n³ + 3n³ = 12n³
```

Witnesses `c = 12`, `n₀ = 1`. ∎

**The witnesses.** `c` is the sum of the coefficients, `5 + 4 + 3`, which is exactly rule 3.
Note it is *loose* — the real ratio `T(n)/n³` settles down to 5, not 12. That does not matter
in the slightest. Chasing `c = 6` would force a larger `n₀` and buy nothing, because the
definition asks only whether **some** constant exists.

> **The general recipe for polynomials:** take `n₀ = 1` and `c =` the sum of the *absolute
> values* of the coefficients. Absolute values, because a negative coefficient must not be
> allowed to shrink the bound.

### 3 · `T(n) = 2n + 100` is `Ω(n)`

**Why `Ω`.** Here the interesting claim is a **floor** — that no implementation trick makes
this sublinear. Upper bounds promise; lower bounds forbid.

Discard rather than inflate, since dropping a positive term only makes the left side smaller:

```
T(n) = 2n + 100 ≥ 2n ≥ 1·n     for n ≥ 1
```

Witnesses `c = 1`, `n₀ = 1`. ∎

**The witnesses.** The `100` was thrown away, so `c` comes from whatever survives — the `2n`.
`c = 2` would also work; `c = 1` is chosen only because it makes the final step trivial. And
the same inflation move as example 1 gives the matching upper bound — `100 ≤ 100n` for
`n ≥ 1`, so `T(n) ≤ 102n` — which means the honest claim here is `Θ(n)`.

### 4 · `T(n) = ½n² + 3n` is `Θ(n²)`

**Why `Θ`.** Both directions are provable, so the strongest true statement is available. `O(n²)`
alone would be true but weaker; `O(n³)` would be true and misleading.

```
lower:  T(n) ≥ ½n²                  → c₁ = ½
upper:  3n ≤ 3n²  (n ≥ 1), so
        T(n) ≤ ½n² + 3n² = 3.5n²    → c₂ = 3.5
```

Witnesses `c₁ = ½`, `c₂ = 3.5`, `n₀ = 1`. ∎

**The witnesses.** The two constants come from **opposite moves** — `c₁` from discarding `3n`,
`c₂` from inflating it — which is exactly why `Θ` needs two. `n₀ = 1` happens to serve both;
where the two arguments need different thresholds, take the larger.

### 5 · `T(n) = n²` is **not** `O(n)`

**Why "not `O`".** Without separation results, `O` would be near-vacuous. This is what makes
"quadratic is genuinely worse than linear" a theorem rather than an impression.

Suppose `c, n₀` existed with `n² ≤ c·n` for all `n ≥ n₀`. Divide by `n`:

```
n ≤ c        for all n ≥ n₀
```

But `c` is *fixed* and `n` runs off to infinity. Take `n = max(n₀, ⌈c⌉ + 1)` — it satisfies
`n ≥ n₀` yet exceeds `c`. Contradiction, so no such constants exist. ∎

**The witnesses — the roles swap.** You no longer supply `c` and `n₀`; your opponent does, and
you must defeat **every** pair they could choose. So your counterexample has to be written
*in terms of* their constants, and it must clear two bars at once:

- `max(n₀, …)` puts it inside the range they claimed, so they cannot dismiss it as too small;
- `⌈c⌉ + 1` puts it above `c`, so the inequality genuinely fails.

Drop either half and the proof collapses. This is the shape of every "not `O`" argument.

### 6 · `T(n) = 3n` is `o(n²)`

**Why `o`.** Not merely "no bigger than `n²`" — `3n` is *negligible* beside `n²`, and their
ratio vanishes. That is the claim you need when discarding a term entirely.

Given any `c > 0`, we need `3n ≤ c·n²`. Divide by `n`: `3 ≤ cn`, i.e. `n ≥ 3/c`. So take
`n₀ = ⌈3/c⌉`. ∎

**The witnesses — `c` is no longer yours.** Little-o must hold for *every* positive `c`, so
you cannot go shopping for a convenient one. Only `n₀` remains yours, and it therefore has to
be computed **as a function of `c`**. Sanity-check it with a hostile value: `c = 0.001` gives
`n₀ = 3000`. Finite, so the claim survives — and it survives for any `c`, however small.

Compare example 1, where a single `c` sufficed. That is the whole difference between `O` and
`o`.

### 7 · `T(n) = max{f(n), g(n)}` is `Θ(f(n) + g(n))`

**Why `Θ`.** The result is used as an *equivalence* — "you may replace the max by the sum" —
and a one-sided bound would not license that substitution.

For non-negative `f` and `g`:

```
max{f, g} ≤ f + g ≤ 2 · max{f, g}
```

Left: the sum is the larger plus something non-negative. Right: replace each of `f`, `g` by
the larger and you have added it to itself. Rearranged, `½(f+g) ≤ T(n) ≤ 1·(f+g)`.

Witnesses `c₁ = ½`, `c₂ = 1`, `n₀ = 1`. ∎

**The witnesses.** These fall out of a chain of inequalities rather than term-by-term
inflation — and notice they do not depend on what `f` and `g` actually *are*. That is what
makes the theorem reusable: the same two constants work for every pair of non-negative
functions.

**Why it matters:** two phases running in sequence cost `Θ` of the *slower* one. You may drop
the faster phase without apology — which is why nobody writes `O(n log n + n)`.

## Two traps

**True but useless.** `T(n) = ½n² + 3n` is honestly `O(n³)`: since `T(n) ≤ 3.5n² ≤ 3.5n³`,
witnesses `c = 3.5`, `n₀ = 1`. Valid, and it throws away everything you knew.

**Bounds cannot be proved by example.** Checking `n = 10` proves nothing — the claim is about
*every* `n` past `n₀`. Finding one spot where the plant is under the fence says nothing about
the rest of the garden. One example can only ever **disprove** (as in example 5).
