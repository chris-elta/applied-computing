Before any of the machinery: **`T(n)` is not something you prove.** It is a name for the
wait. `n` is how much you gave the program; `T(n)` is how long it took. `T(10)` is the wait
for ten things. It is a fact about your program, like "this kettle takes four minutes" —
there is nothing to prove about it.

What gets proved is the sentence you put it in:

```
T(n) = O(n)
```

**That `=` is a liar.** Nothing is being equated — nobody claims the wait *equals* `n`. Read
it as the word *is*: "the wait **is** straight-line-ish". The claim is about the **shape** of
the growth, not about any number.

## The fence

Picture the wait as a plant. Bigger inputs, taller plant. `T(n) = O(n)` says:

> I can build a straight sloping fence, and the plant will never grow through it.

Straight fence, straight-line growth. If the plant bursts through *every* straight fence
anyone builds, the claim is false and the growth is worse than linear.

Two cheats make the idea workable rather than uselessly strict:

- **You choose how steep the fence is.** That is `c`. A fence ten times steeper than `n` is
  still a *straight* fence — which is why `3n + 7` counts as straight-line growth even
  though it is not `n`.
- **You choose when to start caring.** That is `n₀`. Small inputs are allowed to be weird;
  say "from 5 onwards" and nobody checks 1 to 4.

Once you have picked both, the plant must stay under that fence **forever**. Not mostly. Not
for a while.

## What `n₀` is actually for

Small inputs are noisy. A program can be slow on tiny inputs for reasons that say nothing
about how it scales — a warm-up, a fixed setup cost — and that should not disqualify it from
being straight-line-ish. Growth claims are about the **trend**, and a trend only shows up
once things get big.

Watch it earn its keep. Try to fence `3n + 7` in with `4n`. At `n = 1` the wait is 10 against
a fence of 4 — the plant pokes straight through. At `n = 6`, 25 against 24, still through. At
`n = 7`, 28 against 28 — and from there on it never escapes again. That does not make the
claim false. This fence simply needs a starting line of `n₀ = 7`.

**The obvious objection.** Could you set `n₀` enormous and claim anything you like? No — and
this is what keeps the definition honest. After `n₀` the fence must hold for *infinitely
many* inputs. Postponing the start skips a **finite** number of awkward cases, so it can
never rescue a false claim: `n²` bursts through a straight fence eventually, whether you
begin looking at 10 or at 10 billion.

Bigger is always safe, though. If `n₀ = 7` works, so do 8, 100, a million. You never need the
smallest threshold that works.

Written out, that is the definition:

> `T(n) = O(f(n))` if and only if **there exist** positive constants `c` and `n₀` such that
> `T(n) ≤ c·f(n)` **for all** `n ≥ n₀`.

## The quantifier order is the whole thing

- **There exist** `c` and `n₀` — *you* pick the fence and the starting point, and one
  working pair is enough.
- **For all** `n ≥ n₀` — once picked, it must hold at **every** larger input. One place the
  plant pokes through and the claim is dead.

You choose first; then the universe checks every case. Swap those round and you get a
strictly stronger statement — which is exactly how little-o differs from big-O.

The slide's picture is this drawn: `T(n)` ducks under `3·f(n)` at `n₀` and never comes back
up. Note `c = 1` would not have worked — the fence had to be steepened, and that is allowed.

## The two proof shapes

To prove a bound, you hand over three things: **the fence, the starting point, and the
showing.**

> **Name `c` and `n₀`, then check the inequality.**

To disprove one, you must show *no* fence works — so let someone pick any `c` they like, then
point at an `n` where the plant breaks through.

> **Assume the constants exist, cancel, then exhibit one `n` that breaks it.**
