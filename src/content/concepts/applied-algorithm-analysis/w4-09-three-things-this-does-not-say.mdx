Three claims worth being precise about, now that the algorithm and its analysis are both on
the table.

**1 · It is not a worst-case bound.** An unlucky run of pivots still gives `Θ(n²)`. The
randomness is in the algorithm's own coin, not in the input, so no adversary can *force* the
bad case by choosing a clever list — but chance can still deal it, on any input, on any run.

**2 · ¾ is not special.** Any shrink factor strictly below 1 gives a linear algorithm, because
`∑ rᵏ = 1/(1−r)` is a constant whenever `∣r∣ < 1`. A pivot guaranteed to land in only the
25th–75th percentile happened to give `¾`; a weaker guarantee — say, landing in the
10th–90th percentile, shrinking to `9/10` of the list — would still be linear, just with a
much larger constant (`1/(1 − 9/10) = 10`, ten passes instead of four).

**3 · Selection is not sorting-minus-a-step.** Sorting answers the question for every `k` at
once, at a cost of `O(n log n)`. Selection answers it for one `k`, and the entire saving comes
from recursing into **one** sublist instead of two. That single structural change — one
recursive call, not two — is exactly where the `log n` factor goes missing from the running
time.

## Practice

**LeetCode 215 · Kth Largest Element in an Array.** Note the word *largest*: the `k`-th
largest of `n` elements is the `(n − k + 1)`-th smallest, so this algorithm solves it with one
change of index. Most accepted solutions reach for a heap, which runs in `O(n log k)` —
quickselect, the algorithm built across this module, is the `O(n)`-average alternative.
