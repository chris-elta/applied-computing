Modular arithmetic would be worthless if you had to compute the answer first and reduce it
afterwards.

`2³⁴⁵` has 104 decimal digits — large but writable. In cryptography the *exponent* has
hundreds of digits, so `xʸ` would have more digits than there are atoms in the observable
universe. Not slow. **Impossible.**

## The escape

Take `mod N` after **every step**, never at the end. The substitution rule guarantees this
changes nothing.

```
13 × 17 mod 10

long way:   13 × 17 = 221,  then 221 mod 10 = 1
short way:  (13 mod 10)(17 mod 10) = 3 × 7 = 21,  then 21 mod 10 = 1
```

The payoff compounds. Because every intermediate value is dragged back below `N`, **every
operation works on `n`-bit numbers**, however long the computation runs. The cost per step
stays flat; only the number of steps grows.

## But doesn't the reduction cost something?

No — a modular operation costs the same *order* as the ordinary one. The asymmetry between
the two cases is worth understanding rather than memorising.

**Addition.** Both inputs are below `N`, so `a + b < 2N`. It has overshot by at most **one**
copy of `N`. Reducing means: check whether it reached `N`, and if so subtract `N` once.

> add `O(n)` + one subtraction `O(n)` = **`O(n)`**

**Multiplication.** Both inputs are below `N`, so `a · b < N²` — up to `2n` bits. It may
have overshot by nearly a *factor* of `N`, so one subtraction is hopeless; you need a full
**long division** by `N`.

> multiply `O(n²)` + one division `O(n²)` = **`O(n²)`**

In one line: **a sum overshoots by at most one `N`; a product overshoots by up to a factor
of `N`.**

## Two numbers to carry forward

> - One modular **addition** is `O(n)`.
> - One modular **multiplication** is `O(n²)`.

These are the unit prices. The next two pages each take one and multiply by a round count —
and that is their entire analysis.
