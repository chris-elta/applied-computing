## Counting the calls exactly

Give the cost a name: let `C(n)` be the number of calls `fib1(n)` makes. From the code:

- `C(0) = C(1) = 1` — base cases do one call and stop.
- `C(n) = C(n−1) + C(n−2) + 1` — two recursive calls, plus the one you are in.

That middle line is the Fibonacci rule with `+1` bolted on, and it solves to
`C(n) = 2·F(n+1) − 1`.

> **The work `fib1` does *is* the Fibonacci sequence.** Its cost and its output grow at the
> same rate — what comes of taking a definition literally.

Since `F(n) ≈ φⁿ/√5` with `φ ≈ 1.618`, the calls multiply by ~1.618 per extra `n`.

## In seconds, at a billion calls per second

| `n` | `fib1` calls | `fib2` additions | `fib1` at 10⁹/sec |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 20 | 21,891 | 19 | instant |
| 40 | 331,160,281 | 39 | 0.3 seconds |
| 50 | 40,730,022,147 | 49 | 41 seconds |
| 100 | 1.15 × 10²¹ | 99 | 36,000 years |
| 200 | 9.08 × 10⁴¹ | 199 | 10¹⁵ × the age of the universe |

Read the middle columns together: at `n = 200`, one algorithm needs 199 additions and the
other needs more operations than there are atoms to perform them with.

## Why a faster computer does not save you

Buy a machine **twice as fast**. How much further does `fib1` reach? You can afford one
extra multiplication by `φ` for as long as `φ` fits into 2 — which is `log_φ 2 ≈ 1.44`
times. (A logarithm is just the answer to "how many times must I multiply by this to reach
that?")

**Doubling your hardware buys about 1.4 extra values of `n`.** Meanwhile `fib2` reaches
`n = 200` on a phone.

> **The algorithm, not the hardware, decides what is computable.** Every asymptotic argument
> in this course is a way of seeing that without building the table first.
