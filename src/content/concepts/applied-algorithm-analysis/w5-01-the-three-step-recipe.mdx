Suppose someone hands you a table of numbers and a loop that fills it, and the loop gives the
right answer on three test inputs. Is the program correct? You cannot tell — not until you
know what each cell of the table is *supposed* to hold. Dynamic programming is a way of
solving a problem by filling such a table, and the method is three steps, always in the same
order.

| Step | Name | The question it answers |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Define the subproblem | What exactly does `dp[i]` mean? |
| 2 | Establish the recurrence | How does one cell follow from smaller ones? |
| 3 | Initialize and compute | What are the base cases, in what order are cells filled, and where is the answer? |

Every problem this week is these three steps with different contents.

## Step 1 — define the subproblem

Find a smaller version of the problem that helps build the full one, and say **in words**
what a cell holds: "the minimum cost to reach step `i`", "the number of ways to make sum
`i`". The index is called the **state**. For Fibonacci:

> `dp[i]` = the `i`-th Fibonacci number.

If you cannot say the meaning in one English sentence, you do not have a subproblem yet. Most
wrong solutions start here, and they start by skipping the sentence.

## Step 2 — establish the recurrence

Write `dp[i]` in terms of smaller cells. The question that usually finds it is: **what was
the last decision?** There are only a few things it could have been, and each one leaves a
smaller problem of the same kind. Fibonacci has no decision to make, so its recurrence — its
**transition** — is the definition:

```
dp[i] = dp[i−1] + dp[i−2]
```

## Step 3 — initialize and compute

Three things, each small and each a place to slip:

- **Base cases** stop the recurrence: `dp[0] = 0`, `dp[1] = 1`.
- **An order** in which to fill the cells — here `i = 2` up to `n`. The next concept shows
  there are two directions to choose from.
- **Where the answer sits.** Often the last cell, but not always; the longest increasing
  subsequence later this week is a problem where it is not.

Before trusting the loop, compute one cell by hand and compare it with the sentence from
step 1: `dp[2] = dp[1] + dp[0] = 1`, and the second Fibonacci number is indeed 1. Base cases
are where off-by-one errors live, and this check costs ten seconds.

> When you are stuck on a dynamic programming problem, ask which of the three steps you are
> stuck on. It is nearly always the first.
