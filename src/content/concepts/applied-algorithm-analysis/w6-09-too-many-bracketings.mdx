If the order is the only choice, why not try every order and keep the cheapest? Count how
many there are.

## Counting by the last multiplication

Let `T(n)` be the number of ways to bracket a chain of `n` matrices. One matrix needs no
brackets, so `T(1) = 1`.

For a longer chain, look at the **last** multiplication performed. It joins two finished
pieces: the first `k` matrices multiplied together, and the remaining `n − k`. The split
point `k` can be anywhere from 1 to `n − 1`. For a given `k`, the left piece can be
bracketed in `T(k)` ways and the right in `T(n − k)` ways, and any left choice goes with any
right choice, so that split accounts for `T(k) × T(n − k)` bracketings. Add over all splits:

```
T(n) = ∑ T(k) · T(n − k)   for k = 1 … n−1,      T(1) = 1
```

Work the first few by hand:

| `n` | Calculation | `T(n)` |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | — | 1 |
| 2 | `1·1` | 1 |
| 3 | `1·1 + 1·1` | 2 |
| 4 | `1·2 + 1·1 + 2·1` | 5 |

Four matrices: 5 ways. These are the **Catalan numbers**.

## How fast it grows

Because every value is a sum of products of earlier values, the count climbs quickly. Each
extra matrix multiplies it by about 3 at first, and later by almost 4.

| Matrices | Bracketings |
| --- | --- |
| 4 | 5 |
| 20 | 1,767,263,190 |

Twenty matrices is a small chain, and it already has 1.7 billion bracketings. Trying them
all is exponential in `n`.

## The recurrence is a hint

The formula for `T(n)` only counts. But the way it was derived is the same reasoning the
algorithm will use: fix where the last multiplication splits the chain, and then deal with
each side separately. Replace "how many ways" with "what is the cheapest way" and the sum
of products becomes a minimum of sums.

There is also a reason to expect a saving. The 1.7 billion bracketings are built out of the
same pieces again and again. `(BC)` appears inside `A((BC)D)` and inside `(A(BC))D`, and its
cost is the same each time. An exhaustive search pays for it every time it shows up.

**The challenge.** Find the bracketing with the lowest total cost without trying them all.

> A huge number of solutions does not mean a huge number of subproblems. Exponentially many
> bracketings are assembled from a small stock of shared parts, and that sharing is the
> overlap dynamic programming needs.
