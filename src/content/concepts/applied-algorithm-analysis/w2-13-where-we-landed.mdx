One sentence, one table, one habit.

## The sentence

> **For algorithms on numbers the input size is the number of bits, `n ≈ log N` — never the
> value `N`.**

Everything else this week follows from it. An algorithm handed `N` is handed a row of about
`log₂ N` digits, which is why a loop counting up to `N` is exponential rather than linear,
and why arithmetic that looked free turns out to have a price.

## The table

| on `n`-bit numbers | cost |
| --- | --- |
| addition | `O(n)` |
| multiplication | `O(n²)` |
| division and mod | `O(n²)` |
| modular exponentiation | `O(n³)` |
| gcd, by Euclid | `O(n³)` |

Two lines are worth pausing on. **Addition is `Θ(n)` and provably optimal** — upper and lower
bounds meet, so no future cleverness will improve it. **Multiplication is the first operation
that is not linear in the digits**, and whether `O(n²)` is the best possible remains open at
this point in the course.

## The habit: rounds × cost per round

The last two rows were derived identically, and neither needed anything clever:

- **Fast exponentiation** — the exponent at least halves every two calls, so `O(n)` rounds;
  each is one modular multiplication at `O(n²)`.
- **Euclid** — the larger value halves every two rounds, so `2n` rounds; each is one division
  at `O(n²)`.

Find the round count, find the per-round cost, multiply. That accounting is the most reusable
thing in this week's material, and next week's recurrences are its general form.
