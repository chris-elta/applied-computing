notation:
  - symbol: "n = 4"
    reads: n equals four
    means: >-
      The number of points, and the length every coefficient list is padded to. It must be a
      power of two **at least as large as the number of coefficients in the product**, not
      in either input.
  - symbol: "ω = i"
    reads: omega equals i
    means: >-
      For `n = 4` the first root of unity is a quarter turn, which is `i`. Its powers are
      `ω⁰, ω¹, ω², ω³ = 1, i, −1, −i`, always in that order.
  - symbol: "ω⁻¹ = −i"
    reads: omega inverse equals minus i
    means: >-
      A quarter turn the other way. The inverse FFT uses its powers, `1, −i, −1, i`.
  - symbol: "E(y), O(y)"
    reads: E of y, O of y
    means: >-
      The even- and odd-indexed coefficients, each read as a polynomial of their own. For
      `a₀ + a₁x + a₂x² + a₃x³`: `E(y) = a₀ + a₂y` and `O(y) = a₁ + a₃y`.
  - symbol: "(a + bi)(c + di)"
    reads: a plus b i, times c plus d i
    means: >-
      Multiply out as usual, then replace `i²` with `−1`: the result is
      `(ac − bd) + (ad + bc)i`.

      the smallest `n` that works?
    choices:

        correct: true
        explains: >-
          The product has degree `3 + 2 = 5`, so it has 6 coefficients, and 6 values are
          needed to pin it down. The next power of two at or above 6 is 8.
      - text: "4"
        explains: >-
          4 is enough for each input, which has at most 4 coefficients. But the points have
          to recover the **product**, which has 6. With 4 points its high coefficients wrap
          around onto the low ones.
      - text: "6"
        explains: >-
          6 points are enough to determine the product, but the FFT halves its point set at
          every level, so `n` must be a power of two. Pad up to 8.
      - text: "5"
        explains: >-
          5 is the product's degree. A degree-5 polynomial has 6 coefficients, `x⁰` through
          `x⁵`, so it needs 6 points, and the FFT needs a power of two.
    work: |-
      Degrees add: `3 + 2 = 5`.
      Degree 5 means 6 coefficients, so at least 6 points.
      The FFT needs a power of two: 6 rounds up to 8.
      Pad both coefficient lists with zeros to length 8.
  - ask: >-

    choices:
      - text: "Dividing every result by `n = 4`"
        correct: true
        explains: >-
          Evaluating at the powers of `ω⁻¹` gives `n` times each coefficient. Dividing by 4

      - text: "Using `ω⁻¹` instead of `ω` for the inverse"
        explains: >-
          Using `ω` instead would scramble the order, not scale the values: the coefficients

          one, which points to the missing division.
      - text: "Padding the inputs with zeros"
        explains: >-
          Padding happens before the forward FFT, and it was done: both inputs were
          extended to 4 coefficients. A padding error would not multiply every answer by

      - text: "Nothing; the FFT returns the product's values, not its coefficients"

          The forward FFT returns values. The inverse step's job is to turn them back into

The FFT pages so far argue *why* it is fast. This one runs it, start to finish, on a case
small enough to do on paper:

```
A(x) = 1 + 2x        B(x) = 3 + x
```

You can multiply these in your head: `3 + 7x + 2x²`. That is the point. You know the answer,
so you can check each step against it.

## 1 · Choose n

The product has degree `1 + 1 = 2`, so it has **3** coefficients and needs at least 3 points.
Round up to a power of two: `n = 4`. Pad both inputs with zeros to 4 coefficients:

```
A:  1, 2, 0, 0        B:  3, 1, 0, 0
```

The points are the 4th roots of unity, in order: `1, i, −1, −i`.

## 2 · Evaluate, using the even/odd split

For any 4 coefficients, `E(y) = a₀ + a₂y` and `O(y) = a₁ + a₃y`. The points pair up as
`±1`, whose squares are `1`, and `±i`, whose squares are `−1`. So only `E` and `O` at `1` and
`−1` are needed:

```
A(1)  = E(1)  + O(1)          A(−1) = E(1)  − O(1)
A(i)  = E(−1) + i·O(−1)       A(−i) = E(−1) − i·O(−1)
```

For `A`, `E(y) = 1` and `O(y) = 2`. For `B`, `E(y) = 3` and `O(y) = 1`:

| point | `A` | `B` | `A · B` |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `1` | `3` | `4` | `12` |
| `i` | `1 + 2i` | `3 + i` | `1 + 7i` |
| `−1` | `−1` | `2` | `−2` |
| `−i` | `1 − 2i` | `3 − i` | `1 − 7i` |

The middle products need the complex rule: `(1 + 2i)(3 + i) = 3 + i + 6i + 2i² = 1 + 7i`.

## 3 · Multiply pointwise

That is the last column. Four multiplications, and the product's values are done.

## 4 · Interpolate: run it backwards

Read the value list `12, 1+7i, −2, 1−7i` as coefficients and evaluate at the powers of
**`ω⁻¹ = −i`**, which are `1, −i, −1, i`. Then **divide by `n`**:

| point | raw value | `÷ 4` |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `1` | `12` | `3` |
| `−i` | `28` | `7` |
| `−1` | `8` | `2` |
| `i` | `0` | `0` |

The coefficients are `3, 7, 2, 0`: the product is `3 + 7x + 2x²`, as expected. The zero at the
end is the padding coming back out.

## Three slips worth checking for

- **`n` from the inputs, not the product.** Size it from the product's coefficient count.
- **Points out of order.** `1, i, −1, −i` is `ω⁰` to `ω³`. Values in a different order give
  coefficients in a different order.
- **No division at the end.** Every coefficient comes out exactly `n` times too large.

> **The inverse FFT is the forward FFT with `ω⁻¹` and a division by `n`.** At `n = 4`
> that is a page of arithmetic; at a million points it is the same few lines of code.
