Here is an array of eight numbers:

```
10   9   2   5   3   7   101   18
```

Cross out as few as you can so that what remains goes strictly upward, left to right. Cross
out 10, 9, 5 and 18 and you are left with `2, 3, 7, 101` — four numbers. No choice leaves
five. That 4 is the answer to this problem:

> Given an array of numbers, find the length of the longest strictly increasing subsequence.

## The word that causes the wrong answers

The numbers you keep do **not** have to be neighbours. They only have to stay in their
original order. Two words describe a selection taken in order, and only one of them allows
gaps:

| | Positions must be | Example from the array |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Substring** | adjacent, nothing skipped | `2, 5, 3` |
| **Subsequence** | in order, gaps allowed | `2, 5, 7` — skips the 3 |

`2, 5, 7` is a subsequence and not a substring. This problem is about subsequences. Almost
every wrong answer to it starts by solving the substring version by accident, which is
easier and gives a smaller number.

## Seeing it as a dag

Take a different, shorter input: `5 2 8 6 3 6 9 7`. Make each position a node, and draw an
edge from position `i` to a later position `j` whenever `nums[i] < nums[j]` — that is,
whenever the second number could come directly after the first in an increasing
subsequence.

All the edges point rightward, so this is a dag. The two 6s are **not** joined, because the
subsequence must be *strictly* increasing.

Now follow any path through the dag and write down the numbers you pass. They go up at every
step and appear in array order: an increasing subsequence. And every increasing subsequence
is a path. So:

- increasing subsequences are exactly the paths;
- the LIS is the **longest path** in this dag.

The shortest-paths concept already swept a dag left to right taking a `min` at each node.
The same sweep with `max` in place of `min` finds the longest path. Nothing new has to be
invented — what remains is to write it as a recipe, which is the next concept.

> Order must be kept; positions need not be adjacent. Read the problem for that word before
> writing anything.
