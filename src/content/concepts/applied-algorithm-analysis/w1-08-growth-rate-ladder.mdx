Almost every algorithm lands on one of a handful of growth rates. Learning them as a ladder
— and knowing what each rung costs — is most of what asymptotic analysis is *for*.

| order | name | comes from |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `O(1)` | constant | you go straight to the answer — hash lookup |
| `O(log n)` | logarithmic | you halve the problem each step — binary search |
| `O(n)` | linear | you look at everything once — `fib2` |
| `O(n log n)` | linearithmic | everything, `log n` times over — mergesort |
| `O(n²)` | quadratic | every **pair** — two nested loops |
| `O(n³)` | cubic | every **triple** — matrix multiply |
| `O(2ⁿ)` | exponential | every **subset** — `fib1` |
| `O(n!)` | factorial | every **ordering** |

The right-hand column is the useful one: growth rates come from the *shape* of the work.
There are `2ⁿ` subsets because each item is independently in or out, and `n!` orderings
because there are `n` choices for first place, `n−1` for second, and so on. That is why
brute-force search lands on those two rows — they count *combinations*, not items.

## Why the slide switches axes

On a linear axis `2ⁿ` leaves the frame immediately and everything else is squashed flat.
On a **logarithmic** axis, where each gridline is ten times the last, every polynomial
becomes a straight line ranked by degree — and the exponential is still visibly different
in kind.

## What the ladder costs

At roughly one operation per nanosecond:

| `T(n)` | `n = 10` | `n = 1,000` | `n = 1,000,000` |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `log₂ n` | 3 ns | 10 ns | 20 ns |
| `n` | 10 ns | 1 µs | 1 ms |
| `n log₂ n` | 33 ns | 10 µs | 20 ms |
| `n²` | 100 ns | 1 ms | 17 minutes |
| `n³` | 1 µs | 1 second | 32 years |
| `2ⁿ` | 1 µs | 10²⁸⁴ years | never |
| `n!` | 3.6 ms | never | never |

## The line that matters

Scan the last column for where the *character* of the answer changes.

`n³` at a million is 32 years — probably unacceptable, but it is a **number**, and a smaller
input brings it back. Those rows degrade. `2ⁿ` at a thousand is 10²⁸⁴ years: not slow,
**impossible**, and it stays impossible on any hardware that will ever exist.

> The boundary runs between `n³` and `2ⁿ`. Polynomial rows survive a bigger input; the rows
> below never will.
