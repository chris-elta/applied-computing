Two numbers are **congruent** mod `N` when the modulus cannot tell them apart:

```
x ≡ y (mod N)   ⟺   N divides (x − y)
```

The three-bar `≡` deliberately is not `=`. 17 and 5 are not equal, but mod 12 they land in
the same place on the clock.

There are two equivalent readings, and you should be able to move between them freely:

- they leave the **same remainder** when divided by `N`; or
- their **difference is a multiple** of `N`.

| claim | difference a multiple of 12? | same remainder? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `17 ≡ 5 (mod 12)` | `17 − 5 = 12` ✓ | both 5 ✓ |
| `−3 ≡ 9 (mod 12)` | `−3 − 9 = −12` ✓ | both 9 ✓ |
| `14 ≡ 5 (mod 12)` | `14 − 5 = 9` ✗ | 2 vs 5 ✗ |

The middle row is where people trip, for the reason from the last page: `−3 mod 12` is 9.

## One word, two jobs

Worth separating carefully, because the notation reuses "mod" for two different things:

- **`a mod N` is an operation.** It evaluates to a number in `0…N−1`. `13 mod 5` is 3.
- **`x ≡ y (mod N)` is a statement.** It is true or false, and the `(mod N)` computes
  nothing — it only names which modulus `≡` is working with, and it governs the whole line.

## The substitution rule

> If `x ≡ x′ (mod N)` and `y ≡ y′ (mod N)`, then `x + y ≡ x′ + y′` and `xy ≡ x′y′`.

In plain terms: **you may swap any number for any other number in its pile, at any point in
the calculation, without changing the answer.** Addition and multiplication do not care which
representative you use, and associativity, commutativity and distributivity all survive
intact — so ordinary algebra still works.

Watch what that licence buys:

```
2³⁴⁵ ≡ (2⁵)⁶⁹ ≡ 32⁶⁹ ≡ 1⁶⁹ ≡ 1 (mod 31)
```

`2³⁴⁵` is a 104-digit number that we never wrote down. We noticed `32 ≡ 1 (mod 31)`, swapped
it for 1, and the problem collapsed to nothing.

That is not a party trick. It is the reason modular arithmetic is computable at all, and the
next page turns it into a general technique.
