Picture a committee to be chosen from a group of people, where some pairs refuse to sit
together. Draw each person as a vertex and each refusal as an edge. A committee with no
conflicts is a set of vertices with no edge between any two of them.

That is an **independent set**: a set of vertices such that no two of them are joined by an
edge.

In the lecture's six-vertex graph, `{1, 4, 6}` is independent and `{1, 2}` is not, because
1 and 2 are joined. The largest independent set in that graph has three vertices.

## What the definition does and does not forbid

It forbids a *direct* edge between two chosen vertices. It says nothing about vertices that
are connected by a longer route. Two people who each refuse to sit with a third person can
perfectly well sit with each other.

So "independent" does not mean "disconnected from each other" or "far apart". It means: no
edge inside the set.

## The trade-off

The goal is the **largest** independent set. Think about adding one person to the
committee:

- you gain 1 seat;
- you lose everyone who refuses to sit with them.

Sometimes that is a good trade and sometimes not. Someone with many refusals costs a lot of
future seats for one seat now. The recurrence in the next concept is built directly on this
trade: for each vertex, compare the best you can do with it against the best you can do
without it.

## Hard in general, easy on trees

On a general graph, finding the largest independent set is NP-hard. Nobody knows a method
that is fast on every graph.

On a **tree** it takes linear time.

The question is identical. The definition is identical. What changes is the shape of the
input. A tree has no cycles, so removing a vertex breaks it into pieces that have nothing
more to do with each other, and each piece is a smaller tree. Those pieces are the
subproblems. A general graph offers no such clean pieces: remove a vertex and what is left
can still be tangled together through other routes.

This is the third kind of index this week. The knapsack's subproblems were indexed by a
capacity, the matrix chain's by a stretch, and here they will be indexed by a subtree.

> Dynamic programming needs structure to hold on to. A problem that is intractable in
> general can become easy on inputs that break apart into independent smaller copies of
> themselves.
