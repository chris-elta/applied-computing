The algorithm is two journeys in one: the recursion goes **down**, shrinking the exponent
until it hits zero, then comes back **up**, doing the arithmetic.

The animated figure on slide 30 separates those two halves, and it is worth running once
before reading the table.

## Down: shrink the exponent

```
y = 13  odd  → y − 1
y = 12  even → halve
y = 6   even → halve
y = 3   odd  → y − 1
y = 2   even → halve
y = 1   odd  → y − 1
y = 0   base case, turn around
```

Seven calls to get from 13 to 0. Nothing has been computed yet — the descent only works out
*which* powers will be needed.

## Up: compute, reducing every time

| value | how | mod 7 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `3¹` | `3 · 3⁰ = 3` | 3 |
| `3²` | `(3¹)² = 9` | 2 |
| `3³` | `3 · 3² = 6` | 6 |
| `3⁶` | `(3³)² = 36` | 1 |
| `3¹²` | `(3⁶)² = 1` | 1 |
| `3¹³` | `3 · 3¹² = 3` | **3** |

Six rows, and every one is either a squaring or a multiplication by `x`, followed immediately
by a reduction.

## The thing to notice

**The largest number that appears anywhere is 36.** Computed directly, `3¹³ = 1,594,323`.

That gap is the whole argument of this section made concrete. The algorithm never holds the
answer to the original problem — it holds only remainders below 7 and their pairwise
products, which cannot exceed `6² = 36`. Scale the modulus up to a 500-bit `N` and the same
bound applies: nothing the algorithm stores is longer than `2n` bits, ever.

The descent controls **how many** operations you do. Reducing as you go controls **how big**
the numbers are. Both are needed, and they are independent.
