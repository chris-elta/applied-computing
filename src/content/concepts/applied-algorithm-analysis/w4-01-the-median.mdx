**Purpose.** A median summarises a dataset with one typical value, the same job a mean does —
but by a different rule.

**Definition.** The median is the 50th percentile: sort the list, and it is the value with as
many numbers above it as below. For an even-length list there are two middle values; this
course's convention is to take the **smaller** one, so the median is always
`⌊∣S∣/2⌋`-th smallest — one well-defined element, not an average of two.

## Median vs. mean

The mean is used more often, but the median is frequently the more representative number,
for a structural reason: the median is (almost) always one of the data values, and it barely
notices an outlier.

Take a list of 100 ones. Median 1, mean 1 — they agree. Now change a single value to 10,000.
The median doesn't move: sorting still puts that value at one end of the list, so the middle
position holds a 1 exactly as before. The mean moves to `100.99` — a hundredfold jump from
changing one one-hundredth of the data.

The reason is about how each statistic *counts*. The mean gives every element a vote weighted
by its size, so one huge value can drag it anywhere. The median gives every element one vote
about which side of the middle it falls on — and a value's vote doesn't get louder just
because the value itself is large.

## Why this matters for what's next

Nothing in this observation is specific to the median: it generalises immediately to *any*
question of the form "what is the `k`-th smallest element of a list?" The median is the case
`k = ⌊∣S∣/2⌋`, but the same question with `k = 1` asks for the minimum, and with `k = ∣S∣` asks
for the maximum. That generalisation — the **selection problem** — is where the rest of this
week goes.
