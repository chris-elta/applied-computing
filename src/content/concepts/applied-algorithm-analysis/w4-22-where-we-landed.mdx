      - text: >-

        explains: >-
          The master theorem doesn't distinguish randomised from deterministic recurrences —

      Master theorem form: T(n) = aT(n/b) + O(n^d), requiring subproblem size n/b for a

      - text: >-

        explains: >-

One shape, one comparison, one picture — read three different ways this week.

## The shape

Every running time this week had the form `T(n) = aT(n/b) + O(nᵈ)`, and the master theorem
does exactly one thing: **compare `d` with `log_b a`.**

| Algorithm | Recurrence | `a, b, d` | Case | Running time |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Selection, random pivot | `T(n) ≤ T(3n/4)+O(n)` | 1, 4/3, 1 | root pays | `O(n)` |
| Fast Fourier transform | `2T(n/2)+O(n)` | 2, 2, 1 | every level | `O(n log n)` |
| Block matrix product | `8T(n/2)+O(n²)` | 8, 2, 2 | leaves pay | `O(n³)` |
| Strassen | `7T(n/2)+O(n²)` | 7, 2, 2 | leaves pay | `O(n^2.81)` |

All three cases of the master theorem, in one lecture, read off the same two numbers each
time.

## The picture underneath

Selection's saving came from recursing into **one** side of a split instead of two — the
opposite of mergesort's shape, which pulled the recurrence's root above its leaves. The FFT's
saving came from choosing evaluation points that are **closed under squaring**, so the two
half-size recursive calls could share their evaluation points instead of needing fresh ones —
the same halving trick, applied to a totally different kind of problem. Strassen's saving was
the smallest and the most surgical: one fewer product, moving the exponent along the same
leaves-pay case rather than into a different one.
