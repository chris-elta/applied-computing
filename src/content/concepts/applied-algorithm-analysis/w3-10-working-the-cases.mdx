Four recurrences. Which solve to `O(n log n)`?

```
a)  T(n) = 2T(n/2) + O(n)
b)  T(n) = 4T(n/2) + O(n²)
c)  T(n) = 3T(n/3) + O(n)
d)  T(n) = 2T(n/2) + O(n²)
```

Work them before reading on. The method never varies: read off `a`, `b`, `d`, compute
`log_b a`, and see which side `d` lands on.

## The answers

| recurrence | `a, b, d` | `log_b a` | case | `T(n)` | |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| a) `2T(n/2) + O(n)` | 2, 2, 1 | 1 | `d = log_b a` | `O(n log n)` | ✓ |
| b) `4T(n/2) + O(n²)` | 4, 2, 2 | 2 | `d = log_b a` | `O(n² log n)` | |
| c) `3T(n/3) + O(n)` | 3, 3, 1 | 1 | `d = log_b a` | `O(n log n)` | ✓ |
| d) `2T(n/2) + O(n²)` | 2, 2, 2 | 1 | `d > log_b a` | `O(n²)` | |

**(a) and (c).**

## The two traps

**Row (b) is the subtle one.** It *is* balanced, and it *does* pick up a log factor — just
not on `n`. With `d = 2` the balanced answer is `O(nᵈ log n) = O(n² log n)`. Being balanced
tells you the shape of the answer; `d` tells you the exponent. Reading "balanced" as
"`O(n log n)`" is the single most common error with this theorem.

**Row (c) is the other one.** Three subproblems instead of two *sounds* worse than (a). It is
not: they are a third the size rather than a half, and `log₃3 = log₂2 = 1`. The balance is
identical.

> Three of the four rows are balanced. What separates them is not how many subproblems there
> are, but whether `d` matches `log_b a` — and, when it does, what `d` actually is.

Row (d) is the one genuinely different case: the combine step at `O(n²)` swamps the recursion
entirely, the root dominates, and the answer carries no log at all.
