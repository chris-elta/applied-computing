title: The Harmonic Sum Grows Like log n
notation:
  - symbol: "Hₙ"
    reads: H n, or the n-th harmonic number
    means: >-
      The sum `1 + 1/2 + 1/3 + … + 1/n`. The subscript says how many terms, it does not
      multiply: `H₄ = 1 + 1/2 + 1/3 + 1/4 ≈ 2.08`.
  - symbol: "Σ (i = 1 to n) 1/i"
    reads: the sum, for i from 1 to n, of one over i
    means: >-
      The same thing as `Hₙ`, written with a summation sign. `Σ` is capital sigma, Greek
      for S, for "sum".
  - symbol: "2ᵏ"
    reads: two to the k
    means: >-
      The powers of two, 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, …. Here they mark where each block of terms
      starts or ends.
  - symbol: "m = ⌊log₂ n⌋"
    reads: m equals the floor of log base two of n
    means: >-
      The largest `m` with `2ᵐ ≤ n`: how many times you can double 1 and stay at or below
      `n`. For `n = 8`, `m = 3`; for `n = 1000`, `m = 9`.
  - symbol: "ln n"
    reads: L N of n, or natural log of n
    means: >-
      The logarithm in base `e ≈ 2.718`. It differs from `log₂ n` only by a constant
      factor, so `Θ(ln n)` and `Θ(log n)` say the same thing.
questions:
  - ask: >-
      In the upper bound, the terms from `1/4` to `1/7` form one block. Why can that block
      add up to no more than 1?
    choices:
      - text: "It has 4 terms, and each is at most `1/4`"
        correct: true
        explains: >-
          Rounding each denominator down to 4 makes every term bigger, so
          `1/4 + 1/5 + 1/6 + 1/7 ≤ 4 · 1/4 = 1`. The same count-times-size argument works
          for every block: `2ᵏ` terms, each at most `1/2ᵏ`.
      - text: "The terms keep shrinking, so each block is smaller than the one before"
        explains: >-
          The terms shrink, but the blocks get longer at the same rate. The point of the
          grouping is that the two effects cancel, so every block is worth about the same.
      - text: "It has 4 terms, and each is at least `1/8`"
        explains: >-
          That is true, and it is the **lower**-bound argument: it shows the block is at
          least `4 · 1/8 = 1/2`. An upper bound needs each term made bigger, not smaller.
      - text: "`1/4 + 1/5 + 1/6 + 1/7` is exactly 1"
        explains: >-
          It is about 0.76. The bound is not an equality. It only needs each block to stay
          under 1.
    work: |-
      Upper bound: round each denominator down to the power of two at the start of its block.
      Block `1/4 … 1/7`: 4 terms, each ≤ `1/4`, so the block is ≤ `4 · 1/4 = 1`.
      Actual value: `0.25 + 0.2 + 0.167 + 0.143 ≈ 0.76`.
      "Shrinking terms" ignores that the blocks double in length; the `1/8` version is the lower bound.
  - ask: >-
      `1 + 1/2 + 1/4 + 1/8 + …` never passes 2, yet `1 + 1/2 + 1/3 + 1/4 + …` grows without
      limit. What is the difference that matters?
    choices:
      - text: "In the harmonic sum, each power-of-two block is worth at least `1/2`, and there is no end to the blocks"
        correct: true
        explains: >-
          The geometric series halves **every term**, so its total is dominated by the
          first one. The harmonic series halves its terms only once per block, while each
          block doubles in length. A fixed amount per block, with unboundedly many blocks,
          adds up to infinity.
      - text: "The harmonic terms never get close to 0"
        explains: >-
          They do: `1/1000` is small and `1/n` goes to 0. Terms going to 0 is not enough to
          keep a sum finite. That is what makes the harmonic sum surprising.
      - text: "The harmonic sum has more terms"
        explains: >-
          Both run on forever. The difference is how fast the terms shrink, not how many
          there are.
      - text: "Every harmonic term is bigger by a constant factor"
        explains: >-
          A constant factor times a series that stays below 2 would still stay below a
          constant. And the terms are not a constant factor apart: `1/8` against
          `1/2⁷ = 1/128` is already a factor of 16, and the gap keeps widening.
    work: |-
      Geometric: each term is half the last, so the sum is a constant times its first term, and stays below 2.
      Harmonic, in blocks: `1/2`; `1/3 + 1/4 ≥ 1/2`; `1/5 + … + 1/8 ≥ 1/2`; and so on.
      There are about `log₂ n` blocks below `n`, each worth at least `1/2`.
      So the sum grows like `log n`. It never stops growing, but it grows slowly.

---

Add up `1 + 1/2 + 1/3 + 1/4 + …`. The terms keep shrinking, so you might expect the total
to level off, the way `1 + 1/2 + 1/4 + 1/8 + …` never passes 2. It does not level off. It
grows forever, very slowly:

| `n` | 1 | 2 | 4 | 8 | 100 | 1,000,000 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `Hₙ` | 1 | 1.5 | 2.08 | 2.72 | 5.19 | 14.39 |

That sum is the **harmonic sum**, written `Hₙ`. It is close to `ln n + 0.577`, but that
formula is harder to prove than what an algorithm analysis needs, which is `Hₙ = Θ(log n)`.
As with `log(n!)`, you get there without a formula: you **trap** it.

## Group the terms in blocks of doubling length

Cut the terms at the powers of two. Each block has twice as many terms as the one before,
and each term in it is about half the size. Those two effects cancel, so **every block is
worth about the same.** Rounding the denominators makes that exact, once in each direction.

## Above: round the denominators down

Start each block at a power of two, and round every denominator in it **down** to that
power. A smaller denominator makes a bigger fraction, so the sum can only grow:

```
1/2 + 1/3                 ≤ 2 · 1/2 = 1
1/4 + 1/5 + 1/6 + 1/7     ≤ 4 · 1/4 = 1
1/8 + … + 1/15            ≤ 8 · 1/8 = 1
```

The block starting at `1/2ᵏ` has `2ᵏ` terms, each at most `1/2ᵏ`, so it is at most 1. With
`m = ⌊log₂ n⌋`, the blocks start at `1, 2, 4, …, 2ᵐ`. That is `m + 1` blocks, the last one
possibly cut short at `n`:

```
Hₙ ≤ m + 1 ≤ log₂ n + 1
```

## Below: round the denominators up

Now end each block at a power of two, and round every denominator **up** to it. A bigger
denominator makes a smaller fraction, so the sum can only shrink:

```
1/2                       = 1/2
1/3 + 1/4                 ≥ 2 · 1/4 = 1/2
1/5 + 1/6 + 1/7 + 1/8     ≥ 4 · 1/8 = 1/2
```

Every complete block is at least `1/2`. Since `2ᵐ ≤ n`, the blocks ending at
`2, 4, …, 2ᵐ` are all complete, and the leading 1 comes on top:

```
Hₙ ≥ 1 + m/2
```

Rounding `log₂ n` down to `m` loses less than 1, so this is at least `½ log₂ n + ½`.

## The trap, closed

| `n` | lower, `1 + m/2` | actual `Hₙ` | upper, `m + 1` |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 8 | 2.5 | 2.72 | 4 |
| 1,000 | 5.5 | 7.49 | 10 |

Both walls are constant multiples of `log n`, so `Hₙ = Θ(log n)`. The sum never stops
growing, and it never grows fast: it gains about the same fixed amount each time `n`
doubles, and counting doublings is what a logarithm does.

> **Group the terms so each group is easy to bound.** Rounding every term up makes the
> groups simple, and so does rounding every term down. If both simple sums grow at the same
> rate, the real one does too.
