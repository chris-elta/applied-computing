Before building a better sort, it is worth seeing precisely where the bad ones lose.

The figure on slide 31 races **selection**, **insertion** and **bubble** sort on the same
eight numbers. Press play and watch the comparison counters climb.

## What all three have in common

A loop inside a loop. Every pass sweeps whatever is not yet finished, so the comparisons pile
up as `n + (n−1) + (n−2) + …`, which is `O(n²)`.

That shape — *walk the remaining part, once per element* — is where the squared exponent comes
from, and it is the same in all three despite their different characters.

## A detail worth pausing on

Watch selection sort. It barely moves anything: it swaps at most once per pass, **four times
in total** on this input, against fourteen moves each for the other two.

And it still performs **28 comparisons**, the most of the three.

> Comparisons are what the quadratic exponent counts, not moves.

That is a useful separation to carry. Selection sort's low move count is a genuine advantage
when moving items is expensive — but it does nothing at all for the asymptotic cost, because
the nested sweep is untouched.

(Insertion sort manages 19 here rather than its usual count, because this particular input is
already partly in order. Its *worst* case is still quadratic.)

## The target

Mergesort sorts these same eight numbers in **16 comparisons**. On eight items that is
unremarkable — 16 against 28.

The point is the shape, not the count at `n = 8`. At `n = 1000` it is roughly **ten thousand
against half a million**, and the gap widens from there without limit.

Where do those 16 comparisons go? Every one of them happens inside a **merge**, which is the
next two pages.
