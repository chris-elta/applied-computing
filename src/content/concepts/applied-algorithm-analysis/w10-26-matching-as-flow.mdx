Bipartite matching looks like a new problem. It is a maximum-flow problem in disguise, so the
algorithm from the last section solves it.

## The construction

Start from the bipartite graph with sides `L` and `R`, and build a network:

1. Add a **source** `s` with an edge `s → u` to every `u` in `L`.
2. Direct every original edge **from L to R**.
3. Add a **sink** `t` with an edge `v → t` from every `v` in `R`.
4. Give **every edge capacity 1**.

## Paths are pairs

A unit of flow travels `s → u → v → t` with `u` in `L` and `v` in `R`. Its middle step,
`u → v`, is an original edge, so the unit **selects the pair `(u, v)`**.

## Why the pairs never clash

The unit capacities enforce the matching rule, through conservation:

| vertex | capacity that limits it | so |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `u` in `L` | inflow from `s → u` is at most 1 | outflow at most 1: `u` in at most one pair |
| `v` in `R` | outflow on `v → t` is at most 1 | inflow at most 1: `v` in at most one pair |

Each vertex carries at most one unit-flow path, which is exactly the requirement for a
matching.

The correspondence also runs the other way. Any matching gives a flow: send one unit along
`s → u → v → t` for each pair. So

```
maximum matching size  =  maximum flow value
```

## The integrality step

One thing could go wrong: an LP optimum could put ½ a unit on two different edges out of
`u`. Half-matches mean nothing. The slides settle this with a property shown earlier.
**When capacities are integers, Ford–Fulkerson starts from zero and keeps every flow integer.**
So it returns a flow in which every edge carries exactly 0 or 1, and the edges `u → v` with
flow 1 are the matching.

This is the point flagged in the LP relaxation concept. Rounding a fractional LP answer is
not reliable in general, but flow problems with integer capacities give integer answers
directly.

## On the opening example

Run max flow on the eight-person network. The answer is 4, the largest possible with four on
each side, and the four middle edges carrying flow are a perfect matching, for instance
Al–Beatrice, Bob–Danielle, Chet–Alice and Dan–Carol. The jobs example works the same way,
with people on one side, jobs on the other, and an edge wherever a person can do a job.

## Why reductions matter

The flow algorithm was not written for matching. The work was in the construction, and the
correctness argument came down to "unit capacities enforce one partner each". Rewriting a
new problem as one you can already solve is a technique to look for every time you meet one.

> Bipartite matching reduces to max flow: source to the left side, original edges left to
> right, right side to sink, all capacity 1. Unit capacities and conservation give each
> vertex at most one partner, and integer capacities give an integer flow, so the maximum
> flow is a maximum matching.
