A burglar finds more than the bag can hold. The bag carries at most `W` pounds. There are
`n` items; item `i` weighs `wᵢ` pounds and is worth `vᵢ` dollars, and every weight is a
whole number. Which items give the most value without going over the limit?

There are two versions of the question, and they turn out to need different tables:

| Version | Copies of each item | Also called |
| --- | --- | --- |
| With repetition | as many as you like | unbounded knapsack |
| Without repetition | one | 0/1 knapsack |

## The method everyone reaches for

Take the item worth the most per pound, then the next, until nothing fits. Try it with a
10-pound bag and one of each:

| Item | Weight | Value | Per lb |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | 6 lb | $30 | 5.00 |
| B | 3 lb | $14 | 4.67 |
| C | 4 lb | $16 | 4.00 |
| D | 2 lb | $9 | 4.50 |

- **Best value per pound.** A, then B. That is 9 pounds and $44, with one pound spare and
  nothing light enough to use it.
- **Best packing.** A and C. Exactly 10 pounds and $46.

The winning packing contains C, which is the *worst* item on the list by value per pound.

## Why it loses

After A goes in, 4 pounds are left. B fits but wastes a pound. C fills the 4 pounds exactly.
A ratio is a fact about one item on its own, so it cannot see how well the remaining space
gets used. That depends on which other items are in the bag.

It is tempting to patch the rule: break ties by weight, or look one step ahead. But nothing
was tied here. 5.00 and 4.67 are clearly ranked, and following that ranking is what loses.
No tie-breaking rule repairs it.

Methods that commit to the best-looking choice at each step are called greedy, and they get
a lecture of their own. Some problems they solve exactly. This is not one of them.

## What has to happen instead

To know that A and C beats A and B, you have to compare *whole packings*. Listing every
packing is hopeless, because `n` items have `2ⁿ` subsets. The table does the comparison
without the listing: it records the best value for every smaller bag once, and builds larger
bags out of those answers.

> A rule that scores each item separately cannot see wasted space. When the quality of a
> choice depends on what else gets chosen, you need the best answer to every smaller
> problem, and that is a table.
