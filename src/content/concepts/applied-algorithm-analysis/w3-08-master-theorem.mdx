> **Master theorem.** If `T(n) = aT(⌈n/b⌉) + O(nᵈ)` for constants `a > 0`, `b > 1`, `d ≥ 0`,
> then:
>
> | | when | meaning |
> | --- | --- | --- |
> | `O(nᵈ)` | `d > log_b a` | the root dominates |
> | `O(nᵈ log n)` | `d = log_b a` | balanced — every level costs the same |
> | `O(n^log_b a)` | `d < log_b a` | the leaves dominate |

Say the three cases in words before symbols: **the root dominates, every level costs the
same, the leaves dominate.** That is the whole content; the algebra is bookkeeping.

For Karatsuba: `a = 3`, `b = 2`, `d = 1`, and `log₂3 ≈ 1.59 > 1`. Third case, leaves dominate,
`T(n) = O(n^1.59)` — the same answer module 02 got by drawing the tree.

## The proof is the Karatsuba tree with letters

Assume `n` is a power of `b`, so the rounding in `⌈n/b⌉` can be ignored — it changes each
subproblem by less than 1 and the bound absorbs it.

**Height.** Each level divides the size by `b`, so size 1 is reached after `log_b n` levels.

**Work at level `k`.** The branching factor is `a`, so level `k` has `aᵏ` nodes, each of size
`n/bᵏ`. Each does `O((n/bᵏ)ᵈ)` work of its own:

```
aᵏ × O( (n/bᵏ)ᵈ )  =  O(nᵈ) × (a/bᵈ)ᵏ
```

Identical shape to module 02: a fixed `O(nᵈ)` multiplied by the `k`-th power of a single
ratio. For Karatsuba, `a/bᵈ` was `3/2`.

**Three cases.** As `k` runs from 0 to `log_b n`, those level costs form a geometric series
with ratio `a/bᵈ`. Summing has exactly three outcomes, and taking logs turns each into a line
of the theorem:

| ratio | series | sum is | in logs |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `a/bᵈ < 1` | decreasing | within a constant of the **first** term → `O(nᵈ)` | `a < bᵈ` means `d > log_b a` |
| `a/bᵈ = 1` | flat | all `log_b n + 1` terms equal → `O(nᵈ log n)` | `a = bᵈ` means `d = log_b a` |
| `a/bᵈ > 1` | increasing | within a constant of the **last** term → `O(n^log_b a)` | `a > bᵈ` means `d < log_b a` |

That "taking logs" step is the only thing connecting the proof to the statement. Everything
else you already did by hand for Karatsuba.

## Where the log factor comes from

Only the middle case produces `log n`, and the reason is worth holding on to: when the ratio
is exactly 1, the series is not geometric-and-collapsing — every level genuinely costs the
same, so the total is *one level's cost × the number of levels*.

In the other two cases the series collapses onto one end and the level count never enters the
answer.
