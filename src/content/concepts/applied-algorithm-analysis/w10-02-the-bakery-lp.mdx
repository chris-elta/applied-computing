A bakery makes two kinds of cookie and wants to choose how many of each to bake each day.

| cookie | profit each | maximum daily sales |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Classic | $1 | 200 |
| Deluxe | $6 | 300 |

The kitchen can make **400 cookies a day in total**, and everything baked within the sales
limits is sold. How many of each should it make?

Every **linear program** (LP) is built from the same three parts, and building one is
mostly a matter of naming them in order.

## 1 · Decision variables: what you choose

| variable | meaning |
| --- | --- |
| `x₁` | Classic cookies made per day |
| `x₂` | Deluxe cookies made per day |

Choose the variables first. Everything else is written in terms of them.

## 2 · Objective: what you are scoring

Each Classic earns $1 and each Deluxe earns $6, so the daily profit is

```
Z = x₁ + 6x₂
```

and the goal is to **maximise** `Z`.

## 3 · Constraints: what is allowed

| rule | constraint |
| --- | --- |
| Classic sales at most 200 | `x₁ ≤ 200` |
| Deluxe sales at most 300 | `x₂ ≤ 300` |
| total production at most 400 | `x₁ + x₂ ≤ 400` |
| cannot make negative cookies | `x₁ ≥ 0, x₂ ≥ 0` |

The last row is the one people forget. Nothing in the story says it, because no baker would
think of making −50 cookies. A model does not know that, so the rule has to be written down.

## The finished LP

```
maximise    Z = x₁ + 6x₂
subject to  x₁ ≤ 200
            x₂ ≤ 300
            x₁ + x₂ ≤ 400
            x₁, x₂ ≥ 0,   x₁, x₂ ∈ ℝ
```

The last line allows fractions. That is deliberate: a *continuous* LP sets aside the
requirement for whole cookies for now. The concept on the LP relaxation explains why
that is allowed and what it costs.

> An LP is three things written in terms of the decision variables: an objective to
> maximise or minimise, constraints that every plan must satisfy, and a domain for each
> variable. Write each story rule as one constraint, including the obvious ones.
