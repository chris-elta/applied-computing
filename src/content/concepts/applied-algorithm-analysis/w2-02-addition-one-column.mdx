Now that `n` means digits, addition can be priced honestly.

```
  1 1 1 1    carries
    1 1 0 1  (13)
  + 1 0 1 1  (11)
  ───────────
  1 1 0 0 0  (24)
```

## Why column addition works at all

One small fact, true in **any** base `b ≥ 2`:

> The sum of any three single-digit numbers is at most two digits.

Three is the right number to check — the two digits being added, plus an incoming carry. And
because the result is at most two digits, the carry going *out* is at most 1. That is what
lets the method work column by column without anything piling up.

So each column takes three bits in and puts two bits out — one digit of the answer, one
carry — and that work never grows. Only the number of columns grows.

> `n` columns × constant work each = **`O(n)`**

The animated figure on slide 9 walks the carry leftward one column at a time; running it once
makes the "constant work per column" claim concrete.

## And nothing can do better

That gives a ceiling. There is also a floor, and it is the more interesting half.

To produce a correct sum you must at least **read** both numbers, and that alone is `n`
operations. So every conceivable addition algorithm costs at least `Ω(n)`.

Notice what that `Ω` quantifies over. The `O(n)` was a statement about *this* method. The
`Ω(n)` is a statement about **every method there could ever be** — the first time in this
course that a lower bound describes a *problem* rather than an implementation.

Upper and lower bound meet, so addition is `Θ(n)`, and the question is closed. Column
addition is not merely good; it is optimal, and no cleverness will improve it.
