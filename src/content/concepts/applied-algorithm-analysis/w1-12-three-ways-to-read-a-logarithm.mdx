A logarithm answers exactly one question:

> **How many times must I multiply `b` by itself to reach `N`?**

`log₂ 8 = 3` because `2 × 2 × 2 = 8`. The small `b` is the **base** — the thing being
multiplied.

What makes logarithms feel slippery is that this one question appears in three very
different costumes. Recognising which one you are looking at is how you learn to predict
where a logarithm will show up.

## 1 · Digits — the space it takes to write down

Each extra digit position multiplies the range you can express by `b`. So "how many digit
positions?" *is* "how many multiplications by `b`?" This is the reading behind **input size
is bits**.

## 2 · Height — the depth of divide and conquer

`log₂ N` is the height of a binary tree with `N` leaves: each level down doubles the nodes,
so reaching `N` takes `log₂ N` doublings. Any algorithm splitting its problem in half, then
halving those, recurses this deep. This is next week's reading.

## 3 · Halvings — how many rounds until you are done

`log₂ N` is how many times you can halve `N` before reaching 1 — halving being the reverse
of doubling. This is the reading behind binary search, fast exponentiation and Euclid.

| reading | the question | shows up in |
| --- | --- | --- |
| digits | how long is this number to write? | input size, bit complexity |
| height | how deep does the splitting go? | divide and conquer, recurrences |
| halvings | how many rounds until this is 1? | binary search, Euclid, fast exponentiation |

## Why nobody writes the base

`log_b N` and `log₂ N` differ by the fixed factor `1 / log₂ b`, and fixed factors are what
asymptotic notation throws away. So `O(log n)` needs no base.

That licenses being casual about the base — **not** about the reading. When something turns
out logarithmic, knowing which mechanism produced it is what tells you why.
