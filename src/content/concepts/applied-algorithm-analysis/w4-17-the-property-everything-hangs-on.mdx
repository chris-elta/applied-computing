Square every `2n`-th root of unity and you land on the `n`-th roots — each one hit by exactly
two of the `2n` inputs. Algebraically:

```
(e^{2πik/2n})² = e^{2πik/n}
```

The reason is `ω^{k+n} = −ω^k` for a `2n`-th root `ω`: `ω^k` and the root exactly `n` steps
away differ only by a sign, and squaring erases sign. That redundancy — two different inputs
squaring to the same output — is precisely what the fast Fourier transform exploits.

## Why this is worth the detour

The applications go well beyond this course — signal processing, image processing, data
compression, solving partial differential equations, audio and video analysis all lean on
this same halving trick, applied not once but recursively down to a base case. It's widely
considered one of the most important numerical algorithms of the twentieth century, for
exactly that combination: breadth of application, and an efficiency gain that comes from one
clean piece of algebra rather than a bag of special cases. The next module turns this single
observation — half the evaluation points are secretly redundant — into a recurrence.
