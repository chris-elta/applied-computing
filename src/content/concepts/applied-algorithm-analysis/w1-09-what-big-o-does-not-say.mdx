Three mistakes get made with these definitions, and they are worth more attention than the
definitions themselves — you will meet all three in code review.

## 1. `O` is an upper bound, not "the" complexity

An algorithm that genuinely runs in `Θ(n²)` is also, truthfully, `O(n⁵)`. Check it: is `n²`
eventually below some constant times `n⁵`? Easily. The statement is correct — and useless.

`O` only rules out things being *worse*. `Θ` is what pins the rate. Since designers want
guarantees, and a guarantee is naturally an upper bound, `O` is what you read everywhere.

> **Say `Θ` when you mean `Θ`.**

## 2. `O`/`Ω`/`Θ` and best/average/worst are different axes

Usually confused via a belief that `O` means worst case and `Ω` means best case. It does
not. Two unrelated questions:

- **How tight is the bound?** `O` (ceiling), `Ω` (floor), `Θ` (both).
- **Which input?** Best, average, or worst case.

A statement needs one from *each*. So:

> "Quicksort is `O(n²)` in the worst case and `Θ(n log n)` on average."

is one consistent sentence making two independent claims. Nothing contradicts anything.

## 3. Constants vanish only in the limit

Asymptotics discard constants *because* they stop mattering as `n` grows — the catch being
"as `n` grows". Insertion sort is `O(n²)` and mergesort `O(n log n)`, so mergesort wins,
eventually. Below a few dozen elements insertion sort is genuinely faster, and real sorting
libraries switch to it below a threshold.

> **Asymptotics pick the algorithm, not the implementation.**

## The habit

When you read an asymptotic claim, ask which of the three it leans on:

1. Is an upper bound being passed off as the exact rate?
2. Which case is meant — and did anyone say?
3. Is `n` actually large enough for the constants to have stopped mattering?

Most arguments about performance turn out to be arguments about the case, not the bound.
