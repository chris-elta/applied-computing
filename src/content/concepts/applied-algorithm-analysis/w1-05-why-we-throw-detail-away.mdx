Suppose you count carefully and find an algorithm takes exactly `5n³ + 4n + 3` steps.

Read that as English first: three **terms** added together. `n³` means `n × n × n`. The `5`
in front is a **coefficient** — a plain multiplier.

Precise. Also more precise than useful, and here is why we throw most of it away.

## Watch the terms fight

| `n` | `5n³` | `4n + 3` as a share |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 5 | 58% |
| 10 | 5,000 | 0.9% |
| 100 | 5,000,000 | 0.008% |
| 1,000 | 5,000,000,000 | 0.00008% |

The `3` never mattered. The `4n` stops mattering almost immediately. By `n = 100` the answer
is `5n³` and a rounding error. Keeping them would be **noise dressed as precision**.

## The `5` goes for a different reason

The lower-order terms went because they become negligible. The coefficient goes because it
was never really a property of the algorithm: change the language, compiler, processor, or
what you counted as "one step", and 5 becomes 3 or 11. It describes your measuring setup as
much as your algorithm.

So we say `O(n³)`.

> **We report the shape, not the number.**

## Seven words

> Suppress constant factors and lower-order terms.

- **Constant factors** — too machine-dependent to carry.
- **Lower-order terms** — irrelevant once the input is large.

## This granularity was chosen

Not a mathematical accident — a deliberate setting, picked to be **coarse enough** to hide
your hardware and **precise enough** to still separate exponential from linear, quadratic
from linearithmic.

And it deliberately gives something up. `O(n³)` cannot tell you which of two `O(n log n)`
sorts to ship, because it discarded exactly what decides that. Once the shape is settled,
constants matter again — that is engineering, and it comes *after* this analysis.
