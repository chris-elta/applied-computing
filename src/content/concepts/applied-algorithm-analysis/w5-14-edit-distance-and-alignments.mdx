How different are "cat" and "cart"? One letter. "Cat" and "bat"? Also one letter, in a
different sense. A spell-checker needs a single number that covers both.

## Three operations

The **edit distance** between two strings is the minimum number of operations needed to
turn one into the other, where each operation costs 1:

| Operation | Does | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Insertion | adds a character | "cat" → "cart" |
| Deletion | removes a character | "cart" → "cat" |
| Substitution | replaces a character | "cat" → "bat" |

## "At most" is not "exactly"

Turn "kitten" into "sitting":

1. "kitten" → "sitten" — substitute `k → s`
2. "sitten" → "sittin" — substitute `e → i`
3. "sittin" → "sitting" — insert `g`

So the edit distance is **at most** 3. Does this prove it is exactly 3? No. Three operations
show that three are enough; they do not show that two are not. To claim a minimum you have
to account for every possible sequence of edits, and that is what the table in the coming
concepts does.

## Alignments

Thinking in sequences of edits is awkward — the string keeps changing under you. A stiller
picture is an **alignment**: write the two strings one above the other, allowing gaps.
Here are two alignments of SNOWY with SUNNY:

```
S − N O W Y          − S N O W − Y
S U N N − Y          S U N − − N Y
cost 3               cost 5
```

The **cost** of an alignment is the number of columns in which the two symbols differ, and a
gap always differs. Each such column is one edit: a letter over a different letter is a
substitution, a letter over a gap is a deletion, and a gap over a letter is an insertion.

The edit distance is the cost of the **best** alignment.

## Why this picture helps

An alignment is built one column at a time, and each column is one of only three kinds:

- letter over letter,
- letter over gap,
- gap over letter.

Now remove the last column. What remains is an alignment of two shorter strings — a prefix
of each. That is a smaller problem of exactly the same kind. (Removing the first column
works as well; what is left is then two suffixes.)

This is the recipe's step 2 question, "what was the last decision?", answered before any
table exists: the last decision is the last column, and it has three possible shapes.

> An example proves an upper limit. A minimum needs every alternative accounted for — and
> the way to do that is to find a structure, here the last column, that every alternative
> must have.
