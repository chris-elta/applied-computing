Big-O says "no bigger than". That is one of four things you might want to say. The fastest
way in is to read them as comparison signs between functions:

| symbol | say it | behaves like | means |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `O` | big-oh | `≤` | grows no faster than |
| `Ω` | big-omega | `≥` | grows at least as fast as |
| `Θ` | big-theta | `=` | grows at the same rate as |
| `o` | little-oh | `<` | grows strictly slower than |

`Ω` and `Θ` are capital Greek letters, used purely to stay visually distinct from ordinary
algebra.

## Omega and Theta

**`Ω`** is Big-O with the inequality flipped: `T(n) ≥ c·f(n)` beyond `n₀`. Is `n² = Ω(n)`?
Yes — `c = 1`, `n₀ = 1`.

**`Θ`** demands both bounds at once: `c₁·f(n) ≤ T(n) ≤ c₂·f(n)`. Two constants, numbered so
they can be discussed apart — one holds the floor up, the other the ceiling down. `T(n)` is
trapped, so it cannot be growing faster or slower. It grows *the same*.

Is `n² = Θ(n³)`? No. The ceiling is fine, but no constant multiple of `n³` stays **below**
`n²` forever. The floor fails.

## Theta is honest, so why does everyone write O?

Designers want **guarantees**, and a guarantee is naturally an upper bound: "never worse
than…". `Θ(n log n)` promises that *and* rules out doing better — more than a guarantee
needs. The habit is harmless as long as you say `Θ` when you mean it.

## Little-o is a different quantifier

Watch one word move:

- **Big-O**: **there exists** a `c` with `T(n) ≤ c·f(n)` eventually.
- **Little-o**: **for every** `c > 0`, `T(n) ≤ c·f(n)` eventually.

Big-O lets you shop for a constant. Little-o says *every* constant works, however tiny — you
may hand it `c = 0.0001` and it must still hold from some point on. That turns a loose bound
into a strict one.

Is `3n = o(n)`? Take `c = 1`: you would need `3n ≤ n`, which never happens. So no — `3n` is
`O(n)` but not `o(n)`, because they grow at the *same* rate. Is `3n = o(n²)`? Yes: whatever
tiny `c` you name, `3n ≤ c·n²` once `n ≥ 3/c`.

> These relate **functions**, not numbers. The `=` in `n² = Ω(n)` is historical and reads
> better as "is".
