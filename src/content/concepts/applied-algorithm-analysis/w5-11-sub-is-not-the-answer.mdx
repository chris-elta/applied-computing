Run the two rules on `nums = [10, 5, 8, 3, 9]` and the result is `sub = [3, 8, 9]`. It is
tempting to print that as the longest increasing subsequence. Check it against the input
first: in `nums`, the 3 comes **after** the 8. `[3, 8, 9]` is not a subsequence at all.

## What the entries really are

Each entry is the tail of a *different* subsequence:

| Entry | Is the tail of |
| --- | --- |
| `sub[0] = 3` | `[3]` |
| `sub[1] = 8` | `[5, 8]` |
| `sub[2] = 9` | `[5, 8, 9]` |

The real answer is `[5, 8, 9]`. The 5 used to be in `sub[0]`; it was replaced by 3 because 3
is a better tail for length 1. That replacement did not reach back and change the
subsequences that had already been built on the 5.

So `sub` gives the right **length** and the wrong **contents**. Printing it is the classic
bug with this method.

That leaves a fair question: if the contents are wrong, why believe the length? It takes two
short arguments, one for each direction.

## Never too long

Every entry of `sub` is the tail of a real increasing subsequence. When `x` goes into
position `k`, it follows the real subsequence behind `sub[k−1]`, which ends earlier in
`nums`. So if `sub` has `L` entries, a real increasing subsequence of length `L` exists. The
method cannot overclaim.

## Never too short

Two facts: an entry can only be replaced by a smaller or equal number, and `sub` never gets
shorter.

Now follow the real subsequence `5, 8, 9` through the run. The 5 lands at position 0. When 8
arrives, whatever sits in `sub[0]` is at most 5 — it has only been able to shrink — and
`sub[0] ≤ 5 < 8`. So the first entry ≥ 8 is not at position 0; the 8 lands at position 1 or
later. The same reasoning pushes 9 to position 2 or later. So `sub` has at least 3 entries.

The same works for any real increasing subsequence: each of its numbers is forced at least
one position further right than the one before. The method cannot underclaim.

Both together: the length of `sub` equals the length of the LIS.

> A data structure can be right about a number and wrong about everything else. `sub` was
> designed to track lengths, and it threw away the rest on purpose. Getting the subsequence
> back needs extra bookkeeping, which the next two concepts add.
