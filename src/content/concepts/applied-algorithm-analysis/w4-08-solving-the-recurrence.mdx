Write `T(n)` for the **average** running time on a list of `n` elements. One call to selection
does two things, in order:

**1 · Wait for a good pivot.** Pick a random pivot and split. If it isn't good, pick again
from the surviving pile and split again, until a good pivot turns up. The list only ever
shrinks, so each split is one pass over at most `n` elements — and the coin-flip lemma says a
good pivot arrives after 2 picks on average. So this phase costs at most 2 passes over `n`, on
average.

**2 · Recurse.** A good pivot leaves a pile of at most `3n/4` elements, and running selection
on that pile costs `T(3n/4)` — at most, since a longer list can't be quicker.

The two phases happen one after the other, so their costs add:

```
T(n) ≤ 2n  +  T(3n/4)
```

## The conclusion

```
T(n) ≤ T(3n/4) + O(n)   ⟹   T(n) = O(n)
```

On any input, on average, the divide-and-conquer selection algorithm is **linear**.

> **Where the constant comes from.** `n + ¾n + (¾)²n + ⋯ = n/(1−¾) = 4n` — the whole recursion
> costs about four passes over the input.

That series is worth double-checking against the `2n` bound on phase 1: multiplying `2n`
through the same series gives `8n`, not `4n`, and both are legitimate upper bounds — `8n` is
the safe one, `4n` the tighter headline number the deck reports, since the bad pivots wasted
inside phase 1 are themselves shrinking the list rather than doing nothing. Either way, four
or eight passes against `n log n`: at a million elements that is single-digit millions of
operations, not tens of millions — and the gap only widens as `n` grows.
