Termination was easy. A **rate** needs one more result.

## The lemma

> If `a ≥ b` then `a mod b < a/2`.

Two cases, and between them they cover everything:

- **If `b ≤ a/2`** — the remainder is always smaller than `b`, and `b` is already at most
  `a/2`. Done.
- **If `b > a/2`** — then `b` goes into `a` exactly once, so `a mod b = a − b`. And since `b`
  is more than half of `a`, what is left is less than half of `a`. Done.

Either way the larger value **halves every two rounds**. An `n`-bit number can only be halved
`n` times, so there are at most `2n` rounds.

## Seeing it

The figure on slide 37 draws the larger number at each step to scale, for two pairs:

- **`gcd(1035, 759)`** — a typical pair. `1035 → 759 → 276 → 207 → 69 → 0`. Four steps, and
  the bars fall away sharply.
- **`gcd(610, 377)`** — consecutive Fibonacci numbers, the slowest Euclid ever runs. Thirteen
  steps, each remainder a single subtraction: `610 → 377 → 233 → 144 → 89 → 55 → …`

Even in that worst case, every bar is still under half the bar two rows above. The lemma
holds, and the Fibonacci pair is exactly the sequence that hugs it as tightly as possible.

> Fibonacci turning up as Euclid's worst case is not a coincidence. Numbers whose ratio is
> closest to `φ` are the ones that resist division hardest — which is a pleasing place to end
> a course that opened with `fib1`.

## The cost

Same accounting as fast exponentiation — rounds × cost per round:

**Rounds:** at most `2n`, by the lemma.
**Per round:** one division, `a mod b`, priced at `O(n²)`.

> `2n` rounds × `O(n²)` per round = **`O(n³)`**

Use the stepper on slide 38 to run both pairs yourself; watching 610/377 grind out one step
per Fibonacci term makes the worst case concrete in a way the bound does not.
