The bakery from week 10 makes Classic cookies (`x₁` per day) and Deluxe cookies (`x₂` per
day):

```
max   Z = x₁ + 6x₂
s.t.  x₁        ≤ 200
           x₂   ≤ 300
      x₁ + x₂   ≤ 400
      x₁, x₂    ≥ 0
```

The optimal plan found there was `(x₁, x₂) = (100, 300)`, earning `100 + 6(300) = $1900`.

Suppose someone doubts that. Showing them the plan proves $1900 is *possible*. It does not
prove nothing better exists. Last week the reply was "compare every vertex" or "trust
simplex". Is there a proof that uses only the constraints?

## Multiply and add

Every feasible plan obeys all three constraints. So it also obeys any constraint you can
derive from them. Take the three constraints, multiply them by 0, 5 and 1, and add:

| multiplier | constraint | weighted constraint |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | `x₁ ≤ 200` | `0x₁ ≤ 0` |
| 5 | `x₂ ≤ 300` | `5x₂ ≤ 1500` |
| 1 | `x₁ + x₂ ≤ 400` | `x₁ + x₂ ≤ 400` |
| | **sum** | `x₁ + 6x₂ ≤ 1900` |

The left-hand side of the sum is the profit, exactly. So the last line reads: **every
feasible plan earns at most $1900.**

Why is adding allowed? If one quantity is at most 1500 and another is at most 400, their
total is at most 1900. Each weighted line is true of every feasible plan, so the sum is too.

## The two halves

| what you show | what it proves |
| --- | --- |
| the plan `(100, 300)` is feasible and earns 1900 | the maximum is **at least** 1900 |
| the weighted sum `x₁ + 6x₂ ≤ 1900` | the maximum is **at most** 1900 |

Together: the maximum is $1900. The plan attains the bound, and the argument is the flow and
cut argument again with the multipliers `(0, 5, 1)` standing where the cut stood. Nobody has
to rerun an algorithm to check it. They multiply three lines and add.

## The obvious question

The numbers 0, 5 and 1 appeared from nowhere. They work, but a proof that depends on a lucky
guess is not a method. The next concepts work out which multipliers are allowed, and then
how to find the best ones systematically.

> The constraints of an LP can be multiplied by numbers and added to give a new inequality
> that every feasible plan obeys. If its left-hand side is the objective, its right-hand side
> is an upper bound on the optimum, and a plan that attains it is proved optimal.
