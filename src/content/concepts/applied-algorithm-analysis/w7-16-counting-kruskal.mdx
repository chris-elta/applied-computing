Kruskal with union-find has three kinds of work. Count each over the whole run, for a
connected simple graph with `n ≥ 2` vertices and `m` edges.

| Work | How much | Cost |
| --- | --- | --- |
| initialise | one group per vertex | `O(n)` |
| sort edges | all `m` edges by weight | `O(m log m)` |
| check and merge | at most `m` edges, each with two Finds and at most one Union | `O(m log n)` |

The `O(m log n)` line uses the bound from union by rank: every Find and every Union is
`O(log n)`.

## Turning `log m` into `log n`

Sorting is priced in `log m`, everything else in `log n`. For a simple graph,

`m ≤ n(n − 1)/2 < n²`, so `log m < log n² = 2 log n`.

The 2 is a constant, so `log m = O(log n)`, and the sorting term is also `O(m log n)`.

## The total

`O(n + m log m + m log n) = O((n + m) log n)`.

That is the **same bound as heap-based Prim**. The log factor comes from different places in
the two algorithms:

| | Where the `log n` comes from |
| --- | --- |
| Prim | heap operations: extract-min and decrease-key |
| Kruskal | sorting, and the `O(log n)` Find/Union bound |

## When to stop

**Stop after `n − 1` accepted edges.** A spanning tree has exactly that many (Fact 1), so
any edge after that would be rejected. In the five-vertex example, Kruskal stopped after
`(b, d)` and never examined `(c, d)` or `(d, e)`. Stopping early does not change the
worst-case bound, because the last edge needed may be the most expensive one in the graph.

**On a disconnected graph, process every edge.** The count never reaches `n − 1`, because no
edge joins the separate pieces. Kruskal still makes the correct choice inside each piece, and
when the sorted list runs out it has a **minimum spanning forest**: one MST per connected
piece. Kruskal never relied on the graph being connected, only the stopping rule did.

## The sorting term

If the edges arrive already sorted, the `O(m log m)` term disappears, but the component
checks do not. Each edge still needs two Finds, so the total is `O(n + m log n)`. The
comparison slide at the end of the lecture makes the same point.

> Initialise, sort, then check and merge. Since `log m = O(log n)` on a simple graph, the
> total is `O((n + m) log n)`, the same bound as Prim, with the log factor coming from sorting
> and Find instead of a heap.
