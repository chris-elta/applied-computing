Suppose someone hands you a production plan and says it is the best one. How could you
check? Testing every other plan is out of the question when quantities can take any value.
This week's tools all answer that question with the same two-part argument.

For a **maximisation** problem:

| step | what you produce | what it proves |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | a **feasible** answer, one that satisfies every constraint | the optimum is *at least* this good: a **lower bound** |
| 2 | an argument that *every* feasible answer is at most some value | the optimum is *at most* this good: an **upper bound** |

If the two numbers are equal, the plan from step 1 is optimal. Nothing can beat the upper
bound, and the plan already reaches it.

The week's running example is a bakery. A plan that earns $1900 a day is a lower bound. A
proof that no plan can earn more than $1900 is an upper bound. Together they settle the
question.

## Why both halves are needed

Each half alone is weak:

- **A feasible answer alone** proves nothing about the others. The next plan you try might
  be better.
- **An upper bound alone** might be loose. "No plan earns more than a million dollars" is
  true and useless.

Real progress is closing the gap: better plans push the lower bound up, better arguments
push the upper bound down. When they meet, you stop.

## The road through the week

The same idea appears three times:

1. **Linear programs.** For an LP, a corner of the feasible region is always at least as
   good as any other point. Comparing the corners gives both halves at once.
2. **Network flow.** A flow is the feasible answer. A *cut*, a set of edges every unit of
   flow must cross, is the upper bound. When a flow and a cut have equal value, the flow is
   maximum.
3. **Bipartite matching.** Matching is rewritten as a flow problem, so it inherits the
   flow argument.

Matching the flow to the cut is the course's first example of *duality*, which a later
lesson develops in full.

> To prove an answer is best, give a feasible answer and a bound that every feasible answer
> obeys. When the two values are equal, you have both the answer and the proof that it is
> optimal.
