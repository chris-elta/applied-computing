Not everything can be learned by asking people. An **observation study** watches what
happens and counts it. The subjects may be people, animals, plants or non-living objects: a
class, a user at a keyboard, a server room.

The lecture's description:

- the observations are **quantifiable**;
- the study has a **particular, prespecified focus**;
- the behaviour being studied is **quantified in some way**;
- it needs **considerable advance planning**, meticulous attention to detail and a great deal
  of time;
- it offers a **quantitative alternative** to qualitative approaches.

## Keeping it objective

The risk is that the observer's view becomes the data. Five practices push against it:

| Practice | Why |
| --- | --- |
| **Define the behaviour precisely and concretely** so it is easily recognised when it occurs | 'Confused' becomes 'pauses more than ten seconds, or opens help' |
| **Divide the period into small segments** and record whether the behaviour occurs in each | Gives counts rather than impressions |
| **Use a rating scale** to evaluate specific dimensions of the behaviour | Records degree as well as presence |
| **Have two or three people rate independently** | Disagreement becomes visible |
| **Train raters on specific criteria** until ratings are consistent for any single occurrence | Disagreement is then removed at its source |

The last two together are a way of checking *reliability* (Week 3): if independent raters
agree, the instrument is consistent.

> An observation is only data once two different people would have recorded the same thing.
