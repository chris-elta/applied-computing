This is the most misunderstood idea in the week, and the lecture spends three slides on it.

## The tempting reading

After computing the fuel-capacity interval, it is natural to say: *there is a 95% chance that
`μ` lies between 17.8 and 20.0*, or `P(17.8 < μ < 20.0) = 0.95`. The lecture's answer is a
flat **no**.

## The weather forecast

> A meteorologist says there is a 10% chance of rain this weekend. On Monday, does that mean
> there is a 10% chance that it rained over the weekend?

No. **Either it rained or it did not**, so the probability is 0 or 1. The forecast made sense
*before* the weekend, and it is a statement about the forecasting method.

The interval works the same way:

| | The population mean `μ` | The interval |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Before** collecting data | Fixed, unknown | Not yet determined: it will be random, and has a 95% chance of covering `μ` |
| **After** collecting data | Still fixed, unknown | Now fixed: it either contains `μ` or it does not, so the probability is 0 or 1 |

The lecture adds that *the fact that we do not know what `μ` is does not change this.*
Ignorance is not probability.

## The picture

Slide 18 shows **50 intervals**, each a 90% interval from a different sample of size `n` from
the same population. A vertical line marks `μ`. Intervals that cross the line contain it
(black); the ones that miss are red. We **expect about 90%** of the fifty to contain `μ`, so
around five red ones.

The confidence belongs to the **procedure**: if you repeated the whole study many times, about
95% of the intervals would capture the parameter. Your own interval is one draw from that
process.

## A defensible way to say it

*'We used a method that captures the true mean 95% of the time, and this is the interval it
gave us.'* That is longer than the shortcut, and it is correct.

> Confidence describes how reliable the recipe is, not how likely this particular answer is.
