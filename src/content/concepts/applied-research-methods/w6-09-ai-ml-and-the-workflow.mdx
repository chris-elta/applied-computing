The terms get used loosely, so the lecture pins them down first.

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **Artificial intelligence (AI)** | Simulating human intelligence in machines |
| **Machine learning (ML)** | A **subset** of AI that **learns from data** |
| **Algorithm** | The specific procedure that does the learning, such as a decision tree or k-means |

So all ML is AI, but not all AI is ML. A hand-written rule system that plays chess is AI
without learning anything from data. Within ML, the lecture names **three types of
algorithm**: **supervised**, **unsupervised** and **reinforcement** learning. Each gets its
own concept in this section. The data an ML study works with is split three ways, into
**training**, **validation** and **testing** sets, covered in *Training, Validation and Test
Sets*.

## Six steps

The lecture's workflow for an ML study:

1. **Define the goal and available data.**
2. **Choose the problem type**, which determines the algorithm type.
3. **Select a specific algorithm**, considering:
   - the nature of the problem (classification, regression, clustering);
   - the data's size, quality and structure;
   - interpretability and computational efficiency.
4. **Prepare and split the data.**
5. **Train, test and validate.**
6. **Analyse the results.**

The order is the point. The algorithm is chosen at **step 3**, after the goal and the problem
type are fixed. Starting from "I want to use transformers" and looking for a problem is the
ML version of the engineering-task trap from *Engineering Task or Research Problem?*: the
method has been decided, so nothing about it is uncertain.

## The pipeline

The lecture draws steps 4 to 6 as a pipeline:

| Stage | What happens |
| --- | --- |
| **Pre-processing** | Clean and prepare the raw data, then split it |
| **Training data → model building** | The algorithm fits a model to the training data |
| **Testing data → model evaluation** | The model is scored on data it has not seen |

The split happens **before** model building, and the testing data flows *around* model
building straight to evaluation. That is deliberate. A model scored on data it trained on is
being asked questions it has already seen the answers to.

Step 3's criteria matter for research in particular. An algorithm that is slightly more
accurate but impossible to interpret may be the wrong choice if your research question is
*why* something happens. You will have to justify the choice in your methods section, as
*Planning ML Research* asks.

> Goal, then problem type, then algorithm. Keep the data that judges the model away from the
> data that builds it.
