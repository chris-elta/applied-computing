When you want to know what a large group thinks or does, you cannot ask all of them. The
**survey** asks some of them and generalises.

## The design

The lecture states the goal: **learn about a large population by surveying a sample of that
population**. The simple design has three moves:

1. The researcher **poses a series of questions**.
2. The responses are **quantified**.
3. The researcher **draws inferences about the population** from the sample's responses.

Surveys gather **characteristics, opinions and attitudes** from one or more groups of
people, by asking and tabulating answers.

## What a survey cannot do

The lecture marks two limits:

- **It captures a fleeting moment.** The responses are a transitory collection of data, from
  which one extrapolates to the state of affairs over a longer period. That extrapolation is
  an assumption.
- **It relies on self-report.** People may misremember, misunderstand the question, or say
  what seems desirable.

The textbook's example is a survey on environmental attitudes and behaviour.

The rest of Week 4 follows the survey's pipeline: how to **ask** (collecting data), **whom**
to ask (sampling), what can **distort** the answers (bias), and what to do with the numbers
(statistics).

> A survey tells you what a sample said at one moment. Everything beyond that is inference,
> and the quality of the inference depends on the sample.
