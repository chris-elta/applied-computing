Not everything is a mean. Often you want a **proportion**: the share of bulbs that are
defective, users who click, requests that fail. The lecture's key observation is that it is
**the same formula, with the standard deviation for proportions in place of the one for
means**.

| | Mean | Proportion |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Estimate** | `x̄` | `p̂` |
| **Spread of the estimate** | `σ/√n` | `√(p̂(1 − p̂)/n)` |
| **Interval** | `x̄ ± z* · σ/√n` | `p̂ ± z* · √(p̂(1 − p̂)/n)` |

## A condition to check first

The interval uses the normal curve, which is only a good approximation when there are
enough cases in both categories. The lecture's rule of thumb:

`n·p̂ ≥ 5` and `n·(1 − p̂) ≥ 5`

## Worked example: light bulbs

A random sample of **100 bulbs** has **6 defective**. Find a **98% confidence interval** for
the proportion of defective bulbs the plant produces.

**Check the conditions.** `p̂ = 6/100 = 0.06`. Then `n·p̂ = 6 ≥ 5` ✓ and
`n·(1 − p̂) = 94 ≥ 5` ✓.

**Compute.** For 98%, `z* = 2.326`:

| Step | Working |
| --- | --- |
| Interval | `0.06 ± 2.326 × √(0.06 × 0.94 / 100)` |
| Margin | `2.326 × 0.02375 = 0.0552` |
| Result | `(0.0047, 0.1152)` |

**Conclusion.** Somewhere between about **0.5% and 11.5%** of bulbs are expected to be
defective.

The interval is wide, because only 6 defective bulbs were observed. A larger sample would
narrow it, in proportion to `√n`, as with means.

> A proportion is a mean of zeros and ones. That is why the machinery is the same, with its
> own measure of spread.
