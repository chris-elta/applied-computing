Inferential statistics have two main jobs: estimate a population parameter from a random
sample, and test hypotheses. The lecture's first job comes in two forms.

## Point estimate

A **point estimate** is a **single statistic taken as a reasonable estimate of the
corresponding population parameter**. The sample mean `x̄` estimates the population mean `μ`.

The lecture's caution: it **typically does not correspond exactly** with its true equivalent
in the population. Draw another sample and you will get another number. A point estimate by
itself tells you nothing about how far off it might be.

## Interval estimate

An **interval estimate** is a **range within which a population parameter probably lies**.
It is also called a **confidence interval**, and it provides a certain **level of confidence**
that the range does contain the parameter.

| | Point | Interval |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Form** | One number | A range |
| **Example** | 42 minutes a day | Between 38 and 46 minutes a day, with 95% confidence |
| **Says how precise it is?** | No | Yes: the width |

The width of the interval is the useful part: a narrow interval means the sample pins the
parameter down; a wide one says you do not know much yet.

Week 5 gives the arithmetic for building one, with worked examples, and takes care with
what 'confidence' does and does not mean. There the reading is subtler than it looks.

> Report an estimate with its precision. A bare number overstates what a sample can tell you.
