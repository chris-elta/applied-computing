**The problem.** An energy-drink maker claims **40 mg** of sodium per can. A consumer group
takes a **random sample of 50 cans** and finds a **mean of 41.4 mg**; the standard deviation
across all cans is **σ = 7.2 mg**. They suspect **more** than 40 mg.

(Two slides in the deck mention a sample mean of 42.6 mg. The data in the problem and the
calculation both use 41.4, which is what is used here.)

## Step by step

| Step | Working |
| --- | --- |
| **Hypotheses** | `H₀: μ = 40`, `Hₐ: μ > 40` |
| **Sampling distribution under `H₀`** | `N(40, 7.2/√50) = N(40, 1.0182)` |
| **Test statistic** | `z = (41.4 − 40) / 1.0182 = 1.3749` |
| **p-value** (upper tail only) | `P(Z ≥ 1.3749) = 0.0846` |
| **Compare with α** | `0.0846 > 0.05` |

## Conclusions

- **Statistical:** fail to reject `H₀`.
- **Contextual:** there is **not sufficient evidence** to suggest the drink has more than
  40 mg of sodium per can.

## What this result does and does not say

The sample mean *was* higher than the claim, by 1.4 mg. A gap that size would arise about
8.5% of the time from sampling variation alone if the true mean were 40. That is
not rare enough to be convinced, though it is not nothing either: it is the *shade of gray*
that the lecture warns about.

It does **not** say the drink contains exactly 40 mg. This is a possible Type II error, a real
excess that the sample was too small to confirm. Collecting more data would settle it,
since the standard deviation of the mean falls as `√n` grows. Had the same mean of 41.4 come
from 200 cans, `z` would be about 2.75 and the null would be rejected.

Compare this with the soda cans: a smaller gap in standard-deviation terms, a different
verdict. Both used the same procedure; only the amount of surprise differed.

> 'Fail to reject' is the honest conclusion when the data do not settle the question. It is
> not a verdict for the other side.
