Before you can write a good paper you need to read bad and good ones critically. The lecture
asks three questions of any article: *what is its merit, is it presented well, and does it
have a logical structure?* The structure it expects is below.

| Part | What it should do |
| --- | --- |
| **Abstract** | Summarise the paper, including the conclusions as well as the original problem |
| **Introduction** | Explain why the topic is important and review other work |
| **Body** | Approach and analysis; results that are the basis for claims; errors and uncertainties; the "right" level of detail |
| **Conclusions** | Summarise, with no surprises or new information; point to future work |
| **References** | Other work, especially important for recent work |
| **Tables and figures** | Convey findings visually and describe the design and concepts, each with a caption |

## What to check in the body

The body is where the paper earns its claims, so spend your attention there:

- Is the **approach** described well enough that someone could repeat it (replication, from
  earlier this week)?
- Do the **results** actually form the basis for the claims made in the conclusion?
- Does the paper report **errors and uncertainties**, or present everything as exact?
- Is the level of detail right: enough to follow, not so much that the argument is lost?

A paper that fails one of these is not necessarily wrong. It is a paper whose claims you
cannot yet trust, and the fact that you can name why is the skill.

> Read the conclusion, then check whether the body earned it.
