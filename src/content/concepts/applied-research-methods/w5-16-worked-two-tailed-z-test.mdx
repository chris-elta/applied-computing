**The problem.** A computer shop chain sells **3,400** items a week on average (**σ = 500**). A
new marketing campaign starts and you wonder whether sales **changed**, up or down. You
sample **40 stores for a week** and find a mean of **3,550**.

## The steps

| Step | Working |
| --- | --- |
| **Hypotheses** | `H₀: μ = 3400`, `Hₐ: μ ≠ 3400` (a change, either way) |
| **Sampling distribution under `H₀`** | `N(3400, 500/√40) = N(3400, 79.0569)` |
| **Test statistic** | `z = (3550 − 3400) / 79.0569 = 1.8974` |
| **p-value** (both tails) | `2 × P(Z ≥ 1.8974) = 2 × 0.0289 = 0.0578` |
| **Compare** | `0.0578 > 0.05` |

## Conclusions

- **Statistical:** fail to reject `H₀`.
- **Contextual:** there is not sufficient evidence to suggest that the campaign had an impact
  on sales.

## Why double?

The question was *'did sales change?'*, so an *unusually low* sample would have been just as
interesting as an unusually high one. The p-value is the probability of a result **at least as
far from 3,400 in either direction**, so it takes the upper-tail area (beyond +1.90) and adds
the matching lower-tail area (beyond −1.90).

The consequence is important. The same z-score of 1.8974 would give `p = 0.0289` for a
one-sided test and **reject** at 0.05, whereas the two-sided test does **not**. The choice
between one-sided and two-sided is therefore not cosmetic: it changes the answer here.
Which to use must follow from the question, **decided before looking at the data**.

If you wanted to know only whether the campaign *increased* sales, `Hₐ: μ > 3400` would be
the honest question. But if a drop would also have prompted action, the two-sided test is the
one that fits.

> The number of tails follows from the question you asked, not from the result you got.
