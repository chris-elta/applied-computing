Sometimes randomisation is not possible (you cannot randomly assign people to different
offices) or not practical. The lecture calls the designs for those situations
**quasi-experimental**, and says plainly that you **cannot control for all confounding
variables** in them. They compensate by adding **observations over time**.

| Design | Idea |
| --- | --- |
| **8. Nonrandomised control group, pretest-posttest** | Like Design 4, but groups are existing ones rather than randomly assigned |
| **9. Simple time series** | Measure the same thing many times before and after the treatment |
| **10. Control group time series** | A time series for the treated group and another for a comparison group |
| **11. Reversal time series** | Introduce the treatment, withdraw it, and watch whether the measure changes back |
| **12. Alternating treatment** | Switch between treatments repeatedly and compare the measure under each |
| **13. Multiple baseline** | Start the treatment at different times for different people or settings |

## Why time helps

A single before-and-after pair can be explained by anything that happened in between. A
**series** shows the trend that was already under way. If your measure was rising steadily
before the treatment, a rise after it means little; if it was flat and then jumped, that is
more persuasive.

The reversal, alternating and multiple-baseline designs go further by making the treatment
*come and go*. If the measure follows the treatment on and off, or changes exactly when each
person starts, a coincidence becomes hard to sustain.

The slide lists only names. The descriptions above are the plain meaning of each name, and the
textbook has the details.

> Where you cannot randomise, replace one comparison with many.
