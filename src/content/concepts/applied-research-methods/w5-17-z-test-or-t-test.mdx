Every example so far assumed a known population standard deviation `σ`. In real work you
rarely have it. The lecture's two-column slide sets out the choice.

| | **z-test** | **t-test** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Use when** | You know the population standard deviation | You do not |
| **Statistic** | `z = (x̄ − μ₀) / (σ/√n)` | `t = (x̄ − μ₀) / (s/√n)` |
| **Spread used** | Population `σ` | Sample `s` |
| **p-value from** | The normal distribution | A t-distribution with `n − 1` **degrees of freedom** |

## Why a different curve?

`s` is itself an estimate and varies from sample to sample, especially when `n` is small. Using
it in place of `σ` adds uncertainty, and the **t-distribution** has heavier tails to account for
that, so extreme values are a bit more probable than the normal curve says. The right curve
depends on the **degrees of freedom**, `n − 1` for a single sample.

As `n` grows, `s` becomes a better estimate of `σ`, and the t-distribution gets closer to the
normal one. The slide gives no table for this; you look values up in a t-table or use software.

## The practical rule

- Known `σ` (a classroom exercise, or a long-established process): **z-test**.
- Unknown `σ` (nearly every real study): **t-test**.

The procedure is otherwise identical to the z-tests of the last concepts: hypotheses,
statistic, p-value, then conclusions in two layers.

Before using a t-test, check that its assumptions are reasonable. That is the next concept.

> Replace what you do not know with what you measured, and pay for the swap with a fatter
> curve.
