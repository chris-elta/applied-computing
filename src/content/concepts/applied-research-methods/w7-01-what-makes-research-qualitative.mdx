Suppose you want to know why a team stopped using its code-review tool. You could count
review comments per week before and after, which is quantitative. Or you could sit with the
team, talk to the people who quit using it, and read the chat threads where they complained.
You would come back with a messy, detailed account of *how* it happened. That second kind of
study is **qualitative**.

## What they share

The lecture covers several qualitative methodologies. All of them:

- focus on **phenomena that have occurred or are occurring**, studied as they happen
  rather than set up in a lab;
- try to capture and study the **complexity** of those phenomena. The researcher accepts
  that the issue has many sides.

That is why qualitative work is often **the starting point of an inquiry**. It helps you work
out what actually needs to be studied before anyone designs a measurement.

## How it differs from quantitative research

| | Quantitative (Weeks 3–5) | Qualitative |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Questions | Specific and fixed in advance, often a hypothesis | **General** at first, becoming **more specific** as the study proceeds |
| Method | Chosen before data collection and held fixed | May **not be identified ahead of time**, and may **evolve** during the study |
| Instrument | A test, scale or questionnaire | **The researcher** |
| Causation | Experiments can show cause and effect | **Cannot** identify cause-and-effect relationships |

"Evolving" does not mean unplanned. The lecture notes that qualitative studies need
**considerable preparation and planning**. The planning goes into how you will look and
listen, not into a fixed list of measurements.

## The researcher is the instrument

In a survey, the questionnaire does the measuring. Here *you* do: you decide what to notice,
what to ask next, and what it means. So the lecture lists three skills:

- a **firm grasp of previous research** on the problem, so you recognise what matters;
- **training** in observation, interviewing and other data-collection methods;
- the ability to **find meaning in huge amounts of data**, such as transcripts, field notes
  and documents.

Having a person as the instrument is both the method's strength and its risk. Later
concepts this week return to it in *Credibility and Trustworthiness* and *Three Biases*.

> Qualitative research trades control for depth. It can tell you how something happens and
> what it means to the people involved, but not, on its own, what caused it.
