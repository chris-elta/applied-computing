The t-test is useful, and the lecture does not let you use it blindly: **CAUTION! The t-test is
not always appropriate.** To use it, either the **population distribution must be normal**, or
the **sample size must be large enough**.

## How large is large enough?

| Sample size | What the sample must look like |
| --- | --- |
| `n` under 15 | Pretty normal: **no outliers** and very little skewness |
| `n` from 15 to 40 | **No large outliers** and no extreme skewness |
| `n` over 40 | Large enough to use t-procedures **regardless** of shape |

(The slide's ranges write 15 and 40 as boundaries without saying which side they belong to;
treat them as rules of thumb, not sharp edges.)

## Why the shape matters less as `n` grows

The test is about the *sample mean*. Even if individual values are skewed, the **means** of
large samples pile up in a roughly normal shape, a result called the Central Limit Theorem.
With a small sample there is no such help, so the data themselves must already look normal,
because one outlier can shift the mean and inflate `s`.

## What to do about it

1. **Plot the data** (histogram or boxplot) before you test.
2. Under 15, look for outliers and skew; if present, investigate each outlier rather than
   dropping it silently, because Week 4 asked for a rationale for any clean-up.
3. If the assumptions fail and the sample cannot be enlarged, a **nonparametric** method is
   the alternative (Week 4).

This is an application of the Week 4 rule of thumb about parametric versus nonparametric
statistics, with the sample-size escape hatch spelled out.

> Check the plot before you trust the p-value. The test cannot see the problem; you can.
