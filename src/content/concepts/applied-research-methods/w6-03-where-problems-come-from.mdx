"Find a research problem" is hard advice to act on. The lecture replaces it with six places
to look, each of which reliably produces candidates.

| Source | Where to look | The question it yields |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Limitations sections** | Recent papers say what they could not do. **Read the last two pages first.** | Can the stated limitation be removed? |
| **2. Assumptions that break** | A method assumes IID data, ample memory, a benign user, a fixed schema. | Change the setting: does it still hold? |
| **3. Contradictory findings** | Two papers disagree. | Which conditions explain the difference? |
| **4. Technique transfer** | A method from PL, databases or HCI has never been tried in your subfield. | Why not, and would it survive? |
| **5. Measurement gaps** | A belief everyone repeats but nobody has measured. | How big is the effect, really? |
| **6. Practitioner pain** | Something you or an internship team worked around by hand. | Why does no tool exist? |

## Why these work

Every source starts from something **already known** and finds its edge. That is why the
problems they produce pass the four tests from the previous concept almost automatically.
The outcome is uncertain because nobody has checked. A negative result still teaches
something, since knowing that an assumption *does* hold is useful too. And the people who
wrote the original paper have a reason to cite you.

Some notes on individual sources:

- **Limitations sections** are written by the people who know the work best, and they are
  close to an explicit invitation. Reading them first is a fast way to survey a field.
- **Assumptions that break** is the source behind the federated-learning example (the server
  was assumed to know label counts) and the parking app (reporters were assumed to be paid).
- **Measurement gaps** lead to *characterization studies*. These describe how things
  actually are, without proposing a new method. The lecture calls them **cheap and
  citable**, which makes them a good fit for a Master's timeline.
- **Technique transfer** needs care. The question is not only "has anyone tried it?" but
  "why would it help here?" If you cannot answer the second, you have the false gap covered
  in *Finding the Gap with a Comparison Table*.
- **Practitioner pain** links back to Week 1's point that problems are all around you, in
  the work you already do.

> Research problems are found at the edges of existing work: where it stops, where its
> assumptions fail, where it disagrees with itself, or where nobody has measured it.
