Sometimes a random sample is impossible or beside the point. **Nonprobability** designs
select participants by something other than chance. The lecture names three.

| Design | How it works | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Convenience** (accidental) | Take samples that are **readily available** | For **less demanding** research problems |
| **Quota** | A variation of convenience sampling: select participants in the **same proportion found in the general population**, but not randomly | Mirrors the mix, without chance |
| **Purposive** | Select participants **for a particular purpose** | **Always provide a rationale** explaining why this sample |

The slide's wording for quota sampling says 'but randomly', which reads as an error given the
heading; since the design is listed under nonprobability sampling, the selection within each
proportion is not random.

## What you give up

With a probability sample, chance makes the sample representative on average, and you can
calculate how far off it might be. With these designs, representativeness depends on
whoever was chosen, and you cannot calculate it. A convenience sample of classmates may
differ from all developers in ways you never thought to check.

That is why the lecture attaches a condition to each: convenience is for less demanding
problems; purposive sampling requires a stated rationale. In both cases the *reader* decides
whether to trust the sample, so you have to give the information needed.

This is the same lesson as Week 1's assumptions: if you used a sample of convenience, say so.

> Without chance doing the choosing, the researcher's justification has to do it instead.
