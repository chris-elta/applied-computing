A review of fifty sources is useless if you cannot cite them. Record the details as you find
each source, not weeks later.

## What to record

| Source type | Record |
| --- | --- |
| Any source | **Author, title, date of publication, page numbers, publication information** |
| Internet source | **URL**, **date retrieved**, and **DOI** where there is one |

The retrieval date matters because web content changes. The DOI is a permanent identifier that
should lead to the paper even if its address moves.

## Follow a style

Different disciplines prescribe different citation styles. The slide names **APA**,
**Chicago**, **CSE** and **MLA**, and points to a table of commonly used styles in the
textbook (Table 3.2). Which to use depends on your field and on whoever will read the work;
the point is to pick one and apply it **consistently**.

## Let a tool do it

The lecture's note is that **citation managers**, such as **Zotero**, are helpful. A manager
stores each source's details once and can then format the references in whichever style you
need, so a change of style is a menu option rather than a weekend.

Recording details while you read also makes a later step easier: when you organise the review
you need to know, for every source, what it claimed and how it connects to your problem.
Notes on that belong next to the citation.

> The best time to record a source's details is the moment you decide it is worth reading.
