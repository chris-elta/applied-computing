The critical value `z*` is where your choice of **confidence level** enters the formula.

The **confidence level** is how confident you want to be that the interval will contain the
parameter. A **95% confidence level** means that **95% of intervals built this way from
samples of size `n` will capture the population mean**. Picture the standard normal curve with
the middle 95% shaded: `z*` is the number of standard deviations from the middle to the edge
of the shading, and `−z*` is its mirror image on the other side.

## The values to know

| Confidence level | `z*` |
| --- | --- |
| 80% | 1.282 |
| 90% | 1.645 |
| 95% | **1.96** |
| 98% | 2.326 |
| 99% | 2.576 |

The deck shows how to read these from a table or with an inverse-normal function on a
calculator. Statistical software does it for you.

## The trade-off, in numbers

All else equal, the margin of error is proportional to `z*`. Moving from 95% to 99% multiplies
the margin by 2.576 / 1.96 ≈ **1.31**: you pay about a third more width for the extra
confidence.

| Level | Relative width |
| --- | --- |
| 90% | 1.645 / 1.96 ≈ 0.84 |
| 95% | 1.00 |
| 99% | ≈ 1.31 |

There is no free setting. Pick the confidence level for the cost of being wrong: a safety
claim may demand 99%, a casual comparison may be fine at 90%. 95% is the convention.

Note what does *not* change when you move the level: the point estimate at the centre. The
interval is symmetric about `x̄`, so you can always recover the sample mean as the midpoint
and the margin as the half-width.

> A higher confidence level does not make the estimate better. It only makes the interval
> wider.
