Reviewers of student proposals keep writing the same three comments:

| Comment | What the proposal actually contained |
| --- | --- |
| *"I can't tell what is new here."* | An area and a plan to build something, but **no claim that could turn out to be false** |
| *"Why would anyone care about this result?"* | Motivation that the topic is **popular**, not that a decision or design would change if the answer were known |
| *"This is a build plan, not a research plan."* | **Activities** (implement, test, deploy), but nothing that could tell success from failure |

All three have the same cause. The writer stopped at a *topic* and never reached a *problem*,
let alone a *question*.

## Three levels

The lecture separates them, and carries one example through all three:

| Level | What it is | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Topic** | An area. Nothing to be right or wrong about. | Federated learning on edge devices |
| **Problem** | A specific failure, limitation or unknown in current practice, **with a reason it exists** | Accuracy drops sharply when client data are non-IID. Existing corrections assume the server knows each client's label distribution, which it cannot observe. |
| **Question** | Something answerable with evidence, **naming the comparison and the setting** | Can the server estimate label skew from update statistics alone, and does correcting with that estimate recover accuracy relative to FedProx at the same communication budget? |

(*Non-IID* means the clients' data are not drawn from the same distribution. One phone sees
mostly cat photos, another mostly receipts.)

## What each step adds

Read the example from top to bottom and notice what is gained at each step.

- **Topic → problem** adds a *failure* (accuracy drops) and a *reason* (corrections need
  information the server does not have). The reason is what makes it a problem rather than
  a complaint. It points at where a fix might come from.
- **Problem → question** adds a *proposed answer to test* (estimate skew from updates), a
  *baseline* (FedProx), and a *fair condition* (same communication budget). Now a result
  could come out either way, and you would know which way it came out.

This sits directly on Week 1's idea of a research-worthy problem. Week 1 said a problem's
answer must make a difference. This lecture adds that it must be **stated finely enough to
be wrong**.

> A topic is where you look. A problem is what is broken there and why. A question is the
> test that could show you were wrong about the fix.
