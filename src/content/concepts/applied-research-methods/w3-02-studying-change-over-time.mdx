  - ask: A researcher wants to know how programming skill develops with experience. They test 20-year-olds, 30-year-olds and 40-year-olds this month and compare the groups. What is the main weakness of this design?
    choices:
      - text: It cannot be carried out in a single session

      - text: The age groups may differ in life experience, which threatens internal validity
        correct: true
        explains: The 40-year-olds did not simply become 20-year-olds with more years; they grew up with different tools and education. That confounds age with generation.

        explains: That describes a longitudinal design, not a cross-sectional one.
      - text: Participants may drop out over the years
        explains: That is the longitudinal design's problem. Cross-sectional studies collect everything at one time, so no one can drop out between waves.
  - ask: A cohort-sequential design begins with two or more age groups and follows each one over time. Which advantage does the lecture give it?
    choices:
      - text: It avoids needing a sample

      - text: It lets you compute correlations between measures taken at two different times, so predictions can be made across time
        correct: true
        explains: It combines the multiple groups of a cross-sectional study with the repeated measurements of a longitudinal one, patching weaknesses of each.
      - text: It is the cheapest of the three designs
        explains: It involves following groups over time, so it is not cheap.
      - text: It removes every threat to validity

Often the question is about change: how do skills, habits or attitudes differ with age or
experience? Three designs answer it, and each has a cost.

| Design | How it works | Strength | Weakness |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Cross-sectional** | Sample people from different age groups and compare them | Easier: all data are collected at one time | Age groups may differ in life experience, a threat to internal validity; correlations between characteristics at different ages cannot be computed |
| **Longitudinal** | Follow a single group over time, collecting data at several points | Sees real change within the same people | Takes months or years; participants may drop out; people can get used to the measurement instrument and respond differently |
| **Cohort-sequential** | Start with two or more groups and follow each over a period | Addresses weaknesses of both; allows correlations between measures at two different times, so predictions can be made across time | More complex to run |

## Reading the table

**Cross-sectional** is a snapshot: quick and cheap, but it compares *different people*, so
differences between the groups might come from how they were raised rather than from age.
This is a case of the correlation-versus-causation problem again.

**Longitudinal** watches *the same people*, so it avoids that. The price is time and
**attrition**: people who leave are rarely a random subset, and they get practised at the
instrument, so their answers change for reasons unrelated to age.

**Cohort-sequential** is the compromise: several groups, each followed. Because every group
is measured more than once, you can relate a measure at time one to a measure at time two.

> Choose by what you can afford to lose: time, or comparability.
