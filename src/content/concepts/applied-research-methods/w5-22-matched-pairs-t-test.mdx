      - text: No test is possible, because there are two measures

      - text: 'A matched-pairs t-test: compute each developer''s difference, then run a one-sample t-test on the differences'

**The problem.** A weight-loss centre advertises that participants lose **at least 5 pounds
in the first week**. The state's consumer agency doubts it. To test the claim at the **0.05**
level, **12 participants** are randomly selected and weighed at the start and after one week.

## Why pairs need their own treatment

Each person is weighed twice. A person who starts at 215 lb ends near 211; one who starts at
125 ends near 123. The two columns are strongly related, because they are the same
people, so they are not two independent samples. The solution is to look at **what each
person changed**.

| Member | Initial | After 1 week | Difference `d` (initial − after) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 195 | 195 | 0 |
| 2 | 153 | 151 | 2 |
| 3 | 174 | 170 | 4 |
| 4 | 125 | 123 | 2 |
| 5 | 149 | 144 | 5 |
| 6 | 152 | 149 | 3 |
| 7 | 135 | 131 | 4 |
| 8 | 143 | 147 | −4 |
| 9 | 139 | 138 | 1 |
| 10 | 198 | 192 | 6 |
| 11 | 215 | 211 | 4 |
| 12 | 153 | 152 | 1 |

The slide's own note: the last column is **the data we are after**. Twelve differences form
a single sample, and the test is now an ordinary one-sample t-test on them.

## The test

| Step | Working |
| --- | --- |
| **Parameter** | `μ_d` = true mean weight loss (initial − after) |
| **Hypotheses** | `H₀: μ_d = 5`, `Hₐ: μ_d < 5` (the centre's claim is 'at least 5'; the agency suspects less) |
| **Level** | `α = 0.05` |
| **Summary** | `n = 12`, `x̄_d = 2.333`, `s_d = 2.674` |
| **Statistic** | `t = (2.333 − 5) / (2.674/√12) = −2.667 / 0.772 ≈ −3.45` |
| **df and p** | 11 degrees of freedom; one-sided `p ≈ 0.0027` |
| **Compare** | `0.0027 < 0.05` |

## Conclusions

- **Statistical:** reject `H₀`.
- **Contextual:** there is **strong evidence** that the mean weight loss of people on the
  program for one week is **less than 5 pounds**.

## Two things to notice

- The null here is **not 0**. Many matched-pairs examples test 'no change', but the claim
  being tested is a specific amount, so `μ_d` is compared with 5.
- The mean loss of 2.3 lb is real: the centre's participants *did* lose weight. The test
  says that they did not lose *as much as advertised*. A claim can fail without the effect
  being absent.

This closes the course's arc: pose a question precisely (Week 1), find what is known (Week 2),
design a study (Week 3), collect and describe data (Week 4), and test what the sample says
about the population (Week 5), then interpret the answer honestly.

> Pairing turns two related columns into one sample of changes. Then you already know the
> test.
