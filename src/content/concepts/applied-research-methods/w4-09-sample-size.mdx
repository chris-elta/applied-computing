How big should the sample be? The lecture's first rule is *the larger the sample, the better*.
Then it gives rules of thumb for how large is enough.

| Population size | Suggested sample |
| --- | --- |
| 100 or fewer (`N` ≤ 100) | Survey the **entire population** |
| About 500 | **50%**, about 250 |
| About 1,500 | **20%**, about 300 |
| More than 5,000 | **400** is fine |

Read it down the table and a pattern appears: **the larger the population, the smaller the
percentage needed** for a representative sample. The total needed levels off: 250, then 300,
then 400.

The reason is that the accuracy of an estimate depends mainly on how many cases you actually
observe, not on what fraction of the population they are. This course's Week 5 shows the
mechanism: the margin of error in a confidence interval depends on the sample size `n` and
not on the population size.

## Very large populations

For a very large population, such as a country, the lecture says the sample **must be
representative of the population about which inferences are drawn**, and suggests **random
sampling in a multistage design** of areas: choose regions at random, then choose within
them. The textbook's Figure 6.13 illustrates it; the deck does not reproduce it.

## The condition behind the numbers

These numbers assume a *good* sample. A sample of 400 collected from a convenient subgroup is
still a sample of a convenient subgroup. Size reduces random error; it does nothing about
bias, which the next concept covers.

> More cases reduce chance error. Only better sampling reduces bias.
