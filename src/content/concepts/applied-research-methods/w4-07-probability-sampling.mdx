Descriptive studies find out *how things are*. You rarely examine every case, so you study a
**sample** (a population microcosm) and generalise to the **population**. The lecture's
qualifying words for a good sample are *selected, representative, typical, certain, random*.

Designs fall into two kinds. In **probability sampling**, every member has a known chance of
being picked, and chance does the picking. The lecture lists five.

| Design | How it works | When it helps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Simple random** | Choose by simple random selection | The least sophisticated; fine for a list you can reach |
| **Stratified random** | Sample **equally** from each layer (stratum) of the population | When you need every layer represented, even small ones |
| **Proportional stratified** | Sample from each layer **in proportion** to its size | When the sample should mirror the population's mix |
| **Cluster** | Divide a large area into smaller units; randomly select a subset of clusters | When the population is spread over a large area |
| **Systematic** | Select by a predetermined sequence, which must **originate by chance** | A quick way to spread selection across a list |

## Stratified or proportional?

The two are easy to confuse. Imagine 900 developers and 100 testers, and a sample of 50.

- **Stratified** (equal): 25 from each. Both groups are well represented, but testers are
  overrepresented relative to the company.
- **Proportional stratified**: 45 developers, 5 testers. The sample mirrors the company, but
  there are only five testers to analyse.

Which is better depends on whether you are describing the whole or comparing the layers.

For systematic sampling, note the condition in the lecture: the starting point **must come
from chance**. Taking 'every 20th name' from a list sorted by team, after choosing a
favourable start, is not probability sampling.

> Probability sampling lets you say how far a sample can be trusted. Without it you can say
> only what the sample said.
