"Thanks for the PR! Have you considered reading the contributing guide?" A content analysis
might code this as *polite*, because it contains a thank-you. Anyone who has been on the
receiving end knows it can also be a dismissal. **Discourse analysis** studies that second
layer.

## What it is

**Similar to content analysis**: it also examines the contents of **human communications**.
The differences:

- the focus is **language-based** communication, called **discourse**;
- it also examines the **other inherent messages**: **body language**, use of **dialect**,
  and the **situational, personal, social and cultural contexts** of the discourse;
- it takes a **constructivist perspective**. Meanings are presumed to be **constructed by
  the listeners, readers and researcher**, not fixed in the text.

## Side by side

| | Content analysis | Discourse analysis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Material | Any body of verbal or nonverbal communication | Language-based communication, with its context |
| Unit of interest | Defined categories | Meaning in context |
| Core move | Label segments and **count** | **Interpret** how language works in its setting |
| Where meaning lives | In the definition of each category | Built by listeners, readers and the researcher |
| Output | Tabulated frequencies | An interpretation of what the communication does |

## The constructivist point

The last row is the important one. In content analysis, a well-defined code means the same
thing to every coder, and that is the aim. In discourse analysis, the meaning of "have you
considered reading the guide?" depends on who says it to whom: a maintainer to a newcomer,
in a project known for gatekeeping, in a second language. The same words build a different
meaning in each case.

That includes the **researcher**. Your reading is one of the constructions, not a neutral
measurement. This is why the trustworthiness strategies later this week, reflexivity
especially, matter so much here.

Choose discourse analysis when the question is about **how** language does something: how
authority is signalled, how blame is shifted, how a community defines who belongs. Choose
content analysis when the question is **how often** something appears.

> Content analysis counts what is said; discourse analysis interprets what saying it does,
> for those particular people in that particular context.
