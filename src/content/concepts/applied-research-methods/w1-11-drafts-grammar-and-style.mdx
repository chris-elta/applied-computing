Nobody writes well in one pass. The lecture's advice is to *anticipate writing multiple
drafts*, for two audiences.

## Why draft

- **For yourself.** Writing ideas down is different from thinking or talking about them, and
  reading what you wrote clarifies your own thinking, iteratively.
- **For others.** What makes perfect sense to you does not to a reader. Once someone has seen
  a draft they can be biased by it, so it helps to have a few different people look.
- **To evaluate** the logic and ideas, the precision and clarity of expression, and any gaps
  or errors.

## Three lines of defence

1. Built-in **spelling and grammar checkers** in a word processor.
2. **BCIT writing resources** (the library's writing guidance and the Write Away service).
3. A **friend or family member** to proofread.

The first line is not enough alone. The lecture's example — *Watch you're words! Spell-check
may not sea words that are miss used because they are spelled rite!* — passes a spell checker
perfectly.

## Four problems to look for

| Problem | Example | Repair |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Run-on sentence | The project will use TensorFlow, it will be used as the main library. | Split in two, or merge: *The project will use TensorFlow as the main library.* |
| Sentence fragment | This project, which will enhance my expertise in my chosen specialization, which is HCI. | *This project will enhance my expertise in my chosen specialization of HCI.* |
| Missing articles | The game use ANN as algorithm for enemy behaviour. | *The game will use ANN as the algorithm for controlling the enemy's behaviour.* |
| Writing as you speak | I don't intend to do too much optimization. | *Optimizing the code will generally be outside the scope of the project.* |

Look at the last repair. It does more than remove a contraction: it also states a
delimitation, which is the sentence the proposal needed.

> Spell-check checks that words exist. Only a reader can check that they mean what you meant.
