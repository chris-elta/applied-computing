The lecture shows two vague starting points rewritten into answerable questions, and then a
hypothesis built from one of them.

## Two rewrites

| Before | After | What changed |
| --- | --- | --- |
| How can we make LLM code generation better? | Does retrieving **repository-local type signatures** reduce the **compile-error rate** of LLM-generated Rust functions, **compared with no retrieval**, **at an equal token budget**? | Names the **intervention**, the **outcome measure**, the **comparison** and the **constraint**. |
| Study the security of IoT devices. | What fraction of the **50 best-selling consumer IoT devices** accept firmware updates without signature verification, and **does the fraction differ by price tier**? | A topic becomes a **characterization question** with a **defined sample**. |

The first is a checklist you can apply to any experimental question:

- **Intervention** — what you change (retrieving type signatures);
- **Outcome measure** — what you count (compile-error rate);
- **Comparison** — against what (no retrieval);
- **Constraint** — what is held equal so the comparison is fair (token budget).

Leave out the constraint and a reviewer will say retrieval wins only because it was given more
tokens.

The second rewrite proposes no new method. It is a measurement-gap question, the "cheap and
citable" characterization study from *Where Research Problems Come From*. Its precision is in
the **sample**: the 50 best-selling devices, not "IoT devices".

## The hypothesis

The lecture asks for a hypothesis that is **falsifiable, directional, and has a stop rule**:

> "Retrieval reduces the compile-error rate by **at least 20%** relative to no retrieval. If
> the **month-2 pilot on 100 functions** shows **less than 5%**, we switch to the fallback: a
> characterization of which error types retrieval fixes."

| Property | In the example |
| --- | --- |
| **Falsifiable** | A measured reduction under 20% would show it false. |
| **Directional** | "Reduces", with a size. Not just "changes". This is the one-sided alternative from Week 5. |
| **Stop rule** | A pilot at month two, a threshold of 5%, and a named fallback. |

The stop rule is the fix for Heilmeier's Q8 (a check that can fail early), written into the
hypothesis itself. The fallback is still a contribution, so a failed hypothesis does not mean
a failed project. This is Week 1's point again: a hypothesis fixed in advance is what lets
the data disagree with you.

> A good question names what you change, what you measure, what you compare against and what
> you hold equal. A good hypothesis also says when you will give up and what you will do
> instead.
