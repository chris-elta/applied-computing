The lecture introduces significance testing with a problem. A bottling company claims to put
**12 fluid ounces** into every can, with a **standard deviation of σ = 0.18**. We suspect it is
short-changing us, so we take a sample of **24 cans** and find a **mean of 11.92 fluid
ounces**. Is that enough evidence to say the company is not being truthful?

The sample mean is below 12, but sample means vary by chance, so a gap alone proves nothing.
The test is a way of asking whether this gap is bigger than chance reasonably produces.

## Innocent until proven guilty

The lecture frames it as a trial, and the framing is the logic:

1. **Assume the company is innocent**: there really are 12 oz in each can.
2. **Take a sample** and measure it (here, 11.92).
3. **Determine the probability** of getting a sample like this *if* the assumption is true.
4. If that probability is very small, the assumption is hard to believe.

This is, as the slide says, essentially following the **scientific method**: state a claim,
gather data, and see whether the data can survive it. It is the indirect argument from Week 4:
you never prove the suspicion; you show the innocent explanation would rarely produce what you
observed.

## The steps

The lecture numbers them 1, 2 and 4 (3 is missing from the slide). In practice there are four
parts:

| Step | What you do |
| --- | --- |
| **1. Write hypotheses** | A null hypothesis (innocence) and an alternative (the charge) |
| **2. Calculate the test statistic** | How many standard deviations from the claim your sample is |
| **3. Calculate the p-value** | The probability of data like yours if the null is true |
| **4. Conclude** | A *statistical* conclusion (reject, or fail to reject), then a *contextual* one in the words of the problem |

The next concepts take these in turn. After that come three complete examples.

> A significance test is a trial of the null hypothesis, with your sample as the evidence.
