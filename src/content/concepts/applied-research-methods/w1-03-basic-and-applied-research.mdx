Before choosing a question it helps to know what the answer is for. The lecture splits
research by purpose.

| | Basic research | Applied research |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Aim** | Enhance basic knowledge about the physical, biological, psychological or social world | Address issues with immediate relevance to current practices, procedures or policies |
| **Also** | Shed light on historical, cultural or aesthetic phenomena | Make decisions about practical problems |
| **Success looks like** | We understand something we did not | A decision can now be made, or a practice improved |

**Action research** is the applied kind closest to home: the question comes from your own
immediate work environment. This course is called *Applied* Research, and in a technology
setting most of your projects will land here — does this approach make our pipeline faster,
does this interface slow our users down.

The two are not rivals. Applied work borrows from basic work (the theory that tells you what
to measure), and basic work gets its questions from the problems applied work exposes. What
the distinction gives you is an honest answer to the first question a proposal faces: *who
needs this to be true, and for what?*

> Basic research asks "how does this work?", applied research asks "what should we do?". A
> project can be rigorous at either, but it has to know which it is attempting.
