Research leans on a few kinds of reasoning, and each has a characteristic way of failing. The
lecture lists the tools first and then the traps.

## The tools

| Tool | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| **Critical thinking** | Verbal reasoning, argument analysis, probabilistic reasoning, decision making, hypothesis testing |
| **Deductive logic** | Starts from premises or assumptions and proceeds to conclusions |
| **Inductive reasoning** | Starts from observations and proceeds to broader generalisations |
| **Scientific method** | A systematic and rigorous, yet flexible, process |
| **Theory building** | Well-documented findings and defensible reasoning; may use **abduction** |
| **Collaboration** | Other minds catch what yours will not |

Deduction is *truth-preserving*: if the premises hold, the conclusion must. Induction is not.
However many observations you gather, the generalisation is a bet, which is why the sample
you observe matters so much (Week 4).

## Seven pitfalls

1. Confusing what *must* logically be true with what *seems* true — the deductive pitfall.
2. Generalising about a whole category from a restricted subset — the inductive pitfall.
3. Looking only for evidence that supports our hypotheses.
4. Confirming expectations even when the evidence contradicts them.
5. Mistaking dogma for fact.
6. Letting emotions override logic and objectivity.
7. Mistaking correlation for causation.

Pitfalls 3, 4 and 6 are not errors of logic. They are errors of *motivation*: the researcher
wants a particular answer and the reasoning bends toward it. Pitfall 7 returns as a design
problem in Week 3, where the choice of study decides whether a cause can be claimed at all.

> The check against motivated reasoning is procedural, not personal: decide what counts as
> evidence before you see the results, and invite other people to find the holes.
