A point estimate is a single number. A **confidence interval** wraps it in an honest
statement of precision. The lecture builds it in three steps, with an assumption flagged
up front: the **population standard deviation is, for now, assumed known**. (Later this week,
the t-test handles the realistic case where it is not.)

1. Find the **mean from your sample**.
2. Choose a **confidence level**, which sets the critical value `z*`.
3. Compute the **margin of error** from `z*`, the standard deviation and the sample size
   `n`.

## The formula

`x̄ ± z* · σ/√n`

| Part | Role |
| --- | --- |
| `x̄` | An estimate for `μ` from your sample |
| `z*` | Based on the level of confidence, how many standard deviations away you can tolerate |
| `σ/√n` | The standard deviation of the estimate |
| `z* · σ/√n` | The **margin of error** |

## Why σ/√n?

Individual values scatter with standard deviation `σ`. *Averages* scatter less, because highs
and lows within a sample cancel. Averaging `n` values shrinks the scatter by a factor of
`√n`, which is why the denominator is a square root, not `n`.

That square root governs the cost of precision. To halve the margin of error you need *four*
times the data, to cut it to a third you need nine times. This is the reason Week 4's sample
size table levels off: past a few hundred cases, each extra case buys little.

## What you control

| To get a narrower interval | Effect |
| --- | --- |
| Larger `n` | Narrower, with diminishing returns |
| Smaller `σ` (less noisy measurement) | Narrower |
| Lower confidence level (smaller `z*`) | Narrower, but you are less sure it covers `μ` |

The last row is a trade you cannot avoid: **confidence and precision pull in opposite
directions**. The next concept shows the values of `z*`.

> A confidence interval is an estimate plus a margin. The margin is the price of using a
> sample.
