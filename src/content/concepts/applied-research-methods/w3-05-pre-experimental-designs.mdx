The lecture groups experimental designs into **five families**:

1. Pre-experimental
2. True experimental
3. Quasi-experimental
4. Ex post facto
5. Factorial

They run roughly from the weakest claims to the strongest, apart from factorial, which is
about the number of independent variables.

## What 'pre-experimental' means

**Pre-experimental designs cannot show cause and effect**, for one of two reasons: the
independent variable **does not vary**, or the experimental and control groups are **not
equivalent or randomly selected**.

The slide draws each design as rows of symbols read left to right: `Tx` is the treatment and
`Obs` an observation.

| Design | Layout | Problem |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1. One-shot case study** | Group 1: `Tx` then `Obs` | Low internal validity. One measurement, nothing to compare with |
| **2. One-group pretest-posttest** | Group 1: `Obs`, `Tx`, `Obs` | Better: a before and an after. But anything else that changed in between looks like the treatment's effect |
| **3. Static group comparison** | Group 1: `Tx`, `Obs`. Group 2: `---`, `Obs` | There is a comparison, but the groups were not formed by random assignment, so they may have differed already |

Each design fixes a flaw in the one before. Design 2 adds a before-measurement. Design 3
adds a second group. What none has is both a comparison group *and* a way of making the
groups equal at the start, which is what the next family adds.

> A single measurement is a fact. Two measurements are a comparison. A comparison between
> groups that started unequal is still not a test.
