A p-value is a number. A **decision** needs a rule, and the rule is the significance level
`α` that Week 4 introduced.

## The decision rule

Compare the p-value with the **significance level**, `α`, chosen *before* the analysis:

| Result | Decision | Reading |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `p ≤ α` | **Reject `H₀`** | The data are too unlikely under the null, so there is enough evidence for `Hₐ` |
| `p > α` | **Fail to reject `H₀`** | The evidence is not strong enough |

Most of the scientific world uses **0.05** as the standard cutoff. P-values below 0.05 are
treated as small and those above as large.

## A convention, not a cliff

The lecture's caution: this *gets ridiculous* when p-values are 0.0501 or 0.049999. They are
virtually the same evidence. **P-values are in shades of gray, not black and white.** Report
the value itself, not just 'significant' or 'not'.

## The wording of a conclusion

The final slide of the test asks whether the data's evidence was enough to convince us that
the null is not plausible, or whether there was *not enough evidence to refute* it. That
second phrase explains the wording **'fail to reject'**.

- A rejection says: *the data are hard to reconcile with the null.*
- A failure to reject says: *the data do not rule the null out.* It does **not** say the null
  is true: a small sample or a noisy measure might simply have missed a real effect. That is
  the Type II error from Week 4.

## Two layers

State the conclusion twice, as the lecture requires:

| Layer | Example |
| --- | --- |
| **Statistical** | Reject the null hypothesis in favour of the alternative |
| **Contextual** | There is sufficient evidence that the company puts less than 12 oz of soda in its cans |

The statistical layer is for the analyst; the contextual layer is for everyone else.

> 'Not significant' means 'not proven', not 'proven absent'.
