Week 4 named sampling bias and response bias in quantitative research. Qualitative research
has the same two and one more. When the researcher is the instrument, the researcher is also a
source of bias.

## The three

| Bias | Source | What it does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Researcher bias** | The researcher's personal **experiences, perspectives and worldviews** | Influences **which data are collected**, **imposes inaccurate meanings**, and **may alienate participants** |
| **Sampling bias** | Who or what was chosen to study | The sample **may not accurately capture the larger group** the study should draw conclusions about |
| **Response bias** | The participants | Participants **may give inaccurate descriptions** |

## Researcher bias does three things

The lecture names three distinct effects, and they happen at different stages:

1. **Which data are collected.** You follow up on the remarks that interest you and let others
   pass. In an emergent design, that choice compounds, because early data shape later
   collection.
2. **Imposed meanings.** You read your own interpretation into what was said, or into
   behaviour you observed.
3. **Alienated participants.** Who you are, and how you react, changes how people talk to you.
   A former manager asking about why people left may hear only polite answers.

The defences are the trustworthiness strategies: **reflexivity** for your own position,
**seeking exceptions** for imposed meanings, **feedback from others** for readings only you
would make, and an **audit trail** so others can check.

## Sampling bias, qualitatively

Purposive samples are not meant to be statistically representative. But the study still
draws conclusions about **some** larger group, and the sample has to capture it. Interview
only the developers who volunteer, and you may hear only from those with strong opinions.
A snowball sample can stay inside one friendship network. The test is the one from *Purposive
Sampling*: does the sample cover the variability in the phenomenon?

## Response bias

Participants may describe things inaccurately. They may misremember, present themselves well,
or tell you what they think you want to hear. This is what *Running an Interview* means by
**you are not necessarily getting the facts**. Triangulation is the main check: compare what
people say with what the documents, logs or observations show.

> In qualitative work the bias can come from the participants, from who was chosen, or from
> the researcher. The researcher is the one most likely to go unnoticed.
