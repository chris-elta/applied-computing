A **true experimental design** has a great degree of control and internal validity,
compared with the pre-experimental family. The lecture names three.

| Design | Key feature |
| --- | --- |
| **4. Pretest-posttest control group** | **Random assignment** to treatment and control groups; both measured before and after |
| **5. Solomon four-group** | Adds groups so that the effect of the pretest can be examined; enhances **external validity** |
| **6. Within-subjects (repeated measures)** | Every participant gets every condition |

## Why they work

Back to last section's six control strategies. Design 4 combines three of them: a **control
group**, **random assignment**, and a **pretest** to check equivalence. Random assignment is
what pre-experimental designs lacked, and it addresses confounds you did not think of.

Design 6 uses another strategy: *expose participants to all experimental conditions*. Because
each person is compared with themselves, differences between people cancel out. Its cost is
that the order of conditions can matter, so a good version varies the order.

**Design 5** answers a different worry. In Design 4, the pretest itself might change how
people behave: asking about your confidence in a skill might cause you to practise it. The
Solomon design adds groups that skip the pretest, so you can see whether the pretest
mattered. That is why the slide ties it to **external validity**: results that survive
without being tied to the testing procedure carry further.

The slide's design numbers skip from 6 to 8 on the next slide; there is no Design 7 in the
deck.

> True experiments are defined by random assignment. It is the one control that works on
> confounds you cannot name.
