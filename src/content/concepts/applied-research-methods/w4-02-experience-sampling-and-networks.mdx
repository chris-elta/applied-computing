Two descriptive methods fill gaps the others leave.

## Experience sampling

Most surveys ask people to *remember*: how did you feel this week? Memory flattens and
distorts. **Experience-sampling methods** instead collect data **frequently and on an ongoing
basis, from people engaged in a given activity**. A phone buzzes at random moments and asks
three questions.

The lecture lists the advantages:

- increased **accuracy and validity**, because people report in the moment;
- **multiple assessments** of a single characteristic improve **test-retest reliability**;
- it can identify **short-term changes** within a longitudinal study.

The textbook's example is a study of students' engagement across their school day.

## Social network analysis

Here the unit of study is the *relationship*. **Social network analysis** focuses on
**interpersonal dynamics and power structures within a specified group of people**: who
talks to whom, who is central, who is isolated. In a software team, it could map who reviews
whose code. The textbook example is a study of adolescents' friendships and substance use.

The slide is short; the lecture gives the two examples above and no analytic technique.

| Method | Unit of study | Answers |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Experience sampling | A person, repeatedly | How does this experience change moment to moment? |
| Social network analysis | The links between people | How are people connected, and who has influence? |

> Ask people in the moment when memory is the problem; map the links when the relationships
> are the question.
