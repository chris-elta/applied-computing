**Measurement** is how a phenomenon is turned into data: you limit what you record about it,
interpret it, and compare it with a standard. Some measurements are **substantial** (they
have physical substance, like a length) and some **insubstantial** (concepts, ideas and
opinions). Computing deals with many of the second kind: satisfaction, difficulty, code
quality.

The kind of measurement decides which statistics are legitimate. The lecture gives **four
scales**, in order of increasing information: nominal, ordinal, interval and ratio.

## Nominal

Assign **names** to data. There are infinitely many ways to do so, and it is the simplest
scale: it just divides data into **discrete categories**. Operating system, language, team,
bug type.

Statistics: **mode, percentage, chi-square test**.

## Ordinal

Think in terms of the symbols `>` and `<`. Ordinal data can be **rank-ordered**: first,
second, third; low, medium, high. You know the order but not the distances.

Statistics: **median, percentile rank, Spearman's rank-order correlation**.

## The summary so far

| Scale | What you can say | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Nominal** | A is *different from* B | Python vs Java |
| **Ordinal** | A is *bigger or better* than B | Gold, silver, bronze |

The mistake to avoid is computing a statistic from a scale that cannot support it. Averaging
operating-system codes, or treating the distance between 'medium' and 'high' as equal to that
between 'low' and 'medium', gives you a number that means nothing. The next concept adds the
upper two scales, and then the rest of the table.

> Pick the statistic after you know the scale. The data decide what is allowed.
