Anyone can ask a question. A question worth a term's work has to clear a bar, and the lecture
gives a checklist for it.

## Criteria

A good research problem:

- addresses an **important question** — the answer will make a difference;
- **advances the frontiers of knowledge** — it leads to new ways of thinking;
- **suggests possible applications**;
- **paves the way for further research**.

## Things to avoid

The lecture warns against four kinds of project:

- one that is really a ruse for **self-enlightenment**;
- a problem whose **sole purpose is to compare two sets of data**;
- **calculating a correlation coefficient** between two sets of data just to show they are related;
- any problem whose answer is **yes or no**.

What these share is that the answer, whichever way it falls, changes nothing. "Yes, they
correlate" leaves the reader asking *so what should anyone do?* A better version asks which
factors matter, how much, and under what conditions — questions with more than two possible
answers.

## Where to find one

| Source | Why it helps |
| --- | --- |
| Look around you | Problems in your own work are applied research waiting to be stated |
| Read the literature | Papers end by naming what is still unknown |
| Attend professional conferences | You see what practitioners argue about |
| Seek the advice of experts | They know which questions are open |
| A topic that intrigues you | You will need the motivation |
| A topic others will find worthy | Otherwise nobody needs the answer |

> Test a candidate question by imagining both answers. If you would do the same thing either
> way, it is not yet a research problem.
