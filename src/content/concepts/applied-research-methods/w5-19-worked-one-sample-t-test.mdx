**The problem.** A manufacturer requires a bolt's mean shearing strength to be **in excess of
110 lb**. To check, a sample of **25 bolts** is tested. The **sample mean is 112.7 lb** and the
**sample standard deviation is 9.62 lb**. Perform a hypothesis test at **α = 0.05**.

This time σ is not given, only `s`, so this is a **t-test** (n = 25, in the 15 to 40 range, so
check for outliers and extreme skew).

## Step by step

| Step | Working |
| --- | --- |
| **Define the parameter** | `μ` = the true mean shearing strength of this type of bolt |
| **Hypotheses** | `H₀: μ = 110`, `Hₐ: μ > 110` |
| **Significance level** | `α = 0.05` |
| **Test statistic** | `t = (112.7 − 110) / (9.62/√25) = 2.7 / 1.924 ≈ 1.40` |
| **Degrees of freedom** | `n − 1 = 24` |
| **p-value** (upper tail) | `P(t > 1.40) = 0.087` |
| **Compare** | `0.087 > 0.05` |

The slide shows the p-value on a t-curve with the area beyond 1.4 shaded, and a row from the
t-table for 24 degrees of freedom. Reading the table: the one-tail value for `t = 1.318` is
0.10 and for `t = 1.711` it is 0.05. Our 1.40 sits between them, so the p-value lies between
0.05 and 0.10, which is consistent with 0.087.

## Conclusions

- **Statistical:** fail to reject `H₀`.
- **Contextual:** at the 0.05 level, there is **insufficient evidence** to conclude that the
  mean shearing strength of this brand of bolt exceeds 110 lb.

## Compare with the z-test

Had the standard deviation been *known* to be 9.62, the statistic would have been the same
number, 1.40, but the p-value would come from the normal curve: about 0.080. The t-curve's
heavier tails make 0.087 slightly less impressive, which is the price of estimating `s`.

> The sample mean was 2.7 lb above 110. That is not enough, with 25 noisy bolts, to say the
> true mean is.
