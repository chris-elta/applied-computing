The interview is a flexible instrument. The lecture describes standardised interviews, either
**structured** or **semistructured**, and compares three modes.

| Mode | Strengths | Costs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Face-to-face** | Builds **rapport**; the **highest response rates** in survey research | Time and expense may be prohibitive |
| **Video-conferencing** | Reaches distant people | **Limits the audience**: participants need easy access, comfort with the technology and willingness to schedule in advance |
| **Telephone** | **Less expensive and time-consuming**; accessible participants; response rate lower than face-to-face but higher than mailed questionnaires | Lower response than face-to-face |

## Guidelines for conducting an interview

The lecture gives a long list. Grouped:

**Before**
- Identify questions in advance, and **pilot-test** them.
- Consider how participants' **cultural backgrounds** may influence responses.
- Make sure interviewees are **representative** of the group.
- Find a suitable location and get **written permission**.

**During**
- Establish and maintain **rapport**.
- Focus on the **actual** rather than the abstract or hypothetical.
- **Do not put words in people's mouths.** Restrict each question to a **single idea**.
- **Keep your reactions to yourself.**
- Seek **clarifying information** when necessary.
- Save **controversial questions** for the latter part of the interview.

**Recording**
- Record responses **verbatim**.
- Think about how to **quantify** responses as you write the questions, and consider adding
  questions that elicit qualitative information.

One line deserves its own emphasis: **remember you are not necessarily getting the facts.**
An interview records what people *say*, which is shaped by memory, politeness and the
interviewer's own manner.

> An interview gives you a person's account, collected under your influence. Plan the
> questions so you influence it as little as possible.
