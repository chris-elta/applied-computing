Some questions are not about what happened but about what it **felt like**. What is it like
to be the only remote engineer on a co-located team? To be on call the night of a major
outage? A **phenomenological study** sets out to answer that kind of question.

## The goal

To understand **people's perceptions and perspectives** about a particular situation. The
main question takes one form:

> "What is it like to experience such-and-such?"

The interest is in experience as people lived it, in their own terms. It is not the
researcher's account of what happened, nor a count of how often it happened.

## The method

| Feature | Why |
| --- | --- |
| Depends **almost entirely on lengthy interviews** | You can only reach someone's experience by letting them describe it at length |
| A **small, carefully selected** sample | Every participant must have had the experience, and there is time to listen properly to each |
| Possibly a **three-interview series** | The slide names this. In brief, a set of three interviews per participant gives time to build rapport and come back to the experience in depth, instead of relying on one conversation |

Compare a Week 4 survey. A survey of 500 people could report that 62% of remote engineers
felt isolated. A phenomenological study of 8 people might show what that isolation is made
of: missing hallway decisions, a delay before anyone answers, or being the face on the
screen nobody looks at. The two studies answer different questions.

## Suspending what you think you know

The lecture asks the researcher to **suspend preconceived notions** that would unduly
influence what they "hear" participants say. If you were once the lone remote engineer
yourself, you will be tempted to hear your own story in theirs. You will notice the
remarks that match your experience and pass over the ones that do not.

Suspending them does not mean pretending you have no views. It means naming them before you
start and checking, interview by interview, whether you are recording what was said or what
you expected. This is the phenomenologist's version of the **reflexivity** covered in
*Credibility and Trustworthiness*.

Phenomenology studies a **shared** experience across several people. *Narrative Inquiry*,
two concepts on, makes the contrast with a study of **personal** experience.

> Phenomenology asks what an experience is like, through long interviews with a few people
> who have had it, and requires you to set aside what you expect them to say.
