Having an independent and a dependent variable does not yet let you claim that one affects
the other. The question is whether *something else* could be responsible.

## Internal validity

**Internal validity** is how much the study and the data it yields allow you to draw
**accurate conclusions about cause and effect** and other relationships. The lecture is blunt
about the stakes: *without internal validity in experimental designs, results are not
interpretable*.

The enemy is the **confounding variable**: a variable that accounts for differences between
two or more groups that are **not attributable to what is being studied**. If the group using
the new tool also had the more experienced instructor, you cannot say which produced the
higher scores.

## Six ways to deal with confounds

| Strategy | What it does |
| --- | --- |
| **Keep some things constant** | Remove a known source of variation, e.g. use the same task for everyone |
| **Include a control group** | A comparison group that does not get the treatment |
| **Randomly assign people** to groups | Spread all differences, known or not, by chance |
| **Assess equivalence** before treatment with one or more pretests | Check the groups started alike |
| **Expose participants to all conditions** | Every person acts as their own comparison |
| **Statistically control** for confounds | Adjust for a measured variable in the analysis |

The first and last only work for confounds you have thought of. **Random assignment** is the
most powerful strategy on the list because it works on the ones you have not. Every
experimental design on the next slides can be read as a choice about which of these six
strategies it uses.

> Internal validity is the question 'what else could explain this?' Good design answers it
> before the data arrives.
