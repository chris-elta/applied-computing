## Ex post facto

Sometimes the cause has already happened and you cannot undo it. A team adopted pair
programming two years ago. A server outage occurred last month. The **ex post facto**
(*after the fact*) design identifies events that have **already occurred or conditions that
are already present** and then collects data to look for a relationship with later
characteristics or behaviours.

The lecture places it between two neighbours:

| | Like correlational research | Like experimental research |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **What it shares** | Looks at existing circumstances | Has clearly identifiable independent and dependent variables |
| **What it lacks** | | **No direct manipulation** of the independent variable, because the presumed cause has already occurred |

That missing manipulation is the limit. Teams that adopted pair programming may differ in
many other ways from those that did not. The slide's one example is Design 14, the **simple
ex post facto design**.

## Factorial designs

Real outcomes usually have more than one influence. A **factorial design** examines the
effects of **two or more independent variables in a single study**. If interface style (two
types) and screen size (small or large) both might change task time, a factorial study uses
all four combinations. That does two jobs at once: it tests each variable, and it reveals
whether the effect of one *depends on* the other.

Two designs are named:

- **Design 15, randomised two-factor design:** both independent variables are manipulated, and
  participants are randomly assigned.
- **Design 16, combined experimental and ex post facto design:** one variable is manipulated,
  and one is a condition that already exists, such as participants' prior experience.

> Ex post facto trades manipulation for access to causes you could never assign. Factorial
> trades simplicity for being able to see how causes combine.
