The upper two scales have **equal units** between values, which is what allows you to add,
subtract and average.

## Interval

**Equal units of measurement**, but the **zero point is established arbitrarily**. Temperature
in degrees Celsius is the classic case: 10 to 20 is the same step as 20 to 30, but 0 °C does
not mean 'no temperature'. The lecture says **rating scales** such as surveys are *assumed*
to fall on interval scales.

Statistics: **means, standard deviations, Pearson product moment correlations**.

## Ratio

**Equal units** and an **absolute zero**. Because zero means none, you can express values as
**multiples and fractional parts**: 200 ms is twice 100 ms. The lecture notes that ratio
scales are relatively rare outside the physical sciences, though in computing, time, memory
and counts are ratio data.

## All four together

| Scale | A and B differ by… | Statement you may make | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Nominal** | name | A is different from B | Database engine |
| **Ordinal** | rank | A is bigger or better than B | Severity: low, medium, high |
| **Interval** | a number of units | A is so many units more than B | Temperature in °C |
| **Ratio** | a multiple | A is so many times as big as B | Response time |

Each scale includes everything the one above it can say. The statistic you choose has to fit
the *lowest* scale your data really have.

The interval assumption for rating scales deserves care. Is the gap between 'agree' and
'strongly agree' the same as between 'neutral' and 'agree'? Treating it as equal is common
and convenient, and it should be stated as an assumption.

> Ask 'what can I honestly say about the difference between two values?' The answer is your
> scale.
