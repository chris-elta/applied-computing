If the researcher is the instrument, a reader has an obvious worry: how do I know this is
not just your opinion? Quantitative work answers with validity and reliability (Week 3).
Qualitative work aims for **credibility** and **transferability**, and the lecture gives six
strategies for earning them.

## The double-edged instrument

The lecture calls the researcher-as-instrument the **strength** of qualitative research:

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Pro** | You can **discern underlying patterns** that no fixed instrument was built to catch |
| **Con** | You bring **biases** that no fixed instrument has |

The strategies below keep the first and limit the second.

## Six strategies

| Strategy | What you do | Guards against |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Reflexivity** | Regularly **look inward** to identify your own biases | Mistaking your expectations for findings |
| **Triangulation** | Collect **multiple forms of data** | A finding that rests on one source |
| **Considerable time on site** | **Immerse** yourself in the field | A first impression standing in for the real pattern |
| **Seeking exceptions** | **Intentionally** look for contradictory evidence | Seeing only what fits |
| **Others' feedback** | Ask other people about your data and analyses | Readings only you would make |
| **Audit trail** | Keep a record of data and decisions | Conclusions nobody can check |

## Two that are easy to skip

**Seeking exceptions** means more than accepting contradictory evidence if it turns up. It
means **intentionally** looking for it. If your theme is "engineers distrust the pipeline",
go looking for the engineer who trusts it, and find out why. Either the theme survives, or
you learn its limits. Both make the finding stronger.

**The audit trail** is what lets someone else check your work. It pulls together the memos
from *Emergent Design and Its Data*, the transcripts and the coding decisions. It is the
qualitative counterpart of Week 6's reproducibility: another researcher may not reach your
conclusion, but they can see how you reached it.

## Transferability

A qualitative study does not claim that its findings hold everywhere. It describes its
setting richly enough for a **reader** to judge whether the findings would carry over to
theirs. A study of a ten-person start-up can be useful to a large bank, but only if it says
clearly that it studied a ten-person start-up.

> A qualitative finding is trusted when it rests on several kinds of evidence, has survived
> a deliberate search for counter-examples, and has a trail someone else could follow.
