It is tempting to treat the literature review as a hurdle between you and the real work. The
lecture argues the opposite: it is the cheapest way to improve everything that follows. Its
reasons fall into three groups.

## What the review tells you about the problem

- whether others have **already addressed and solved** it;
- who else **works in the same area**;
- **new ideas, perspectives and approaches** you might not have thought of;
- **controversial issues and gaps** in understanding.

The last is where research questions come from. A gap is a place where the literature stops.

## What it tells you about how to do the study

- how others handled **methodology and design** in similar studies;
- **sources of data** you did not know existed;
- **assessment strategies** others have used successfully;
- **measurement tools** others developed;
- ways of **dealing with similar problem situations**.

Why invent a survey instrument when a validated one exists? Why collect data that is already
public? This group of reasons is the one students most often forget.

## What it tells you about your results

- how to **interpret findings** and tie them to the work of those who came before.

A result with no context is a number. A result placed next to earlier results is a claim.

There is also a plain confidence benefit: others have invested considerable time, effort and
resources in the topic, which is evidence that it is worth studying.

> The more you know about related investigations and perspectives, the more effectively you
> can tackle your own problem.
