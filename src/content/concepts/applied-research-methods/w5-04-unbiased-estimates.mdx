A statistic is used to **estimate a parameter**: the average salary, the mean latency, the
average frame rate, the average sales. The lecture asks what makes an estimate good. Its first
answer is that it should be **unbiased**: *on average, the statistic equals the parameter of
interest.*

## The target picture

Slide 9 draws a target. The **centre is the parameter**, the thing you want to know. Each
**blue point is a statistic, computed from a different sample**. In the picture they scatter
around the centre, and on average they land on it. That is *unbiased with small variance*.

Two separate qualities are in play:

| Quality | Question | Picture |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Bias** | Do the estimates, on average, land on the parameter? | Is the cluster *centred* on the bullseye? |
| **Variance** | How much do estimates differ from sample to sample? | How *tight* is the cluster? |

You want both: centred and tight. A cluster can be tight and off-centre (a precise but
systematically wrong instrument), or centred and wide (right on average, unreliable on any
one try). That echoes Week 3's *validity* and *reliability*; here the object is a statistic
rather than an instrument.

## Where this leads

The sample mean is unbiased for the population mean, so it is the natural point estimate. But
a single sample gives a single point, and you cannot tell whether yours is a lucky or an
unlucky one. The **confidence interval** adds an honest statement of how far off it may be,
using how much sample means scatter, which the next concept builds.

Do not confuse this *estimation* bias with the *sampling* bias of Week 4. They share a name
and a direction-of-error idea, but the second is about who got into the sample.

> Unbiased means 'right on average'. It does not mean 'right this time'.
