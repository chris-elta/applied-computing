Many statistical tools assume the data follow a particular shape. This concept covers the
shape, and the choice of tool that follows from it.

## The normal distribution

The **normal distribution**, also called the *bell curve* or *normal curve*, has three
distinguishing properties in the lecture:

1. It is **horizontally symmetrical**.
2. Its **highest point is at its midpoint**.
3. **Predictable percentages of the population lie within any given portion** of the curve.

The third is what makes it useful. If you know a variable is normal, then once you know its
centre and spread you know how rare any value is. Week 5's z-scores and p-values rest on it.

## Parametric or nonparametric?

| | Used when |
| --- | --- |
| **Parametric** statistics | Data reflect an **interval or ratio** scale, **and** fall in a **normal** distribution |
| **Nonparametric** statistics | Data are **ordinal**, **or** the distribution is **nonnormal** |

Notice *and* in the first row and *or* in the second. Parametric tools need both conditions.
The moment either fails, you move to a nonparametric tool.

The two families line up with the Week 3 scales. The mean, standard deviation and Pearson
correlation belong with interval data (parametric); the median, percentiles and Spearman's
rank correlation belong with ordinal data (nonparametric).

Why not always choose the safer nonparametric option? Because the lecture later says a way to
increase statistical power is to **use parametric rather than nonparametric statistics
whenever possible**: when their assumptions hold, they find real effects more readily.

> Check the scale, then check the shape. The statistic you may use depends on both.
