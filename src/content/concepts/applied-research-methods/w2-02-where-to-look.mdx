The literature is more than journal articles. The lecture lists the places to look and the
tools that search them.

## Kinds of source

| Source | Notes |
| --- | --- |
| **Books** | Overviews and depth |
| **Journals** | The main channel for research reports |
| **Newspapers and magazines** | Context and public debate |
| **Government publications and white papers** | Often the source for applied topics |
| **Conference presentations** | Frequently the most recent work in computing |
| **Patents** | What has been built and claimed |
| **References from previous papers** | A trail someone else has already walked |

## Finding them

Start from **keywords** drawn from your research problem or question and its subproblems.
Indexes use keywords; so do search engines.

Databases search thousands of journals and other sources, sometimes at the level of the
abstract and sometimes the whole document. The lecture singles out **Web of Science**,
which also records **which publications cite which**, and a **Citation Network** feature for
searching reference lists. That lets you work both ways: back through what a paper cited and
forward through what cited it.

Professional associations publish electronic copies of their journals. There are also
**open-access** journals on the Internet, some peer-reviewed and some not. A free paper is
not automatically a reliable one.

Chapter 3 of the textbook has a table of sources (Table 3.1); the slides refer to it but do not
reproduce it.

> Your keywords are your problem's vocabulary. A fuzzy problem produces fuzzy searches.
