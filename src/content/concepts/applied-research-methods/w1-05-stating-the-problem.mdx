A problem you cannot say clearly is a problem you cannot solve. The lecture gives a short list
of habits for **stating the problem**:

- state it **clearly and completely**;
- think through the **feasibility** of the project the problem implies;
- say **precisely** what you mean;
- absolute **honesty and integrity** are the rule;
- state it so it reflects an **open mind** about its solution;
- **edit** your work.

"Open mind" is easy to violate by accident. *"Show that our new cache speeds up the site"*
has a verdict built in. *"Determine how the new cache affects page-load time"* leaves room for
the answer to be none.

## Subproblems

Most problems are too big to attack whole, so you divide them. A **subproblem** is:

- a subpart of the main problem, and an integral part of it;
- a **completely researchable unit**;
- clearly tied to the **interpretation of data**;
- one of a **small number** that **add up to the totality** of the problem.

Then there are **pseudo-subproblems**: procedural issues, such as decisions that must be made
before you can resolve the problem and its subproblems. *Which language do we use? How many
machines do we have?* They are real work, but answering them yields no data, so they belong
in the plan (step 5 of the cycle) rather than the list of subproblems.

A useful test is to ask, of each candidate: *what data would answer this?* If the answer is
"none, it is a choice we make", it is a pseudo-subproblem.

> Subproblems should be answerable, few, and jointly sufficient. If you could answer all of
> them and still not have solved the problem, you split it wrongly.
