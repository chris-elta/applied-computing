A survey is designed, approved and then sent out unchanged. A qualitative study often works
differently. Its third interview can look nothing like its first, because the first two
taught you what to ask.

## Many forms of data

Qualitative studies draw on **multiple forms of data**: **observations, interviews, objects,
written documents, audiovisual materials, electronic entities**, and more. In computing that
covers commit histories, chat logs, screen recordings, design documents and the whiteboard
photo from a planning meeting. The lecture's point is that **potential sources are
unlimited**.

## Memos

Alongside the data, the researcher writes **memos**. The lecture names three kinds:

| Memo | Records | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Reflective** | Your own reactions and assumptions | "I was irritated by that answer. Did it change my follow-up?" |
| **Methodological** | Decisions about method, and why | "Switching to evening interviews; on-call staff can't do mornings." |
| **Analytical** | Emerging ideas about the data | "Third person to say 'the tool is fine, the process isn't'. A theme?" |

Memos are what later let you, and a reader, reconstruct why the study went the way it did.

## Emergent design

Qualitative studies often use an **emergent design**. **Data collected early influence the
methods used afterwards**, and the kinds of data that are collected next. Grounded theory's
constant comparative method is the clearest case.

The lecture spells out what follows:

- **Early data shape later data.** That is a strength, because you follow what matters. It
  is also a risk, because an early impression can steer everything after it.
- **Data collection takes a great deal of time.** You cannot run it in parallel from a fixed
  script.
- **Methods must be consistent with the ethical principles of research.** Changing the method
  mid-study does not suspend them.

## The ethics-board problem

The lecture names the disadvantage plainly. An emergent design makes it hard to present a
**well-thought-out plan to an institutional review board**. The board needs to know what
participants will be asked to do before it approves anything.

In practice you plan the **boundaries**, not every question: the general interview questions,
the kinds of data you might collect, how consent and recording will work, and what you will
not do. The specifics emerge inside those boundaries. Week 1's proposal applies here too: an
evolving method still needs a written plan.

> In an emergent design, early data shape later collection. Plan the boundaries in
> advance, keep memos, and record why each change was made.
