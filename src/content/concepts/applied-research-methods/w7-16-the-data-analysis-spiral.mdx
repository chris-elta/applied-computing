You finish data collection with thirty hours of recordings, two notebooks of field notes and
a folder of screenshots. There is no formula to apply. The lecture gives a process instead:
the **data analysis spiral**.

## The four turns

| Step | What you do | With interview data, for example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Organise** the data | Put it into a form you can work with | Transcribe, name files consistently, link each transcript to its participant notes |
| **Peruse** the data | Read it all, to get a sense of what it contains **as a whole** | Read every transcript once without coding anything; write analytical memos as ideas occur |
| **Identify categories or themes**, then **classify** | Find general categories or themes (and possible subthemes), then sort the data into them | "Workarounds", with the subthemes "scripts" and "asking a colleague"; label each passage |
| **Integrate and summarise** | Pull the classified data together into findings | "Engineers trust the pipeline for routine releases and route around it under deadline pressure" |

## Why a spiral and not a list

Each step sends you back over the data. Classifying shows you a theme you missed while
perusing, so you read again. Integrating shows that two themes are really one, so you
reclassify. You pass through the same material several times, with a tighter question each
time. That is the iterative, recursive process from *What Makes Research Qualitative*,
applied to analysis.

## The step people skip

**Perusing** is the one most often skipped, because it feels like wasted time when nothing
is being labelled. But categories invented from the first transcript become a lens for all
the others. You will find "workarounds" everywhere because you are looking for them, and
miss the theme that only appears in transcripts 12 to 30. Reading the whole set first is a
defence against researcher bias, covered next.

## Compared with the designs

The spiral is general. Several designs add their own structure on top of it:

- **case study**: organise, categorise, interpret single instances, identify patterns,
  synthesise;
- **grounded theory**: open, then axial, then selective coding;
- **content analysis**: categories defined in advance, then counted.

Each one is the spiral made more specific.

> Organise, read it all, classify, integrate, and expect to go round more than once. Read
> the whole data set before deciding what the categories are.
