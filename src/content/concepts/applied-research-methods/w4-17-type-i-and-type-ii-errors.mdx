A hypothesis test makes a decision about a population from a sample, so it can be wrong in
exactly two ways.

| | **Null is actually true** (nothing is going on) | **Null is actually false** (an effect exists) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **You reject the null** | **Type I error** (false alarm) | Correct |
| **You fail to reject** | Correct | **Type II error** (miss) |

The lecture's wording:

- **Type I error:** the erroneous conclusion that a result was *not* due to chance when in
  fact it was. You **incorrectly reject** the null hypothesis.
- **Type II error:** the erroneous conclusion that a result *was* due to chance when in fact it
  was not. You **incorrectly fail to reject** a null hypothesis that is actually false. Also
  known as a **beta error**.

## Why you cannot just avoid both

Make the cutoff stricter (a smaller α) and you cut Type I errors, but you reject less often,
so more real effects slip through as Type II errors. Make it looser and the reverse happens.
The significance level is a choice about **which mistake you would rather risk**. Week 5
says it directly: when you choose α, you are choosing your willingness to accept a certain
chance of a Type I error.

You never know which error you have made, because you never see the truth about the
population. What you can do is design the study to make both less likely. That is the next
concept.

> Type I: you saw something that was not there. Type II: you missed something that was.
