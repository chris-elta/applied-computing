A great deal of computing work is hard, skilled and worth doing, and still is not research.
The lecture puts two versions of the same idea side by side:

| Engineering task | Research problem |
| --- | --- |
| Build a mobile app that shows campus parking occupancy from crowd-sourced reports. | Crowd-sourced occupancy prediction is accurate in studies that **pay reporters**. Whether accuracy **survives when incentives are removed** is unknown. |

The app on the left could be built, and probably should be. But when it is finished, the
world knows nothing it did not know before. The right-hand version starts from what earlier
studies found, points at an assumption baked into them (payment), and asks whether the result
still holds without it.

## The four tests

1. **Is the outcome uncertain?** If you already know it will work, it is a task.
2. **Would a negative result still teach the field something?** If accuracy collapses
   without payment, that is a finding. If the app fails to ship, that is just a failure.
3. **Would someone outside your group want to cite the answer?** Others building
   crowd-sourced systems would cite the incentive result. Nobody cites your app.
4. **Is success established by evidence, or just by effort and delivery?** A task is done
   when it is delivered. A research problem is done when evidence answers it.

The tests depend on one another. If the outcome is certain (test 1), there is no negative
result to learn from (test 2), nothing worth citing (test 3), and only effort to show (test 4).
Start with test 1.

## The misconception

It is tempting to think a task becomes research by getting **bigger or more technical**:
more features, a newer framework, a machine-learning model inside. None of that adds
uncertainty. What turns the parking app into research is a **question whose answer you do not
know** and that someone else would want answered.

The build is not wasted. The app may be the *instrument* you need to collect data for the
incentive question. It just is not the contribution. This is the "build plan, not a research
plan" comment from the previous concept, seen from the writer's side.

> If you can be sure it will work before you start, you are describing a job, not a study.
