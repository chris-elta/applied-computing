Academic writing is not a different language from clear writing. It is clear writing with
stricter habits, and the lecture lists six.

| Habit | What it asks |
| --- | --- |
| **Be specific and precise** | Choose words for their exact meaning; say what you mean and mean what you say |
| **Have a clear objective and focus** | What is the main aim? How does every source and statement relate to it? |
| **Organise** | Headings and subheadings; the story has a *direction*, not aimless musings |
| **Use an overview** | Tell the reader what to expect before the section begins |
| **Use concrete examples** | Especially for abstract ideas |
| **Use figures and tables** | Annotated, captioned, referenced, easy to read |
| **Summarise each section** | Mirror the overview, highlight the main points and the flow |

## The overview

The slide's example opens a review by announcing three parts: the theories behind one kind of
questioning, how it becomes self-generated questioning, and the link to note taking. A reader
holding that map can tell, halfway through, whether they are lost.

## Abstract is not vague

The lecture's example shows an *abstract* idea made concrete. The abstract claim: a shallow
coding scheme misses the true quality of notes. The concrete case: Student A records an
outline; Student B records an outline, examples, definitions and mnemonics. Counting only main
ideas rates the two as equal. The reader does not have to take the claim on trust; they can
see it fail.

Writing a proposal, you are telling a story, or outlining one. It should still have a
direction: every source and sentence should answer *how does this relate to my problem?*

> A reader should always know where they are, where they are going, and why the last paragraph
> was there.
