Two ideas let you say where a value sits within its group.

## Percentile

Percentiles are the values that **divide a rank-ordered set of elements into 100 equal
parts**. They are rank-based, so they belong with the ordinal tools of Week 3.

## The standard score

A **standard score**, or **z-score**, says **how many standard deviations an element is from
the mean**:

`z = (X − μ) / σ`

where `z` is the z-score, `X` the value, `μ` the population mean and `σ` its standard
deviation.

| Student | Score | Class mean | σ | z |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | 85 | 70 | 10 | (85 − 70) / 10 = **1.5** |
| B | 60 | 45 | 5 | (60 − 45) / 5 = **3.0** |

A scored higher in raw points, but B is further above their own class. The raw numbers are in
different units (each exam's own scale); the z-scores are in *standard deviations*, which are
comparable.

## Why this matters for the rest of the week

Every test this week reduces your result to a z-score or something like it: *how far, in
standard deviations, is what I saw from what a claim predicts?* The answer is then turned
into a probability using the normal curve from Week 4, which is where the p-value comes from.

| z | Roughly |
| --- | --- |
| 0 | Exactly at the mean |
| ±1 | One standard deviation out: common |
| ±2 | Two out: uncommon (about 5% of a normal distribution lies beyond ±1.96) |
| ±3 | Three out: rare |

> A z-score converts any measurement into 'how unusual is this?', in units the whole field
> shares.
