questions:
  - ask: A weight-loss centre advertises that clients lose at least 5 pounds in the first week. A consumer agency suspects they lose less. Which are the right hypotheses for the mean weight loss μ?
    choices:
      - text: 'H₀: μ = 5 and Hₐ: μ > 5'
        explains: The agency suspects less loss, not more.
      - text: 'H₀: μ = 5 and Hₐ: μ < 5'
        correct: true
        explains: The null is the claim being tested (5 pounds); the alternative is the agency's suspicion, which is 'less than'.
      - text: 'H₀: μ ≠ 5 and Hₐ: μ = 5'
        explains: The null states the specific claimed value. 'Not equal' can only be an alternative.
      - text: 'H₀: μ < 5 and Hₐ: μ = 5'
        explains: That swaps them. The null has the equality and represents the claim; the alternative is the suspicion.
  - ask: A marketing team wants to know whether a new campaign changed weekly sales, in either direction, from the usual 3,400. Which alternative hypothesis fits?
    choices:
      - text: 'Hₐ: μ = 3400'
        explains: That is the null, not an alternative.
      - text: 'Hₐ: μ < 3400, because campaigns often fail'
        explains: A one-sided guess in the wrong direction would miss an increase.
      - text: 'Hₐ: μ ≠ 3400, because the question allows an increase or a decrease'
        correct: true
        explains: A change in either direction is the 'different from' alternative, which leads to a two-sided test.
      - text: 'Hₐ: μ > 3400, because the aim is to increase sales'
        explains: That answers 'did it increase?'. The question asked about a change in either direction.

Every test begins by writing two competing statements about the **population**, not about the
sample.

| | Null hypothesis | Alternative hypothesis |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Symbol** | `H₀` ('H-nought') | `Hₐ` ('H-sub-a') |
| **It is** | A belief about the world, or the population | Typically *your* or the study's suspicion |
| **Usually says** | No change; things are as they should be; **innocence** | What you believe to be true |
| **Role** | What you are trying to show is **false** | What you accept if the null is rejected |

## Three alternatives, only three

The lecture is firm that there are only three possible forms, relative to the null value
`μ₀`:

| Form | Reads | Use when you suspect |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `Hₐ: μ < μ₀` | The mean is **less** than the null value | A shortfall |
| `Hₐ: μ > μ₀` | The mean is **greater** | An excess |
| `Hₐ: μ ≠ μ₀` | The mean is **different** | A change in either direction |

The first two are **one-sided** (one-tailed) and the third **two-sided** (two-tailed), a
distinction that matters when the p-value is computed.

## Writing them for the three worked examples

| Problem | Suspicion | `H₀` | `Hₐ` |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Soda cans | Less than 12 oz | `μ = 12` | `μ < 12` |
| Energy-drink sodium | More than 40 mg | `μ = 40` | `μ > 40` |
| Marketing campaign | Sales changed | `μ = 3400` | `μ ≠ 3400` |

In each, the null is the specific claim; the alternative is the suspicion.

## Choose the alternative before the data

The direction comes from the **question you set out to ask**, not from which way the sample
turned out. Picking `μ < 12` *after* seeing a low sample mean is a way of halving your
p-value to look more convincing. This is Week 1's pitfall of favouring the evidence that
supports you, in test form.

> The null is the claim on trial. The alternative is the accusation, chosen before the
> evidence is seen.
