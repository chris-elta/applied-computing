Week 2's literature review explained how to compare earlier studies in words. A
**meta-analysis** does it with numbers: it **analyses and draws conclusions about other
researchers' statistical analyses**.

The researcher:

1. Conducts an **extensive search** for relevant studies.
2. **Identifies the appropriate studies** to include.
3. **Converts each study's results to a common statistical index.**
4. **Posits possible explanations** of discrepant findings.
5. **Describes the research methodology and findings** in detail.

## Why the conversion step matters

One study reports mean task time in seconds, another reports a satisfaction score out of 7,
a third reports a count of errors. They are about the same question, but their numbers are
not comparable. Step 3 turns each into the same kind of number so they can be pooled. The
textbook calls the usual choice an *effect size*; the slide does not name it.

## What you gain and what you must still do

Pooling many small studies has more power than any one of them (see the last concept), so a
meta-analysis can detect an effect none of them could.

But the quality of the answer depends on the quality of steps 1 and 2. A meta-analysis that
leaves out studies that went against the hypothesis is just a biased literature review with
more arithmetic. That is why steps 1 and 5 are about being **extensive** and **detailed**:
a reader has to be able to see exactly what went in.

Step 4 mirrors the review skill from Week 2: where findings disagree, say why that might be.

> A meta-analysis is a literature review that does the sums, and so must be as honest about its
> inclusion rules as any review.
