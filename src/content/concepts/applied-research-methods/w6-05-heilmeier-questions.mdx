George Heilmeier, a former director of the US research agency DARPA, judged proposals with a
short list of plain questions. The lecture picks four of them, each paired with **how
proposals typically fail it** and **the fix**.

| Question | How proposals fail it | Fix |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Q1** What are you trying to do? No jargon. | *"We propose a contrastive self-supervised framework for cross-modal alignment."* | Rewrite so a smart friend in another department can repeat it back. |
| **Q2** How is it done today, and what are the limits? | The proposal skips straight to the new idea. The reviewer suspects the writer does not know current practice. | Two honest paragraphs on the state of the art and **exactly where it stops working**. |
| **Q4** Who cares? What difference will it make? | *"This is an active and important area."* | Name a **decision, a design or a belief** that changes if the answer is yes. **Name who holds it.** |
| **Q8** What checks tell you whether it is working? | The only check is the final result. | A **checkpoint at month two** that could already tell you the approach is failing. |

## What each failure looks like from the reviewer's chair

- **Q1.** The jargon sentence sounds technical but says nothing a reader could check. If a
  smart outsider cannot repeat the goal back, the reviewer suspects the writer cannot either.
- **Q2.** Skipping current practice reads as not knowing it. The fix is precise: say where the
  state of the art **stops working**. That stopping point is your problem. This links Q2 to
  *Where Research Problems Come From*.
- **Q4.** "Active and important area" is popularity again, the motivation reviewers reject.
  The fix matches FINER's *Relevant*: a named decision and a named person.
- **Q8.** A plan whose only test is the final result finds out it has failed at the end, with
  no time left. A month-two checkpoint makes failure cheap and early. The stop rule in
  *From Problem to Question to Hypothesis* is this fix written into the hypothesis.

## FINER and Heilmeier together

The two lists overlap but do different jobs. **FINER** is a filter for choosing *which*
question to pursue. **Heilmeier** is a test of whether you can *explain and defend* the one
you chose. Run FINER while choosing, and Heilmeier while drafting the proposal.

> Say it plainly, know where today's methods stop, name who would act on the answer, and plan
> a check that can fail early.
