Once the problem is clear, you need something that tells you *which* data to gather and how to
read it. That is the job of the hypothesis or the research question.

## Two ways to guide a study

| | Hypothesis | Research question |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Typical home** | Experimental research | Qualitative research |
| **Form** | A claim you expect to hold | An open question you aim to answer |
| **Example** | Pull-request review shortens the release cycle | How do teams experience moving to pull-request review? |

The lecture gives them the same duties. Both:

- provide **guidance for the data to be collected**;
- suggest **how the data should be analysed and interpreted**;
- may **originate in the subproblems**;
- give the researcher **a position from which to start exploring**;
- act as **checkpoints** against which the findings are tested.

The last one matters most. A hypothesis fixed in advance is what lets the data disagree with
you. Choose it after seeing the results and it will always agree.

## Delimit the research

To **delimit** a study is to say what you are *not* going to do. The lecture's three
instructions are to state what is out of scope, to avoid data extraneous to the problem, and
to stick with what is relevant to it.

Every study could grow. Interested in web performance? Then also mobile, also networks, also
accessibility. A delimitation turns that temptation into a written decision: *this study
concerns web apps on desktop browsers; native apps are out.* The reader learns where the
conclusions stop applying, and you learn what to ignore.

> The hypothesis says what would count as an answer. The delimitation says where the answer
> applies. Write both down before collecting any data.
