A **proposal** is the plan for a project, written so that other people can judge it before you
spend months on it. The lecture's overview lists what goes in, and the order is worth noticing:

1. State the **subproblems**.
2. Write the **hypotheses or questions**.
3. Write the **delimitations**.
4. Write the **definitions of terms**.
5. Write the **assumptions**.
6. Describe the **importance** of the study.
7. **Type** the proposal.

Each of items 1–5 was a separate idea earlier this week. The proposal is where they sit
together, so that a reader can see how the problem divides, what you expect, where the study
ends, what the words mean and what you took for granted.

## Questions to ask of your draft

- Have you done a **thorough literature search** to justify the time and effort?
- Have you looked at the problem from **all angles** to minimise unwanted surprises?
- What **research procedures** will you follow?
- What **research tools** are available?
- Can **others read and understand** the proposal?

## Fine-tuning the problem

The lecture's advice is mostly about getting outside your own head:

- conduct a thorough literature review (Week 2 is entirely this);
- try to see the problem from all sides;
- think through the process, and use all available tools and resources;
- discuss it with others, and **hold the project up for others to examine and critique**;
- remember that the project will take a great deal of time.

The last line is not a warning for its own sake. A problem that looked easy in a paragraph
becomes a term of work, and a vague one becomes a term of wandering.

> A proposal is a test you run on your own idea while it is still cheap to change.
