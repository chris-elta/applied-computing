**Bias** is any influence, condition or set of conditions that, singly or together,
**distorts the data**. Unlike random error, it pushes in one direction. The lecture names
three kinds.

| Bias | What distorts the data | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Sampling bias** | Any factor yielding a **nonrepresentative sample** | Surveying only people who answer the phone in the day |
| **Assessment bias** | The degree to which an **assessment strategy** slants results one way | A scale that only offers positive options |
| **Response bias** | Participants make **inaccurate statements**, often inadvertently, in interviews or questionnaires | Saying what seems socially acceptable |

## Checking for sampling bias

The worry is always the same: *the people who replied are not like the people who did not*.
The lecture lists three checks:

1. **Scrutinise the questionnaire** for items that may be influenced by factors that
   distinguish respondents from nonrespondents.
2. **Compare responses that came back quickly with those that came back later.** Late
   responders may resemble the nonrespondents.
3. **Randomly select a small number of nonrespondents** and match their answers against the
   respondents'.

The second is clever and cheap: it uses data you already have. If early and late responses
look alike, the worry is smaller; if they differ, then the people who never replied likely
differ too.

The textbook adds a guideline set and a checklist on identifying bias in descriptive studies,
which the slides refer to but do not reproduce.

Notice that each bias maps to something from earlier: sampling bias to *how you chose*, assessment bias to *validity*, and response bias to the self-report problem.

> A big sample does not cure bias. Check who is missing before trusting what the respondents say.
