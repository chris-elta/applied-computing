Three common instruments turn judgements into data, each one more detailed than the last.

| Instrument | What it records | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Checklist** | Whether each item is **observed, present or true** | 'Has a README' ✓, 'Has tests' ✗ |
| **Rating scale** | **Quantity or quality** of something, on a continuum | 'This tool is easy to use': strongly disagree … strongly agree |
| **Rubric** | **Two or more rating scales**, each with concrete descriptions of the levels of proficiency | Correctness, readability and testing, each scored 1–4 with written descriptions |

## The cost of a rating scale

A rating scale is **quick**, which is why it is everywhere. The lecture is specific about its
weaknesses:

- it gives **no information about why** participants respond as they do;
- there is **disagreement about what the points along the scale mean**: your 'agree' may be
  my 'strongly agree'.

There is also a **mixed view about including a neutral option**: some designers think it
gives people an easy way out; others think forcing a side misrepresents people who really are
neutral.

The rubric's answer to the second weakness is in its definition: concrete descriptions of what
each level looks like make the points mean the same to everyone. That connects to
*validity* and *reliability*: describe the levels and the scale becomes more consistent.

Rating scales also feed straight into the measurement scales from Week 3: they are commonly
assumed to be interval.

> A rating scale tells you how much. It does not tell you why, and it does not guarantee that
> everyone's 'four' is the same.
