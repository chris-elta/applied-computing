A **questionnaire** is a written set of questions sent to people to complete on their own.

| Advantages | Drawbacks |
| --- | --- |
| Can go to **large groups** over large **geographical areas** | **Low return rate** |
| **Anonymity**, which may make respondents more truthful than in face-to-face or phone interviews | Respondents **may not be a representative sample** |
| | They may not answer thoroughly, or may **misinterpret questions**, and there is **no follow-up** |

Questionnaires often use **checklists** (a list of behaviours, characteristics or other
entities) and **rating scales** (used when something is to be evaluated on a continuum, such
as 'never' to 'always').

## Guidelines

The lecture's two slides of guidelines amount to four groups:

**Keep the respondent's job easy**
- Keep it **short**, keep the task **simple**, give **clear instructions**.
- Use **simple, clear, unambiguous language**; give a rationale for any item whose purpose is
  unclear.
- Make it **attractive and professional looking**.

**Do not steer the answers**
- Check for **unwarranted assumptions** implicit in a question.
- Word questions so they **give no clues about preferred or more desirable responses**.

**Plan the analysis**
- Determine **in advance how to code** responses.
- **Check for consistency.**

**Test it**
- **Pilot-test** to determine the questionnaire's validity.
- Scrutinise the almost-final version once more to ensure it addresses your needs.

The textbook has three further guideline sets (constructing a questionnaire, using technology
to administer it, and maximising response rate) that the slides refer to but do not
reproduce.

> Everything that makes a question easy to misread will be misread by someone. A pilot test
> finds them before the real study does.
