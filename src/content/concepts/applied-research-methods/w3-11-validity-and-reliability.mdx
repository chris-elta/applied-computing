A measurement has to be *about the right thing* and *steady*. The lecture gives each its own
name, and they are independent.

| Property | Question | Fails when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Validity** | Does the instrument measure **what it is intended to measure**? | You measure something easy that is not the thing you care about |
| **Reliability** | Does it yield the **same result** when the entity being measured has not changed? | Readings wobble for no reason |

## The four combinations

Picture a set of darts on a target. Reliability is how tightly they cluster. Validity is
whether the cluster sits on the bullseye.

| | Valid | Not valid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Reliable** | Tight cluster on the bullseye: the goal | Tight cluster off-centre: a consistent error, such as a scale that always reads 2 kg heavy |
| **Not reliable** | Scattered around the bullseye: right on average, noisy | Scattered and off-centre: useless |

A reliable instrument is not necessarily valid, because it can be consistently wrong. But an
unreliable instrument cannot be valid: if it gives different answers about an unchanged thing,
it cannot be measuring that thing.

## Where this matters

This is the problem hiding in every metric. *Lines of code committed* is reliable and may not
be valid for productivity. A *survey about satisfaction* may be valid but unreliable if the
wording invites random answers. Week 4 shows what to do about it: pilot-test questions, train
raters, and have two or three people rate behaviour independently.

The deck's overview also lists 'using statistics' under data and measurement, but the Week 3
slides do not cover it. It arrives in Weeks 4 and 5.

> Reliable means repeatable. Valid means right. You need to check them separately.
