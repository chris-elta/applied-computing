Statistics do two different jobs, and keeping them apart prevents most confusion in later
weeks.

| | Descriptive | Inferential |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Purpose** | Describe what the data look like | Make inferences about large populations from small samples |
| **Two main functions** | | Estimate a population parameter from a random sample; test statistically based hypotheses |
| **Claim reaches** | The data you have | Beyond the data you have |

## Statistic versus parameter

The vocabulary the lecture sets up here is used throughout Week 5.

| | Calculated from | Example | Known? |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Parameter** | The whole **population** | True mean response time of all requests | Usually not |
| **Statistic** | A **sample** | Mean of the 50 requests you timed | Yes, you computed it |

A statistic is an **estimate** of the matching parameter. It will almost never equal it
exactly, because a different sample would give a different value. How far it can be from the
parameter, and how to say so honestly, is the subject of confidence intervals and hypothesis
tests.

## Choosing the right statistic

The lecture says different statistics suit different data. It names three questions to ask:

- **Single-group or multi-group** data?
- **Continuous or discrete** variables?
- Which **scale of measurement** (nominal, ordinal, interval or ratio)?

This is the Week 3 measurement scales put to work. The next concept adds a fourth question:
the shape of the distribution.

> A statistic describes your sample. A parameter describes the world. Inference is the bridge
> between them.
