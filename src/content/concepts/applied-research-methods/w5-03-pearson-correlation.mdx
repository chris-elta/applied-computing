Week 3 said correlational research asks whether two variables go together. The
**correlation coefficient** puts a number on how tightly they do. The lecture usually means
the **Pearson product-moment correlation coefficient**, written `r`.

## Reading the number

| `r` | Reading |
| --- | --- |
| Between **−1 and 1** | Always: the value cannot leave this range |
| **Absolute value near 1** | A strong linear relationship; −1 and 1 are the strongest |
| **0** | The weakest linear relationship |
| **Positive** | As one variable gets bigger, the other tends to get bigger |
| **Negative** | As one variable gets bigger, the other tends to get smaller |

Two separate things are packed into the number: the **sign** gives the direction and the
**absolute value** gives the strength. `−0.9` is a *stronger* relationship than `+0.4`, not a
weaker one.

## The word 'linear'

The lecture says 'linear' every time, and it is not decoration. The coefficient asks whether
the points hug a *straight line*. A perfect U-shape, such as performance that rises and then
falls with workload, can give `r` close to 0 while the relationship is plain to the eye. So
**always plot the data first**.

Because Pearson's `r` works with means and standard deviations, it belongs with interval or
ratio data (Week 3). For ordinal data the matching tool is Spearman's rank correlation.

## What it does not say

It does not say that one variable *causes* the other: that is Week 3's correlation ≠
causation. And it says nothing about whether the relationship would hold in the whole
population: a coefficient from a small sample can be large by chance, which is a question for
significance testing, later this week.

> `r` measures how well a straight line summarises the pairs. Direction is the sign. Strength
> is the size.
