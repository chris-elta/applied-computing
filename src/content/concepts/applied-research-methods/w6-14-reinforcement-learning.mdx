**Reinforcement learning (RL)** has no data set of right answers at all. An **agent** learns
by **interacting with an environment**, receiving **rewards or penalties** for its actions.
It is the type to use when you need a system to **make a sequence of decisions** or
**maximise a reward** over time.

## The loop

The lecture draws it as a cycle:

| Element | Role |
| --- | --- |
| **Environment** | The world the agent acts in: a road, a game, a factory floor |
| **Observation** | What the agent can see of the environment's current state |
| **Action** | What the agent does |
| **Reward** | The environment's signal of how good the result was |
| **Policy** | The agent's strategy: which action to take given an observation |
| **ML algorithm** | Updates the policy using the rewards |

The agent observes, acts, receives a reward and a new observation, and the algorithm adjusts
the policy so that actions leading to more reward become more likely. Repeat many times.

The lecture's example is **autonomous driving**: a car learns to navigate by maximising
**safety and efficiency** rewards.

## How it differs from supervised learning

A supervised model is told the right answer for every example. An RL agent is only told **how
well it did**, and often only later. A driving agent may find out that a lane change was a
mistake several seconds afterwards. Working out which earlier action deserves the credit or
blame is what makes RL hard, and why it needs so many trials.

## Pros and cons

| Pros | Cons |
| --- | --- |
| Handles **sequential decision-making** problems | **Requires many interactions** (computationally expensive) |
| Learns **optimal strategies through trial and error** | Can be **unstable or fail to converge** |
| Useful for **dynamic environments** | **Reward design is challenging** |

## Reward design is the research design

The agent optimises **exactly** what the reward measures, and nothing else. Reward only speed
and it will drive dangerously. That is why the lecture's driving example names two rewards,
not one. Choosing the reward is like choosing the outcome measure in *From Problem to
Question to Hypothesis*: if it does not capture the goal, a perfect score means nothing.

The other two cons are **feasibility** questions in FINER's sense. "Many interactions" usually
means a simulator and a lot of compute, and possibly the environmental cost FINER's *Ethical*
row mentions. "May fail to converge" calls for Heilmeier's early checkpoint, so you find out
in month two rather than month six.

> An RL agent gets exactly what you reward. Most of the design work is choosing a reward
> that means what you meant.
