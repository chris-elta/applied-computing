**The problem.** A bottler claims **12 fluid ounces** per can with `σ = 0.18`. We suspect
short-changing. A sample of **24 cans** has a mean of **11.92 oz**. Is that evidence the
company is not telling the truth?

## Step 1: hypotheses

`H₀: μ = 12` (the company is truthful). `Hₐ: μ < 12` (we suspect it puts in *less*).

## Step 2: the sampling distribution under the null

If the null is true and the sample means are approximately normal (a normal population, or a
large enough sample by the Central Limit Theorem), sample means follow

`N(12, 0.18/√24) = N(12, 0.0367)`

centred on the claim, with the spread of a mean of 24 cans.

## Step 3: the test statistic

`z = (11.92 − 12) / 0.0367 = −2.18`

The slide reports −2.1798, using a rounded standard deviation of 0.0367. The sample mean is
about 2.18 standard deviations *below* the claim. The negative sign is just direction.

## Step 4: the p-value

Because `Hₐ` is `μ < 12`, the p-value is the area in the **lower tail** only:

`p = P(Z ≤ −2.18) ≈ 0.0146` (about 1.5%)

## Step 5: conclusions

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Interpretation** | There is only about a 1.46% chance of getting a sample like this if the company were telling the truth |
| **Compare** | `0.0146 < 0.05` |
| **Statistical conclusion** | Reject `H₀` in favour of `Hₐ` |
| **Contextual conclusion** | There is sufficient evidence to suggest that the company is putting less than 12 oz of soda into its cans |

## Reading the result carefully

The *size* of the shortfall is 0.08 oz, under 1%. It is statistically detectable because the
standard deviation of the mean is small. Whether a 0.08 oz shortfall matters to a customer is
a question of **practical significance**, which Week 4's interpretation concept raised and the
test cannot answer.

The lecture's 'Central Limit Theorem' is a result about sample means; it is named here but not
developed in the slides.

> Hypotheses, sampling distribution, test statistic, p-value, then two conclusions. Always the
> same five steps.
