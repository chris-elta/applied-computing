Suppose you want to know whether error messages in popular command-line tools blame the user.
You could collect 500 messages, decide what counts as "blaming", label each message and count
the results. That is **content analysis**, the most systematic design in the lecture and the
one closest to quantitative work.

## What it is

A **detailed, systematic examination** of the contents of a **body of material**. The
material is typically some form of **verbal or nonverbal communication**: documents,
commit messages, forum threads, app-store reviews, images, video. The **purpose** is to
identify general **patterns, themes or biases**.

It begins with a **main research question**. The researcher then:

1. identifies the **sample materials**;
2. **defines the specific characteristics** or qualities to examine;
3. **scrutinises each piece** and **imposes category labels**;
4. **tabulates the frequencies** of each category. This is the **quantitative element**.

## The method, step by step

| Step | In the error-message study |
| --- | --- |
| **Identify** the material to be studied | Every error string in the 20 most-installed CLI tools |
| **Define** the characteristics to be studied | "Blames user" means the message names the user's action as the fault ("you entered an invalid…"); "neutral" states the condition |
| **Break down** complex items into smaller segments | Split multi-sentence messages so each sentence is coded separately |
| **Scrutinise** the material for those characteristics | Code every segment, with two coders working independently |

Precise definitions are what make the counts mean anything. If "blames user" is left vague,
two coders will apply it differently, and the frequencies measure the coders rather than the
messages. This is Week 3's **reliability** problem.

## Analysis and report

Analysis is a **tabulation of the frequency** of each characteristic, with **tabulations and
statistical analyses** used to interpret them. You could, for example, compare the share of
blaming messages in older and newer tools.

The report gives a description of the **material studied**, **precise definitions** of the
characteristics, the **coding or rating procedures**, and the **tabulations** for each
characteristic. Another researcher should be able to apply your codes to the same material and
reach the same counts.

*Discourse Analysis*, next, studies the same kind of material for something different: what
the language means in its context, rather than how often a category appears.

> Content analysis turns a body of communication into counts: define the categories
> precisely, code every segment, then tabulate.
