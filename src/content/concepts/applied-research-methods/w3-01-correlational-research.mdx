A very common first study design asks: *does this go with that?* Do teams that review more
code ship fewer bugs? Do users who see more tutorials stay longer?

That is **correlational research**. The lecture's definition: it **examines the extent to
which differences in one variable or characteristic are related to differences in one or more
others**. The researcher gathers data about **two or more characteristics for a particular
group** and looks for any interrelationships.

The usual picture is a **scatter plot**. Each dot is a case — a team, a user — placed by its
value on each variable. If the dots drift upward to the right, as one variable increases so
does the other; if they drift downward, one goes up as the other goes down. That is
Week 4's description of correlation: *when one variable increases, another increases or
decreases predictably*. Week 5 gives the number that measures how tightly dots follow a line.

## The warning

The lecture writes it twice, in capitals: **correlation ≠ causation**. Nothing was
manipulated and nobody was randomly assigned, so any unmeasured third factor might explain
the pattern. This is the Week 1 pitfall, now attached to a study design: *the design decides
what you are entitled to conclude*.

Correlational research is not second-rate. Often it is the only ethical or practical option,
and it is a good way to find what is worth testing more tightly. It just answers a
different question: *what goes with what*, not *what makes what happen*.

> Correlation tells you where to look for a cause. It does not tell you that you found one.
