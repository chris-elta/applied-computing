Two small acts of honesty make a proposal checkable: saying what your words mean and saying
what you are taking for granted.

## Define terms

Every term in the problem and its subproblems needs a definition. The lecture's rule is
that you define each term **as it will be used in the project** — you may make a term mean
whatever you wish *within the context of the problem and its subproblems*. That freedom is
the point. "Responsive" might mean a page that renders under 200 ms or one that fits any
screen. Both are legitimate. What is not legitimate is leaving the reader to guess, because
the two readings would lead to different measurements.

A defined term is also one you can *measure*. If you cannot say how you would tell whether the
thing is present, the definition is not finished. Measurement itself returns in Week 3.

## State assumptions

**Assumptions** are basic to the research problem. The lecture asks that every assumption
with material bearing on the problem be **openly and unreservedly** set forth, for two
reasons:

- other people **need** them to evaluate your conclusions — a result that holds only when all
  phones share a network speed is a different result from one that holds in general;
- writing them down reveals **what you may be taking for granted** to yourself.

The second reason is the more practical one. Assumptions are invisible exactly because they
feel like facts. Listing them is how you find the ones that are not. Assumptions also sit at
step 4 of the research cycle, which is why they appear before the plan.

> A defined term says what you measured. A stated assumption says when to stop trusting the
> result. A reader needs both.
