Two criteria apply to any research project, and both are about people other than you.

## Universality

A project should be one that might be carried out by **any competent person**. The lecture's
picture of the researcher is deliberately modest: a **catalyst** who collects, organises and
reports *what the collected data seem to indicate*. The researcher is not supposed to be part
of the result.

If the findings depend on something only you can supply — a hunch about which entries look
suspicious, a tool nobody else has — the project fails this test. The fix is rarely to remove
the human. It is to write the procedure down so that someone else's judgement would match
yours.

## Replication

Research should be **repeatable**. Any other competent person should be able to take the
problem, collect data **under the same circumstances and within the same parameters**, and
achieve **comparable results**.

Notice the word *comparable*. Samples differ, so repeats will not match to the last digit.
What replication demands is that the circumstances are described well enough for a repeat to
be possible, and that the result is the kind of thing that a repeat could confirm or fail to
confirm.

| | Asks | Fails when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Universality | Could any competent person carry this out? | It relies on unwritten judgement or private tools |
| Replication | Could they get comparable results? | Circumstances and parameters are not reported |

This is the practical reason for the earlier ideas this week. Defined terms, stated
assumptions and delimitations are all things a replicator needs.

> If a result cannot be checked by someone else, it is an anecdote with a methods section.
