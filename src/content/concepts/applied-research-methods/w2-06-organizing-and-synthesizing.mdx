Collecting papers is half the job. The other half is deciding what they add up to.

## Never take conclusions at face value

The lecture's first rule is to **determine for yourself whether the conclusions are justified
by the data presented**. An author's confidence is not evidence. Look at the data behind the
claim, with the checks from last week's article-reading concept in mind.

## Organise by idea

The ideas you meet should be organised and **synthesised into a cohesive review**. The
lecture lists ways to do that:

| Move | What it looks like |
| --- | --- |
| **Compare and contrast** theoretical positions | Two schools that explain the same behaviour differently |
| **Show change over time** | How approaches shifted, and why |
| **Describe general trends** in findings | What most studies agree on |
| **Identify and explain discrepancies**, and suggest explanations | Why two studies disagree |
| **Identify general themes** that run through the literature | The questions everyone keeps returning to |

Notice that the unit is the *idea* rather than the *paper*. A single paper may appear under
three themes, and a theme may draw on ten papers.

## The honesty rule

Do **not intentionally bias** the review by omitting or misrepresenting prior research
findings. A review that leaves out the studies against your hypothesis is the same pitfall as
ignoring contradictory evidence in your own data (Week 1).

> A review answers 'what do we know, what do we not, and why do the sources disagree?'. A list
> of summaries does not.
