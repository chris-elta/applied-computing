Because a test can miss a real effect, the lecture lists ways to **increase statistical
power**: the chance of detecting an effect that exists.

## Raising power

- Use **as large a sample as is reasonably possible**.
- **Maximise the validity and reliability** of the measures. Noise hides signals.
- Use **parametric rather than nonparametric** statistics whenever possible.

Each acts on the same thing: how clearly the signal stands out from the noise. More cases
reduce the noise from chance, better instruments reduce noise from measurement, and a
suitable parametric test uses more of the information in the data.

## The warning: more tests, more false alarms

The lecture closes the slide with a note: **whenever we test more than one statistical
hypothesis, we increase the probability of making at least one Type I error.**

An example makes the size of it clear. At α = 0.05 each test has a 5% chance of a false alarm
when the null is true. Run 20 independent tests where nothing real exists, and the chance of at
least one false alarm is

`1 − 0.95²⁰ ≈ 0.64`

That is about two chances in three of finding something 'significant' in pure noise. Look at
enough subgroups, outcomes or time windows and something will pass the cutoff.

This links back to Week 1's pitfalls, in particular looking only for evidence that supports
your hypothesis. The honest practice is to state the hypotheses and the number of tests in
advance, and to report all of them.

> Power helps you find what is there. Restraint with the number of tests protects you from
> finding what is not.
