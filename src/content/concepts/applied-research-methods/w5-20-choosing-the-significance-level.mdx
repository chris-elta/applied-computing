The bolt problem has a coda that unsettles people. **What if the significance level had been
0.10 instead of 0.05?**

The p-value is the same, 0.087. But `0.087 < 0.10`, so **we reject `H₀`**, and the conclusion
becomes: *at the 0.10 level of significance there is sufficient evidence that the mean
shearing strength exceeds 110 lb.* (The slide's text says '120', which is a slip for 110.)

Same data, same calculation, opposite verdict.

## Why that is not a contradiction

The lecture acknowledges that many people are bothered by this, and answers it directly: this
is **natural for a process in which you control the probability of being wrong**. When you
select the significance level, **you choose your willingness to accept a certain chance of a
Type I error.**

| | `α = 0.05` | `α = 0.10` |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **You accept** | A 5% chance of a false alarm | A 10% chance |
| **Threshold for 'enough evidence'** | Stricter | Looser |
| **With p = 0.087** | Fail to reject | Reject |

The two conclusions are different *decisions* made with different tolerance for error, from
one body of evidence. Neither is wrong: the evidence is a little suggestive, and whether
that is enough depends on how costly each kind of mistake would be.

## The rule that keeps it honest

This only works if `α` is **fixed before the analysis**. The decision rule is a promise about
the error rate. If you pick `α` after seeing `p`, you could always choose the value that gives
the verdict you wanted, and the promise means nothing. That is why Week 4 described `α` as a
*pre-set* cutoff.

A practical habit: write the hypotheses, the significance level and the sidedness in your
proposal (Week 1), then run the test. And report the p-value itself, so readers can apply
their own `α`.

> α is a decision about risk, made in advance. After the fact, report p and let readers
> judge.
