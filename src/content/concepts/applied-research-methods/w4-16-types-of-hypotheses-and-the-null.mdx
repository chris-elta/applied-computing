Week 5 is largely about *carrying out* a hypothesis test. This concept sets out the logic.

## Types of hypothesis

| Type | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| **A priori research hypothesis** | Posited by the researcher **before data collection** began |
| **Exploratory hypothesis** | Early analysis indicated that it might be worth testing |
| **Null hypothesis** | Any result observed is the result of **chance alone** |

The a priori versus exploratory split matters because of Week 1's pitfall of looking only for
confirming evidence. A hypothesis chosen *after* the data suggested it has, in effect, already
been favoured by the data, so it is a lead to follow up rather than a finding.

## Testing the null

The researcher starts from the **null hypothesis** and asks whether the sample is surprising
enough to abandon it.

- Use **data from a sample** to make inferences about the population it came from.
- Two readings of the result are on the table: it is **due to chance**, or it **accurately
  reflects the population**.
- The researcher **rejects 'chance alone'** if the result **surpasses a pre-set cutoff**.
  That cutoff is the **significance level**, or **alpha level**, written α.

It is an indirect argument. You never directly prove your hypothesis. You show that the
alternative, *chance alone*, would rarely produce what you saw.

Because α is chosen **before** the analysis, the researcher cannot fit it to the result. Week
5 works through exactly this, in the soda-can and bolt examples, and returns to what α means
in terms of errors.

> The null hypothesis is the sceptic's position. A test asks whether the data can make the
> sceptic give way.
