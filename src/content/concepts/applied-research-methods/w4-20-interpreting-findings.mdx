## Two basic principles

1. The **purpose of research is to seek the answer to a problem or question** in light of data
   that relate to it.
2. **Extracting meaning from the data — interpretation — is paramount** and must never be
   neglected.

From these: **all activity is subordinate to the research problem or question.** Every
choice of sample, instrument and statistic is justified by what it contributes to answering
the question.

## Four jobs of interpretation

| Job | What it looks like |
| --- | --- |
| **Relate findings to the original problem**, questions and hypotheses | Does the result answer what you asked? |
| **Relate findings to the existing literature**, concepts, theories and studies | Does it agree with, extend or contradict earlier work? (Week 2) |
| **Determine whether the findings have practical significance** as well as statistical significance | Is it big enough to matter? |
| **Identify the limitations** of the study | Where would the conclusion stop holding? (Week 1: assumptions, delimitations) |

## Statistical versus practical

With a large enough sample, a trivially small difference can reach significance, because the
test is detecting that the difference is *not zero*, not that it is *important*. Conversely, a
large effect in a small sample may fail to reach significance. A good report states both: the
test result and what the size of the effect means for the people the problem concerns.

That returns the course to the cycle from the first concept: step 7 is interpretation, and the
answer to 'so what?' usually raises the next question.

> A p-value says whether chance is a believable explanation. It does not say whether anyone
> should care.
