Pearson correlation asks how well the points fit a line. That is the wrong question when the
relationship is consistent but curved, and it is easily thrown by a single extreme value.
Spearman rank correlation asks a different question: **when one variable goes up, does the
other?** The lecture's phrase is that it counts **disordered pairs, not how well the data fits
a line**.

## Compute it from ranks

1. Replace each `x` by its rank in sorted order, 1 to `n`. Do the same for `y`.
2. For each point take the rank difference `dᵢ = rank(xᵢ) − rank(yᵢ)`.
3. Plug into the slide's formula:

```
ρ = 1 − 6 Σ dᵢ² / ( n (n² − 1) )
```

Or, equivalently as the slide states, compute the **Pearson correlation of the ranks**.

## A worked example

Five students' hours studied against marks:

| Hours | 2 | 3 | 5 | 8 | 40 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Mark | 50 | 55 | 61 | 70 | 72 |
| Hours rank | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
| Mark rank | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 |

Every `dᵢ` is 0, so `ρ = 1`. The 40-hour student is an outlier that Pearson would find
awkward (it would give a value below 1 because the point lies far off the trend). Spearman
sees only that more hours went with a higher mark every time.

The formula's shape explains itself: if two rankings differ a lot, `Σ dᵢ²` grows and `ρ`
drops. A fully reversed ranking pushes `ρ` to −1.

## When to choose it

- **Non-linear but monotonic** relationships: Spearman rewards steady rise or fall of any
  shape.
- **Outliers**: ranks cap how far any value can pull. The biggest value is just "rank `n`"
  however big it is.
- **Ordinal data**: things you can order but not measure, such as satisfaction ratings.

The formula shown assumes no tied values. With ties, use the Pearson-of-ranks definition,
giving tied values the average of the ranks they occupy.

> Pearson asks "how straight?", Spearman asks "how ordered?". Pick the one that matches the
> question you are asking.
