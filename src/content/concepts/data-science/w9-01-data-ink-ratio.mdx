Slide 2 puts a classical painting beside a Jackson Pollock and asks which is better. The
point is that you cannot answer without an *aesthetic*: a set of principles and a vocabulary
for arguing about them. Charts are the same. "I don't like it" is not a critique; "it has a
lie factor of 3" is.

## Tufte's four principles

The lecture takes its vocabulary from Edward Tufte's *The Visual Display of Quantitative
Information*:

| principle | what it guards against |
| --- | --- |
| maximise the data-ink ratio | ink spent on decoration rather than numbers |
| minimise the lie factor | graphics that exaggerate or shrink the effect |
| minimise chartjunk | elements that distract from the message |
| use proper scales and clear labels | a reader who cannot decode what they see |

The next two concepts take the lie factor and chartjunk in turn. This one is about ink.

## The ratio

```
data-ink ratio = data ink / total ink used in the graphic
```

**Data ink** is the ink that would change if the numbers changed: the bars, the points, the
line. Everything else, such as frames, shading, background colour, heavy gridlines and 3-D
depth, is non-data ink. It may be needed (an axis is), but each piece has to earn its place.

Slide 4 draws the same grouped bar chart twice. The pseudo-3D version spends much of its ink
on side faces and a floor, and the perspective makes the heights harder to compare. The flat
version shows exactly the same numbers with a fraction of the ink, and you can read it
faster.

## How to use it

The ratio is a direction to push, not a score to report. Look at each element and ask: *if I
erased this, would the reader lose any information?* If not, erase it, or make it lighter.
Gridlines are a common case: often they can go to a pale grey rather than disappear.

The ratio can be pushed too far. An axis with no labels has very little non-data ink and is
useless. The principle sits alongside "use proper scales and clear labelling", not above it.

> A chart's job is to show numbers. Ink that does not show a number, or help the reader decode
> one, is a cost. Remove it or lighten it.
