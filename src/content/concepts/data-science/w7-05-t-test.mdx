A sample of 20 men and 20 women take an IQ test. The two sample means will certainly differ;
two samples almost never match exactly. The slide's question is whether **the difference is
significant**. The **t-test** answers it: it evaluates whether the *population* means of two
samples are different.

## What makes two means look different

The six plots on slide 11 show the idea before any formula. Two bell curves are easier to
tell apart when:

- the **means move apart**, or
- the **spread decreases**, so each curve is narrower and they overlap less.

Slide 12 adds a third factor. Two means differ significantly if:

| condition | why it helps |
| --- | --- |
| the mean difference is relatively large | more signal |
| the standard deviations are small enough | less noise |
| the samples are large enough | the means themselves are pinned down more precisely |

## Welch's t-statistic

These three ideas combine into one number:

```
         x̄₁ − x̄₂
t = ─────────────────
     √( s₁²/n₁ + s₂²/n₂ )
```

It is a **signal-to-noise ratio**.

- The **numerator** is the signal: the difference between the two sample means.
- The **denominator** is the noise: how much a difference in means would wobble from sample
  to sample. Each group contributes its variance `s²` divided by its size `n`. Bigger
  samples shrink this term, just as the central limit theorem shrank the spread of a mean to
  `σ/√n` last week.

So `t` measures how many "units of noise" apart the two means are. A `t` of 0.5 is well within
the wobble. A `t` of 4 is far outside it. The sign only says which group is larger. For a
two-sided test you compare its size, ignoring the sign.

This version is **Welch's** t-test, which lets the two groups have different variances. It
does not assume the spreads are equal.

## Degrees of freedom and the table

To turn `t` into a decision you compare it with a **critical value** from a t-distribution
table. Which row of the table depends on the **degrees of freedom**, `df`, which the slide
describes as *the amount of information we have to estimate variability*. For two independent
samples the slides use

```
df ≈ n₁ + n₂ − 2
```

With few degrees of freedom the t-distribution has wider tails, because a variance estimated
from a handful of points is itself uncertain. So small samples need a bigger `t` to count as
significant. As `df` grows, the critical value for `α = 0.05` (two-tailed) falls towards
about 1.96, the normal distribution's value.

The rule is: if `|t|` is larger than the critical value, reject `H₀`. The next concept works
through one in full. In practice, statistics software skips the table and reports the p-value
for your `t` and `df` directly. The decision is the same: reject `H₀` when `p < α`.

> The t-statistic is the gap between two means divided by its uncertainty. Bigger gaps, smaller
> spreads and larger samples all push it up, and the table says how big is big enough.
