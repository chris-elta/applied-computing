After a catalogue of chart types, the lecture shows what the best visualisations achieve.
Slide 36 sets the standard. Great visualisations:

- **display data accurately and clearly**;
- **tell a story that the data reveals**;
- **are rich enough to make you want to look carefully and study the data**.

The third point is the surprise. Great graphics are often *dense*, not minimal. They obey the
data-ink principle by making nearly every mark carry data, not by having few marks.

## Four examples

| graphic | what it encodes | the story |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Minard, Napoleon's march (slide 37) | army size as band width, position on a map, direction by colour, temperature on the retreat | the army shrinks from 422,000 to 10,000, and the retreat freezes |
| New York weather, one year (slide 38) | daily highs, lows, record and normal ranges, plus rainfall | over 3,000 numbers, readable as one year |
| Marey's train schedule (slide 39) | each train a line through time (across) and stations (down) | speed as slope, stops as flat segments, passing points as crossings |
| Snow's cholera map (slide 40) | each death as a mark on a street map | deaths cluster round one water pump, the source of the 1854 epidemic |

Minard's graphic is usually called the best statistical chart ever drawn because it shows six
variables at once without a legend to decode.

Snow's map is the one that changed something. Plotting deaths by address made the cluster
around the Broad Street pump obvious in a way a list of addresses never could, and it pointed
to the cause.

## Marey and "never imprison your data"

Slide 39 asks what you can see in Marey's chart that you cannot in a normal timetable.
Because time runs across and distance down, every line's **slope is the train's speed**.
Express trains are steep; slow trains are shallow; a flat stretch is a stop; two lines
crossing are trains passing.

The slide's critique is that it "would be even better with a lighter datagrid": the heavy
grid competes with the train lines. *Never imprison your data* behind bars of gridlines.

Slide 41 adds a modern case: a 2020 election graphic that uses a winding, colour-coded path,
"what might have been chartjunk", to show every state's margin in order in one view. Decoration
is fine when it encodes something.

> A great graphic shows many variables at once, makes the important pattern visible without
> reading numbers, and rewards a second look. Richness is fine; junk is not.
