Aligning events from different data sets and systems can be problematic. A timestamp that
reads 09:00 means nothing until you know *where* and *under which calendar*.

## Use UTC

The slide's recommendation is **Coordinated Universal Time (UTC), a modern standard
subsuming GMT.** Store everything in one reference time, so that equal timestamps mean
simultaneous events whichever system recorded them.

## Calendars with gaps

**Financial time series are tricky because of weekends and holidays.** Markets have no prices
on days they are closed, while temperature has a value every day. So the slide asks: *how do
you correlate stock prices and temperatures?* Options include comparing only trading days,
or carrying the last price forward; each choice is a decision that belongs in the pipeline,
written down.

## The September 1752 calendar

The slide shows the calendar for September 1752, which has a 2nd followed immediately by the
14th. Dates in the lecture's calendar jump by 11 days that month, a reminder that the
calendar itself has changed over history. Few projects reach back that far, but any
long-range data set can contain a date convention that is not today's.

> Choose one time standard at the start, and make every alignment of calendars an explicit
> step rather than an accident of how the files were joined.
