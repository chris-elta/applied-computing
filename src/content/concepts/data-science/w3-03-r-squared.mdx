Last week you learnt that variance measures how spread out data is. A model earns its keep
by shrinking that spread. Predict every `y` with the plain mean and you are left with the
full variance. Predict with a good line and what remains, the **residuals** `y − f(x)`, is
more tightly clustered.

## How much did the line help?

Compare the two amounts of leftover spread:

```
SS_tot = Σ (y − ȳ)²        error if you always guess the mean
SS_res = Σ (y − f(x))²     error after using the model

R² = 1 − SS_res / SS_tot
```

If the model removes half the squared error, `R² = 0.5`. If it removes none, `R² = 0`. That
is the slide's **coefficient of determination**, and the lecture's shorthand for it is the
identity `1 − r² = V(residuals) / V(y)`: what remains is the fraction that `x` did not
explain.

## Link to r

For a simple linear regression, `R²` equals `r²`, the square of the correlation. So `r²`
**estimates the fraction of the variance in `Y` explained by `X`**.

Worked examples from the slides:

- Height and weight: `r ≈ 0.8`, so `r² ≈ 0.64`, about **two thirds** of the variance.
- A fit with `r = 0.94` gives `r² = 0.884`. The slide's scatter plot explains **88.4%**
  of `V(y)`.

## The consequence

Because of the square, correlations shrink fast. `r = 0.5` explains 25% of the variance, and
`r = 0.3` explains 9%. The slide's warning: **the predictive value of a correlation decreases
quadratically with `r`.** The 0.19 between video games and violent behaviour, from the
previous concept, accounts for under 4% of the variation.

## Beyond a line

`R²` is defined through residuals, so it is not tied to straight lines. The slide's note
that **models better than linear regression can produce a better `R²`** is the reason to
define it this way: any model that predicts `y` from `x`, however elaborate, can be scored by
how much squared error it removes.

> `r` tells you how tightly the points hug a line. `r²` tells you what share of the
> variation you can actually account for, and it is always smaller than it first looks.
