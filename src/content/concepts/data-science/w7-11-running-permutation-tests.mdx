The previous concept built a distribution of shuffled statistics. This one reads a p-value off
it, and covers how to run the test well.

## The rank is the p-value

From slide 25: **the rank of the real data among the random permutations determines
significance.**

Each histogram on the slide shows the statistic from about a thousand shuffles. The purple line
marks the real value.

- **Left:** the real value sits among the shuffles, near the middle. Plenty of shuffled data
  sets do as well. Not significant.
- **Right:** the real value is far to the right of every shuffle. Random relabelling never got
  close. Highly significant.

To turn this into a number, count the shuffles at least as extreme as the real one:

```
p ≈ k / N
```

For example, with `N = 1,000` shuffles and `k = 12` that match or beat the real statistic,
`p ≈ 12/1000 = 0.012`. This is the same definition as before: how often a world where `H₀`
holds produces something at least this extreme. The only difference is that you simulated that
world rather than looking it up.

## How many permutations

Slide 26: **the more permutations you try (at least 1,000), the more impressive your
significance can be.**

The reason is resolution. If the real value beats all `N` shuffles, the best you can claim is
`p < 1/N`. With 100 shuffles you cannot show anything stronger than `p < 0.01`, however
extreme your data. With 10,000 you can show `p < 0.0001`. Shuffling is cheap, so run plenty.
This matters especially after a Bonferroni correction, when the threshold might be 0.0001.

## What to shuffle

The slide says to keep comparisons **apples-to-apples**. Typically you permute:

- **the values of a field across records**, such as which person has which label; or
- **time-points within a record**, such as the order of one person's measurements.

Shuffle only the thing your hypothesis says does not matter, and keep everything else fixed. For
the heights example, shuffle the gender labels and keep each person's height. Shuffling heights
across unrelated data sets would break comparisons the hypothesis never questioned.

## A sanity check for models

The slide ends with a warning: **if your model shows decent performance trained on random data,
you have a problem.**

This turns the permutation test into a check on a whole pipeline. Shuffle the labels, retrain,
and evaluate. There is now nothing real to learn, so the score should fall to chance. If it
stays high, the evaluation is rewarding something other than the signal. Common culprits are a
test set dominated by one class, or information leaking from the labels into the features.

> Shuffle many times, count how often chance matches the real result, and that fraction is your
> p-value. A model that succeeds on shuffled labels is measuring something else.
