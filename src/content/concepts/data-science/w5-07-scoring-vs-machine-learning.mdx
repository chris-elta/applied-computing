If you already know some machine learning, the last few concepts may have seemed strange.
Why design a scoring formula by hand and argue about weights? Why not let an algorithm learn
the best weights from data?

The slides answer directly: **the critical issue in designing scoring functions is that
there is no gold standard / right answer.**

## What learning needs

A method like linear regression can learn a scoring function from features. It needs
*training data* to do so: a pile of examples, each with the right answer attached. It
adjusts its weights until its outputs match those answers. The slide adds: **which
generally you don't** have.

| you have | you can | example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A gold standard | **Train** a regression function to predict it accurately | Images labelled "cancer" or "no cancer" by doctors |
| Only proxies | **Evaluate** your scoring function, and no more | A student-potential score checked against later results |

The second row describes most of this week. With proxies you can design a score and then
ask whether it lines up with things you would expect it to line up with. You cannot fit it
to the truth, because the truth is not in your data.

## Google is a partial exception

The slide gives one line: **Google's ranking algorithms train on click data.** Nobody knows
the true best page for a search. But when millions of people click the third result and not
the first, that behaviour is close enough to an answer to train on. Large amounts of
behaviour can stand in for a gold standard, which is why companies with that kind of data
learn their rankings while everyone else designs theirs.

Clicks are still a proxy, though. People also click on what is eye-catching.

## Weapons of math destruction

The last line of the slide: **"Weapons of *Math* Destruction" happen when you confuse
proxies for gold standards**, for example student test scores for teaching quality. The
phrase is the title of Cathy O'Neil's book.

Student test scores are a reasonable proxy for teaching quality, in that the two are
related. They are also driven by class size, home resources, what students knew the year
before and plain luck. Use them to prompt a conversation and little harm is done. Use them
as the truth, and rank, reward and dismiss teachers on them, and you are making consequential
decisions on a number that mostly measures other things. Because it is a number, it is
harder to argue with than a person's judgement would be.

What makes a score dangerous is how firmly people act on it compared with how well it
tracks the goal.

> A gold standard lets you train a predictor. A proxy only lets you evaluate a score. The
> damage comes from treating the second situation as the first.
