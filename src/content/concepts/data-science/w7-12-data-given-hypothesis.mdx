The p-value concept warned that a p-value is not the probability that the null hypothesis is
true. Slide 27 puts the same warning on permutation tests, labelled *caveat!*

## Two conditionals

From the slide: **permutation tests give you the probability of your data given your
hypothesis. This is not the same as the probability of your hypothesis given your data, which is
the traditional goal of significance testing.**

| | what it says | who computes it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| data given hypothesis | if this hypothesis held, how likely is data like this? | permutation tests, t-tests, p-values |
| hypothesis given data | given this data, how likely is the hypothesis? | what you actually want to know |

The two can be wildly different. The chance that a person is a professional basketball player
given that they are over two metres tall is low. The chance that they are over two metres tall
given that they are a professional basketball player is high. Swapping the conditional is an
easy mistake to make, and it is the most common error in reading significance.

## Connecting them

Slide 21 explains why significance tests can work at all. They come from two ideas:

- **probabilities of samples drawn from distributions** with a given mean and standard
  deviation. That is `Pr(data | distribution)`, what the tests compute;
- **Bayes' theorem**, from week 2, which converts `Pr(data | distribution)` into
  `Pr(distribution | data)`.

The conversion needs one more ingredient: the **prior**, `Pr(H)`, how plausible the hypothesis
was before you looked.

```
Pr(H | D) = Pr(D | H) · Pr(H) / Pr(D)
```

## A worked example

A lab screens 1,000 compounds for an effect. Suppose 10 truly work. Each test uses `α = 0.05`
and detects a working compound 80% of the time.

| | count | test significant |
| --- | --- | --- |
| working compounds | 10 | `10 × 0.80 = 8` |
| compounds with no effect | 990 | `990 × 0.05 ≈ 50` |
| **total significant** | | **≈ 58** |

Every test passed `p < 0.05`. Yet only `8 / 58 ≈ 14%` of the significant compounds actually
work. The rest are the false positives the Bonferroni concept warned about. When real effects
are rare, most significant results can be false, even with every test done correctly.

## What to take from it

A small p-value says the data would be surprising if nothing were going on. How much that
should change your belief depends on how plausible the effect was to begin with. A surprising
result for a long-shot idea deserves more doubt than the same p-value for a well-motivated one.

> Significance tests compute the probability of the data given the hypothesis. Getting to the
> probability of the hypothesis needs Bayes' theorem and a prior, and when real effects are rare,
> most significant results are false.
