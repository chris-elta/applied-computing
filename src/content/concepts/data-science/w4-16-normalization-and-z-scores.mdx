Suppose you want to compare how unusual a person's height is with how unusual their income
is. Inches and dollars cannot be put on one scale directly. The slide's rule: **it is
critical to normalize different variables to make their range and distribution comparable.**
It adds that *your biggest analysis sins will come in using unnormalized variables.*

## The z-score

Subtract the mean, then divide by the standard deviation:

`Z_i = (X_i − X̄) / σ`

The numerator is how far value `i` is from the average, in the variable's own units. Dividing
by `σ` re-counts that distance in standard deviations, so the units cancel.

## The slide's example

Ten values of `B` have mean `μ(B) = 21.9` and standard deviation `σ(B) = 1.92`.

| B | 19 | 22 | 24 | 20 | 23 | 19 | 21 | 24 | 24 | 23 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Z | −1.51 | 0.05 | 1.09 | −0.98 | 0.57 | −1.51 | −0.46 | 1.09 | 1.09 | 0.57 |

Three things to read off it:

- **Z-scores have mean 0 and standard deviation 1**, whatever the original variable was.
- So **z-scores of different variables are of comparable magnitude.**
- **The sign says above or below the mean.** 19 is below, so −1.51; 24 is above, so 1.09.

## Why the units vanish

Z-scores of height in inches are the same as of height in miles. Converting units multiplies
every value, the mean, and `σ` by the same factor, and the factor cancels in the division.

This is the tool for the problem in the previous concept, and the cure for combining
variables on very different scales into one analysis, where the one with the biggest numbers
would otherwise dominate.

> A z-score answers "how unusual is this, for this variable?" That question has no units,
> which is why it can be asked of anything.
