Ask ten practitioners what data science is and you get ten answers. The lecture says so
directly: like any emerging field, it **isn't well defined**. What it can do is name the
ingredients it mixes.

| Ingredient | What it contributes |
| --- | --- |
| Exploratory data analysis and visualisation | Finding out what is in the data, and showing it |
| Machine learning and statistics | Turning data into predictions and defensible claims |
| High-performance computing | Coping when the data is too big to handle casually |

No single row is the field. A person who only visualises is an analyst; one who only fits
models is a modeller. The job is the combination.

## Why now

Three things changed, and the field followed them:

1. **Capture.** New technology makes it possible to record vast amounts of logging and
   sensor data, where before there was nothing to analyse.
2. **Compute.** Computing advances let us analyse data at ever larger scales.
3. **Proof.** Prominent role models — Google, *Moneyball*, hedge funds, Nate Silver — showed
   that modern data analytics really does pay.

The third reason is easy to skip past. Data and compute being available is not enough for a
field to form; people have to believe the effort returns something. The role models are what
turned a capability into a career.

## Data is not new to computing

The next slide makes a point that is easy to lose: computing has always dealt in data. What
is new is the *volume* captured and the *ambition* of what people now ask of it, not the
existence of data itself.

> A field defined by its ingredients, not its boundary. When someone asks what you do, the
> honest answer is a combination of looking, modelling and scaling.
