Two sources are free to use, and worth checking first.

## Government

City, state and federal governments are increasingly committed to open data. The slide's
example is Data.gov, with over 250,000 open data sets. Where something you need is not open,
the **Freedom of Information Act (FOI)** lets you ask.

The constraint is **privacy**. Whether a data set can be released often depends on whether
the people in it can be protected, so some data comes aggregated or not at all.

## Academic

Making data available is now a requirement for publication in many fields. The slide expects
you to find **economic, medical, demographic and meteorological** data if you look hard
enough. Three tactics are listed:

1. Track the data down from relevant papers.
2. Ask the authors.
3. Search for the topic together with "Open Science" or "data".

## Why start here

These sources are documented, legal to use and often curated. They also come with a cost the
earlier concepts hinted at: they were collected for someone else's question, so check that
the definitions and the time period fit yours.

> Search government and academic sources before anything harder, and when data is missing,
> ask — the answer is sometimes yes.
