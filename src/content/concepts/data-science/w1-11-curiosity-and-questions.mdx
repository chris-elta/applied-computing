Two habits come out of these slides, and they feed each other.

## Curiosity

The good data scientist develops a **curiosity about the domain** they work in. They
**talk shop with the people whose data they are working on**. A dataset is a record of
something that happened; the people who were there know what the columns actually mean and
which values to distrust.

## Asking good questions

The lecture makes a pointed contrast: software developers are not encouraged to ask
questions, but data scientists are. Three prompts to keep to hand:

- What exciting things might you be able to **learn** from a given data set?
- What do you, or your people, **really want to know**?
- What data sets might **get you there**?

Notice the order. Question first, data second. The reverse, picking whatever data exists and
seeing what it says, is how you produce a chart that answers nothing.

## Practising

The next slide sets an exercise: for each of four datasets — Baseball-Reference.com, the
International Movie Database (IMDb), Google Ngrams and NYC taxi cab records — ask **who,
what, where, when and why**. The following concepts walk through them.

As you read them, look at how the lecturer's questions relate to the data. Some can be
answered directly from a column, and some need other data joined in. Working out which is
which is the skill.

> Good data science starts before the data: with a question somebody cares about.
