The previous concept said not to use zero. What *should* you put in a gap?

With enough training data, one might **drop all records with missing values**. But we may want
to use the model on records with missing fields, and then throwing away the model's input is
not an option. The lecture's position: **often it is better to estimate, or impute, missing
values instead of leaving them blank.**

The slide's example: a good guess for your year of death is birth + 80. It is wrong for any
individual and reasonable as a placeholder.

## Three methods

| Method | What it does | What the slide says about it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Mean value** | Replace each gap with the mean of the field | Leaves the mean the same |
| **Random value** | Fill gaps with randomly chosen values, repeatedly | Allows statistical evaluation of the impact of imputation |
| **Interpolation** | Predict the value from the record's other fields, using linear regression | Works well if few fields are missing per record |

## Choosing

- **Mean** is the simplest. It keeps the average and shrinks the spread, because the filled-in
  values are all identical.
- **Random** values, drawn many times, let you ask how much your conclusion depends on the
  guesses: if the answer changes a lot between runs, the missing data matters.
- **Interpolation** uses more information, and so does better when most of the record is
  present. If many fields are missing there is little to predict from.

> Imputed numbers are guesses. Use them to keep the pipeline running, and check how much the
> result moves when the guesses change.
