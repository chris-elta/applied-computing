Suppose a die is rolled. There are six outcomes, and no matter how you feel about the die,
some rules apply.

## The two rules

Probability theory gives a formal framework for reasoning about the likelihood of events.
The probability `p(s)` of an outcome `s` satisfies:

1. `0 ≤ p(s) ≤ 1` — no outcome is less than impossible or more than certain.
2. The probabilities of all outcomes add to one: `∑ p(s) = 1`, summing over every `s` in `S`.

The second rule says something is bound to happen. If you have listed every outcome and the
numbers do not add to 1, either a probability is wrong or an outcome is missing.

The lecture adds a warning: **these basic properties are often violated in casual use** of
the word "probability" in data science. Scores from a classifier, for example, may be called
probabilities without ever being checked against these two rules.

## Probability versus statistics

The two words are used loosely, but the slides separate them:

| | Probability | Statistics |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Looks at | Likelihood of *future* events | Frequency of *past* events |
| Nature | A theoretical branch of mathematics: the consequences of definitions | Applied mathematics: making sense of real-world observations |
| Starts from | A model of how things work | Data that was observed |

Probability runs forwards: given the rules of the game, what will happen? Statistics runs
backwards: given what happened, what were the rules?

Data science mostly lives on the statistics side, since it starts with observations. But it
borrows probability's language to make claims, so the rules above must still be respected.

> If a set of "probabilities" does not add to one, it is not a probability distribution,
> whatever it is called.
