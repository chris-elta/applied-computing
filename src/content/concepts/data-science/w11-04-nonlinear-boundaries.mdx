A straight line cannot separate every pair of classes. Slide 11 shows blue circles clustered
around the origin with red crosses in a ring around them. No line puts all the circles on one
side.

Week 10 met the same limitation with curved data and solved it by adding polynomial features.
The same move works here.

## Adding squared features

Give the model two more features, `x₁²` and `x₂²`:

```
hθ(x) = g(θ₀ + θ₁x₁ + θ₂x₂ + θ₃x₁² + θ₄x₂²)
```

Suppose training gives `θ₀ = −1`, `θ₁ = 0`, `θ₂ = 0`, `θ₃ = 1`, `θ₄ = 1`. The rule from the
previous concept says predict `y = 1` when the sum inside `g` is at least zero:

```
−1 + x₁² + x₂² ≥ 0      that is,      x₁² + x₂² ≥ 1
```

`x₁² + x₂² = 1` is a circle of radius 1 centred on the origin. Points outside the circle are
predicted positive, points inside negative.

| point | `x₁² + x₂²` | prediction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `(0, 0)` | 0 | `y = 0` (inside) |
| `(0.5, 0.5)` | 0.5 | `y = 0` (inside) |
| `(1, 1)` | 2 | `y = 1` (outside) |

## Still a linear model

Nothing about the method changed. `θᵀx` is still "multiply each feature by its parameter and
add". The model draws a flat boundary in a space with four features, and that flat boundary
appears as a circle when you plot only `x₁` and `x₂`. This is what the lecturer's later slide
means by a separator that is "linear in the possibly non-linear features".

## Going further, with care

The second half of slide 11 adds higher-order terms such as `x₁²x₂`, `x₁²x₂²` and `x₁³x₂`.
With enough of them the boundary can bend into almost any shape.

That power has a price you have already paid once. A boundary flexible enough to wrap around
every training point will also wrap around the noise. This is the overfitting from week 10,
and the same remedy, regularisation, applies.

> Logistic regression draws curved boundaries when you hand it curved features. The model
> stays linear in θ, so everything about fitting it stays the same. More features mean more
> flexibility and more risk of overfitting.
