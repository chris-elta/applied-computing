Slide 65 sorts models by where their structure comes from.

## Two methodologies

| | first-principles model | data-driven model |
| --- | --- | --- |
| built from | a theoretical explanation of how the system works | observed correlations between inputs and outcome |
| examples | simulations, scientific formulae | regression, classifiers learned from data |
| strength | explains *why*; works outside the range of past data | needs no theory; captures effects nobody wrote down |
| typical failure | **bias**: the theory leaves something out | **overfitting**: finds patterns in noise |

The lecture notes' first-principles example is Newton's law of gravity,
`F = G·m₁·m₂ / r²`. Nobody fitted it to a scatter plot of apples. It comes from a theory, and
it predicts the orbit of a planet nobody has measured before.

A data-driven model learns the shape of the relationship from examples, "without prior
assumptions". It will happily find that ice-cream sales predict drownings. That correlation
is real in the data, and useful for prediction while summers stay summers, but it says
nothing about cause.

The failure column links back to the bias-variance concept: theory constrains a model, which
protects against variance and risks bias; data frees it, which does the reverse.

## Good models mix both

The slide's conclusion, in blue: **"Good models are typically a mixture of both."**

In practice that means using theory to choose the model's form and features, then letting
data set the parameters and correct what the theory misses. A delivery-time model might start
from *distance ÷ road speed* and learn adjustments for traffic and time of day. A model of
house prices starts from the knowledge that bigger houses cost more, and uses data to find
out how much more.

Google Flu Trends, from the previous concept, is the warning for the purely data-driven case:
with no theory of why searches tracked flu, nothing in the model could notice when they
stopped.

> Theory gives a model structure that does not overfit; data fills in what theory misses. Use
> both.
