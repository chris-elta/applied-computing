Each LED bulb from a factory has a 1% chance of burning out on any given day. How long will
a bulb last?

## Building the distribution

The slide frames it as a question about you: **if your chance of surviving any given day is
probability `p`, what is your lifespan distribution?** The answer comes from one
observation: **a lifespan of `n` days means dying for the first time on day `n`.**

So you need `n − 1` survivals followed by one failure. With independent days, multiply. The
slide writes it with `p` for survival:

```
Pr(n) = pⁿ⁻¹ (1 − p)
```

**A note on `p`.** The next slide reuses `p` for the chance of *failing*, which is
`p = 0.01` for the bulbs. Both conventions are common. This page writes `q` for survival and
`p` for failure, so the formula reads `Pr(n) = qⁿ⁻¹ p`.

For the bulbs, `p = 0.01` and `q = 0.99`:

| lifespan (days) | formula | probability |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | `0.01` | 0.0100 |
| 2 | `0.99 × 0.01` | 0.0099 |
| 3 | `0.99² × 0.01` | 0.0098 |
| 10 | `0.99⁹ × 0.01` | 0.0091 |
| 100 | `0.99⁹⁹ × 0.01` | 0.0037 |

The single most likely lifespan is 1 day. Every later day requires surviving all the days
before, so each probability is a little smaller than the last.

## Expected lifespan

The mean has a simple form:

```
E[X] = 1/p = 1/0.01 = 100 days
```

It makes sense: an event with a 1-in-100 chance per day takes 100 days on average to happen.

## Why not "dead bulbs per 1,000 hours"?

The slide says **lightbulb lifespans are better modeled with such a distribution, not dead
bulbs per 1,000 hours.** A failure rate tells you how many bulbs die in a period. The
geometric distribution tells you how long *one* bulb lasts, which is what a buyer wants to
know. It also shows the spread: some bulbs die in the first week, and a few last for years.

## The assumption

Everything rests on the failure chance being **the same every day**, regardless of age. A
bulb that has lasted 200 days is no more likely to fail tomorrow than a new one. That suits
some electronics. It is a poor fit for people, who age, and for machines that wear out. The
human-lifespan concept later in this section returns to this.

> Waiting for a first event with a fixed chance `p` per try gives the geometric
> distribution: `Pr(n) = (1−p)ⁿ⁻¹ p`, mean `1/p`. It holds only when that chance does not
> change with age.
