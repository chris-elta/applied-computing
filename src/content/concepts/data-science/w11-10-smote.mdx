The previous concept ended with replicating the minority class "ideally with random
perturbation". **SMOTE**, the Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique, is a specific way of
doing that. It does not copy rare examples. It invents new ones in plausible places.

## Where to put a new example

Suppose you have two real fraud cases that resemble each other. A transaction halfway between
them, in every feature, would probably also be fraud. SMOTE generates points like that.

The recipe on slide 11:

1. Take a minority example `p`.
2. Find a near neighbour `n` of `p` that is also in the minority class.
3. Pick a random number `c` between 0 and 1.
4. Create the new example at `p + c·(n − p)`.

`n − p` is the step from `p` to `n`. Multiplying it by `c` takes part of that step, so the new
point lies somewhere on the straight segment joining the two.

## Worked example

Let `p = (2, 3)` and `n = (4, 7)`. Then `n − p = (2, 4)`.

| `c` | `c·(n − p)` | new point |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | `(0, 0)` | `(2, 3)`, which is `p` |
| 0.25 | `(0.5, 1)` | `(2.5, 4)` |
| 0.5 | `(1, 2)` | `(3, 5)`, the midpoint |
| 1 | `(2, 4)` | `(4, 7)`, which is `n` |

Because `c` is random, repeating the recipe scatters new points along the segments between
neighbouring minority examples. The slide's lower figure shows this: a selected minority
sample, its nearest neighbours, and synthetic instances on the lines between them.

## Why not plain copies

Exact duplicates stack on top of the original. The model sees the same 50 points many times
and can draw a tight boundary around each. SMOTE's points fill in the region the minority
class occupies, so the model learns an area and not a handful of coordinates.

The slide's phrase is "in the right places". Random noise in any direction could push a
synthetic point into majority territory. Staying between two real minority examples keeps it
where the minority class has actually been seen.

## What SMOTE cannot do

Synthetic points are interpolations. They contain no information that was not already in the
real examples, and they assume the space between two minority points belongs to the minority
class. If the classes interleave, that assumption is false and SMOTE manufactures mislabelled
data. As with any balancing, apply it to the training set only, and evaluate on untouched
data.

> SMOTE balances classes by creating new minority examples at a random position on the
> line between a minority point and one of its minority neighbours. It spreads the class out;
> it does not add knowledge.
