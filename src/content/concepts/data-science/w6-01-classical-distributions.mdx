A polling company phones 1,000 voters and finds 52% support a policy. What it wants to know
is what *all* voters think. Getting from one to the other is the job of statistics, and this
week's distributions are the main tools for it.

## The central dogma

The lecture's diagram has two arrows between a **population** and a **sample**:

| direction | question | tool |
| --- | --- | --- |
| population → sample | If the population looks like this, what samples should I expect? | probability |
| sample → population | Given the sample I got, what does the population probably look like? | inferential statistics |

Summarising the sample itself, with a mean, a median or a standard deviation, is
**descriptive statistics**, the subject of Week 2.

The arrow that matters in practice is inference, and it depends on the probability arrow.
You can only judge whether 52% from 1,000 calls is surprising if you know how much samples of
1,000 tend to wander. That is a question about a distribution.

## Four distributions that keep turning up

Week 2 described a random variable by its distribution: how likely each value is. Any
distribution is possible, but a few shapes recur so often in practice and in theory that
they have names. This week covers four:

| distribution | typical question |
| --- | --- |
| binomial | How many heads in 200 coin flips? |
| normal | How unusual is an IQ of 130? |
| Poisson | How many calls will arrive in the next hour? |
| power law | How big is the biggest city likely to be? |

## Why name them

The slide gives two reasons to look out for them:

1. They **arise often**, so recognising one saves you modelling from scratch.
2. **Closed-form formulas and special statistical tests** exist for them. If a count is
   binomial, its spread is `√(np(1−p))` and you do not have to estimate it.

## The warning

The slide puts its caution in a different colour: **your observed data does not necessarily
come from a particular distribution just because the shape looks similar.**

A bell-shaped histogram might be normal. It might also be two normals added together, or a
distribution with much heavier tails. Using the normal's formulas on such data gives
confident numbers that are wrong. Later concepts this week show both failures: the bell
curves that are not normal, and city sizes that a normal distribution says are impossible.

> A named distribution is a hypothesis about where your data came from. Its formulas are
> only as good as that hypothesis.
