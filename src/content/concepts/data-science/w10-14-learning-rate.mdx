Gradient descent's rule has two ingredients: the slope, which gives the direction, and `α`,
which gives the step size. Part 2 of the lecture goes back to one parameter to see each
clearly.

## The derivative gives the direction

With one parameter, the rule is (slide 5):

```
θ₁ := θ₁ − α · d J(θ₁)/dθ₁
```

The derivative is the slope of the tangent line at the current θ₁ (the course notes: "the
slope of tangent aka derivative").

| where θ₁ is | slope | update | effect |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| right of the minimum | positive | `θ₁ − α × (positive)` | θ₁ moves left |
| left of the minimum | negative | `θ₁ − α × (negative)` | θ₁ moves right |
| at a minimum | zero | `θ₁ − 0` | θ₁ stays put |

The minus sign makes it go downhill from either side.

## A worked step

Use the data (1, 1), (2, 2), (3, 3) from the one-parameter cost concept, with `hθ(x) = θ₁x`.
The derivative of that `J` is `(1/m) Σ (θ₁x⁽ⁱ⁾ − y⁽ⁱ⁾) x⁽ⁱ⁾`, derived in the next concept.

Start at θ₁ = 0, with α = 0.1:

```
slope = (1/3) × [(0 − 1)·1 + (0 − 2)·2 + (0 − 3)·3] = (1/3) × (−14) ≈ −4.67
θ₁   := 0 − 0.1 × (−4.67) ≈ 0.47
```

One step moves θ₁ from 0 to 0.47, toward the best value of 1. The next step will be smaller,
because closer to the minimum the slope is gentler.

## α gives the size

From slide 4:

- **If α is too small**, gradient descent can be slow. Each step is tiny; it gets there in the
  end, after many iterations.
- **If α is too large**, gradient descent can overshoot the minimum. It may fail to converge,
  or even diverge.

Try α = 1 on the same example. From θ₁ = 0 the step is +4.67, landing at θ₁ ≈ 4.67, far past 1
and higher up the bowl than where it started. There the slope is steeper still, about +17, so
the next step goes to roughly −12. Each overshoot is bigger than the last: that is divergence.

The course notes' practical test: plot `J` against the number of iterations. It should fall
every time. **If J ever increases, α is probably too large; decrease it.** Andrew Ng recommends
trying values that differ by factors of about 3: 0.001, 0.003, 0.01, 0.03, 0.1, and so on.

## α can stay fixed

Slide 5 also makes a quieter point: at a local optimum the derivative is zero, so the update
leaves θ₁ unchanged. More generally, the steps shrink on their own as you approach a minimum,
because the slope shrinks. You do not need to reduce α over time for gradient descent to settle.

> The derivative's sign sends θ downhill; α sets the stride. Too small is slow; too large
> overshoots and can diverge. If J ever goes up, reduce α.
