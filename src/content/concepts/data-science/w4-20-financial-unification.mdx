Money looks like the easiest quantity to compare, and it is full of traps. The slide lists
four corrections to apply before comparing financial data.

| Problem | Correction |
| --- | --- |
| Different currencies | Convert with exchange rates |
| Splits and dividends change the quoted price | Correct stock prices for splits and dividends |
| Absolute price changes depend on the price level | Use returns, the percentage change |
| A dollar in 1990 is not a dollar today | Correct for inflation (the time value of money) |

## Why each matters

A **stock split** makes a share cheaper without changing the company's value. A series that
is not corrected shows the split as a crash. **Returns** put a $5 move in a $10 stock and a
$5 move in a $500 stock on their own scales, +50% and +1%.

**Inflation** is the one behind the slide's question: **why do stock and oil prices correlate
over 30 years?** Both are quoted in a currency that loses value, so both drift up together in
raw dollars. This is the same trap as the correlation-and-causation concepts from week 3: two
series with a shared trend correlate without being related.

> Before correlating anything measured in money, ask which currency, which year, and whether
> the price was corrected for events that change the number but not the value.
