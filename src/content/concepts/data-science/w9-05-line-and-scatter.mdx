Line charts and scatter plots are both made of points on two axes. They differ in what the
points mean, and that decides what you are allowed to draw between them.

## Line charts

From slide 20:

- **Show data points, not just fits.** A smooth line alone hides how much data supports it.
- **Line segments show connections**, so do not use them for categorical data. A line from
  "apples" to "oranges" implies something in between.
- **Connecting points by lines is often chartjunk.** For noisy data, better is usually a
  trend line or fit *with* the data points visible.

A line says "these values are ordered and continuous". Time series qualify; categories do not.

## Scatter plots

A scatter plot shows the value of every point, and is "a great way to present 2D data sets"
(slide 21). For more than two dimensions you have choices:

- **project to 2D**, for example with principal component analysis;
- **encode extra dimensions in the dots**: colour, shape, size and shading. That is a
  **bubble chart**. Slide 24 shows Hans Rosling's health-against-wealth chart, where position
  gives two variables, bubble size gives population and colour gives region.

## Overplotting

With many points, they land on top of each other, and the dense middle becomes a solid
patch. You can no longer see where most of the data is, which is often the most important
thing.

| fix | slide | what it does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| smaller points | 22 | lets gaps show between points, so density becomes visible as darkness |
| colour by frequency | 23 | a heatmap: bins the plane and colours each bin by its count |

Slide 23's height-and-weight data shows why this matters. Drawn plainly it is a blue
rectangle; coloured by frequency, a clear dense core appears, along with the direction of the
relationship.

> Draw lines only between points that are genuinely ordered. When points pile up, shrink them
> or colour by count, because a solid blob hides the density you most need to see.
