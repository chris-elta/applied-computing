A significance level of 0.05 means that, if nothing is going on, a test will still come out
significant one time in twenty. For one carefully chosen hypothesis, that is an acceptable
risk. Now run a thousand tests.

## Fishing expeditions

Suppose you test 1,000 possible links (between features and outcomes, say) and none of them
is real. At `α = 0.05`, you should expect about

```
1,000 × 0.05 = 50
```

of them to come out "significant". Every one is a false positive, and nothing in the individual
results tells you which.

The slide calls this a **fishing expedition**: test enough hypotheses and something will bite.
The small chart in the corner of the slide shows the result. Arcade revenue and computer
science doctorates track each other closely over the years. Search enough pairs of time series
and you will find one like that.

The risk grows quickly even for modest numbers. With 20 independent tests and no real effects,
the chance that at least one comes out significant is

```
1 − 0.95²⁰ ≈ 1 − 0.36 = 0.64
```

So it is more likely than not.

## The correction

The slide's rule: **in testing `n` hypotheses, a result must reach `α/n` to be considered
significant at level `α`.**

| tests `n` | per-test threshold for overall `α = 0.05` |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | 0.05 |
| 20 | 0.0025 |
| 1,000 | 0.00005 |
| 1,000,000 | 0.00000005 |

Dividing by `n` keeps the chance of *any* false positive across the whole set at or below
`α`. That is why the slide says fishing expeditions *must be held to higher standards*.

## The cost, and the habit

The correction is deliberately cautious. With a very strict threshold, real but modest effects
can be missed. That is a price you pay for searching widely instead of testing one idea you had
in advance.

The practical habit is to **count your tests honestly**. That includes the ones you tried and
did not report: every feature you checked, every subgroup you sliced, every time window you
tried. A p-value of 0.03 means something very different after one test than after fifty.

> Every test is another chance for luck to look like a finding. When you test `n` hypotheses,
> require `p < α/n`, and count every test you ran.
