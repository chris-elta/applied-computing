The lecture builds an example around a bibliographic study. Using PubMed data, the team
identified the **year of first publication for the 100,000 most frequently cited authors**,
then plotted how many top authors started in each year.

Before showing the plot, the slide asks: **what *should* the distribution look like?** The
lesson is stated outright: *it is important to have a preconception of any result to help
detect anomalies.*

## What the plot showed

The histogram has a tall peak in the mid-1960s and a long decline after it. That much is plausible. But there is also a **sharp isolated spike around
2002**, with almost nothing around it. The lecturer's remark: a student once tried to foist
this off on them.

Real careers do not do that. Nothing happened in 2002 to make thousands of scientists publish
for the first time in one year, so the spike looks like an artifact. The slide asks what
artifacts you see and what could cause them.

## The cause

PubMed **began using authors' first names in 2002.** Before that an author was "F Riahi"; from
2002 they were "Fatemeh Riahi". A program matching exact names concluded that every author
whose name changed form was a new person appearing in 2002.

After unifying the names the spike is gone, and the distribution is the smooth decline you
would have predicted. The slide's summary: *data cleaning gets rid of such artifacts.*

## The method

1. State what the result ought to look like.
2. Compare with what you got.
3. Treat any mismatch as a bug until shown otherwise.
4. Look for a change in how the data was *recorded* at the date of the anomaly.

> The error was not in anyone's science. A change in record format created a feature that
> looked like a finding.
