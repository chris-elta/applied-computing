When one processor is too slow, use several. There are two ways of doing that, and slide 17
separates them by **how tightly coupled the machines are**.

| | parallel processing | distributed processing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| where | one machine | many machines |
| how the workers are made | threads and operating-system processes | separate computers |
| how they talk | shared memory, fast | network communication, slow and unreliable |

On one machine, the cores share the same memory, so passing data between them is nearly
free, and they fail together. Across many machines, every exchange travels over a network,
taking time and sometimes not arriving, and any machine can die while the others carry on.

## What makes a job easy

The slide gives the rule: **easy parallel jobs do not communicate much.**

If each worker can take its share and finish without asking the others anything, adding
workers just divides the time. Converting a million images to thumbnails is like this. If
the workers must keep exchanging partial results, they spend their time waiting for one
another, and more workers can make things slower.

So the first question to ask of any big job is how much its pieces need to talk.

The slide adds that training huge models on arrays of GPUs is a mix of both: many
processors inside each machine, and many machines connected by a network.

## Data parallelism

Slide 18 describes the easiest way to exploit parallelism: **partition the data among
multiple machines and train independent models**, one per piece.

Each machine has its own data and builds its own model. Nothing is communicated during
training, so this is an easy parallel job by the rule above.

Where to cut? The slide lists natural partitions:

- by **time**, such as one piece per month or year;
- by **clustering algorithms**, which group similar records together;
- by **given categories**, such as region or product line.

The idea connects to an earlier one. Many small models, each on its own slice, is what
personalisation looked like, and it parallelises perfectly because the slices never
interact.

## The catch

The slide ends: it is **typically hard to combine the results of these runs together
later**.

If what you wanted was five separate models, you are done. If you wanted one model of
everything, you now hold five models that never saw each other's data. Averaging their
parameters does not in general give the model that training on all the data would have
produced. The communication you avoided during training comes due at the end.

That is the reason for the systems in the rest of this section. They are designed around a
combining step that is defined in advance.

> Parallel means many workers in one machine, and distributed means many machines on a
> network. The less the workers must communicate, the easier the job. Splitting the data
> and training separately is easy; merging the results afterwards is the hard part.
