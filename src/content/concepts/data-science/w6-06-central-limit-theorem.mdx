Roll one die and every face from 1 to 6 is equally likely, a flat distribution with no bell
in sight. Now roll ten dice and take the average. Do it thousands of times and plot the
averages. They bunch around 3.5 in a clear bell.

That is the **central limit theorem**, and it is why the normal distribution is everywhere.

## The statement

From the slide: **the mean of `n` independent samples from any distribution converges to the
normal distribution.**

Two words carry it:

- **any** — the population can be flat, skewed, two-humped or lumpy. The slide's grid shows
  normal, uniform, skewed and irregular populations, and by `n = 30` all four rows of sample
  means look like the same bell.
- **independent** — each value must not depend on the others.

## Centre and spread

The slide gives the numbers: **if the population distribution has mean `μ` and SD `σ`, the
mean distribution of `n` samples has mean `μ` and SD `σ/√n`.**

| | single values | means of `n` values |
| --- | --- | --- |
| centre | `μ` | `μ` |
| spread | `σ` | `σ/√n` |
| shape | anything | approximately normal |

The spread shrinks because averaging cancels: a high value in the sample tends to be offset
by a low one. It shrinks by `√n`, not `n`, so it gets expensive. Halving the uncertainty of
an average needs four times the data.

For example, IQ has `σ = 15`. The average IQ of a random group of 25 has standard deviation
`15/√25 = 3`. A group averaging 106 is two of those units above 100, which by the
68–95–99.7 rule happens about 2.5% of the time.

## Why it matters

This is the link between the two arrows of the central dogma. You almost never know the
population's distribution. But if your statistic is an average, or a sum, the central limit
theorem tells you its distribution anyway: normal, centred on the truth, with spread
`σ/√n`. The 68–95–99.7 rule then becomes a statement about how far your estimate can be
trusted.

The theorem is about **means**, not individual values. Delivery times stay skewed no matter
how many you collect. It is the *average* delivery time that becomes normal. And the
convergence is gradual. Heavily skewed populations need larger `n` before the bell appears,
and the power laws at the end of this week can defeat it altogether.

> Averages of independent values come out normal, centred on the population mean, with
> spread `σ/√n`, whatever the population looks like.
