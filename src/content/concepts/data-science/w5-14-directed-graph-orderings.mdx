Elo processed match results one at a time. Another way to use them is to lay out every
result at once as a picture. Draw a dot for each team. Whenever A beats B, draw an arrow
from A to B. The slide: **treating the vote (A>B) as an edge (A,B) yields a directed
graph.**

A ranking is then a way of lining the dots up from left to right. A good ranking has the
arrows pointing forward, from better to worse.

## The consistent case

Suppose the results never contradict each other. There is no loop where A beats B, B beats
C, and C beats A. **If there are no inconsistencies, we get a directed acyclic graph
(DAG).** Acyclic means that following the arrows can never bring you back to where you
began.

For a DAG a perfect line-up always exists: an order in which **every** arrow points
forward. Finding one is called a *topological sort*. The slide: **topologically sorting this
DAG gives a reasonable order.**

## The slide's graph

The graph has seven vertices and these arrows:

- A → B
- B → C, B → D, B → E
- C → E
- D → E
- G → D
- E → F

Build an order by repeatedly taking a vertex that has no arrows coming in from vertices
still waiting.

1. Nothing beats A, and nothing beats G. Either can go first.
2. Take A. Now B is free. Take B, then C.
3. D still waits for G. Take G, then D.
4. E needed B, C and D, and all are placed. Take E, then F.

That gives **ABCGDEF**, one of the two orders on the slide. Take G first in step 1 and you
get the other, **GABCDEF**.

## Two answers

The data does not settle where G belongs. G was compared with D and nobody else, so the
votes say G is above D and say nothing about G against A, B or C. Both orders respect every
vote cast.

A topological sort tells you which orderings are consistent with the evidence. When several
are, the evidence is incomplete, and the algorithm's choice among them is arbitrary. Elo and
PageRank will always give you a single number per item. A DAG ordering shows you where the
comparisons run out.

It is also easy to explain. For any two items joined by a path of arrows you can point to
the chain of results that puts one above the other.

## The condition

All of this depends on the word *acyclic*. Real results contain upsets, and one upset can
close a loop. Then no order puts every arrow forward, and a topological sort does not exist.
The next concept deals with that case.

> If the comparisons never contradict each other, a topological sort gives an order that
> respects all of them, and there may be more than one.
