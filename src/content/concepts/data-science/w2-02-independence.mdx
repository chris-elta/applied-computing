Start with the slide's setup. Half of your students are female, and call that event `A`.
Half of your students are above the median, and call that event `B`. The question: *what is
the probability that a student is both?*

The tempting answer is a quarter, half of a half. That answer is correct **only if the two
events are independent**.

## The definition

Events `A` and `B` are independent if and only if

```
P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B)
```

In words: learning that `B` happened tells you nothing new about `A`. The lecture also
calls this **zero correlation**.

If the two are independent, the answer to the slide's question is `0.5 × 0.5 = 0.25`. If
they are not, the product is the wrong tool and you need more information, such as how many
above-median students are female. That extra information is what the next concept, on
conditional probability, formalises.

## A worked check

Suppose you count and find that 30% of all students are both female and above the median.
The independence test compares two numbers:

| Quantity | Value |
| --- | --- |
| `P(A) × P(B)` | 0.5 × 0.5 = 0.25 |
| `P(A ∩ B)` observed | 0.30 |

They differ, so the events are not independent. In this class, being female makes being above
the median a little more likely than it would be otherwise.

## Why the lecture warns about it

The slide ends with a sentence to hold on to:

> Independence (zero correlation) is good to simplify calculations but bad for prediction.

Both halves matter. Assuming independence turns a hard joint problem into a product of easy
single ones, which is why it is so tempting. But **prediction works precisely because
features are related**: if knowing one told you nothing about another, no feature could help
predict anything. An independence assumption throws away exactly the structure you hoped to
exploit.

## The misconception

People take "the events have equal probabilities" or "the events look unrelated" as evidence
of independence. Neither is. Independence is a numerical claim, checked by the product rule.

Independence returns in two places later this week. Conditional probability is trivial when
events are independent, and Bayes' theorem matters only when they are not.
