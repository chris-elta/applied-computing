Put the intercept `θ₀` back, and `J` depends on two numbers. Slide 26 restates the problem:

```
hθ(x) = θ₀ + θ₁x         minimise J(θ₀, θ₁) over θ₀, θ₁
```

## A surface

With two parameters, `J` is a surface over a plane. Slide 28 draws it in 3-D: `θ₀` and `θ₁`
along the floor, `J` as height. It is the one-parameter bowl from the previous concept, extended
in both directions. One point at the bottom is the best line.

3-D surfaces are hard to read on a page, so the slides switch to a **contour plot**: the same
surface seen from directly above, drawn as a set of closed curves. Each curve joins points of
**equal cost**. For the squared-error cost of a line, the curves are **ellipses**, nested
inside each other, and the minimum is at the centre of the innermost one.

## Reading it

- **Same ellipse, same cost.** Two points on one contour are two different lines that fit the
  data equally badly. A steep line with a low intercept can tie with a flat line with a high one.
- **The ellipses are tilted.** Changing `θ₀` and `θ₁` together along the tilt costs little,
  because a higher intercept can make up for a lower slope. That slant will matter for gradient
  descent.
- **The centre is the answer.** Finding the best line means finding the lowest point of this
  surface.

You would not want to search by eye with more parameters. The next section describes the
algorithm that does it.

> With two parameters, J is a bowl-shaped surface, drawn from above as nested ellipses of equal
> cost. Each point is one line, and the centre is the best fit.
