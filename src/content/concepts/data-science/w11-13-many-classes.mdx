One-vs-all works for three classes. Nothing stops you using it for three thousand, and slide
20 explains why that is much harder than it sounds.

## The monkey baseline

Week 9 introduced the monkey: a classifier that guesses at random. With `k` equally likely
classes it is right `1/k` of the time.

| classes `k` | random accuracy |
| --- | --- |
| 2 | 50% |
| 10 | 10% |
| 1,000 | 0.1% |

Every added class lowers the floor and gives the model more ways to be wrong. An accuracy
figure means nothing until you know `k`: 60% is weak for two classes and remarkable for a
thousand.

The slide adds two further difficulties.

**Some mistakes are more acceptable than others.** With many classes, some are near
neighbours. Calling a husky a wolf is a smaller error than calling it a toaster, yet plain
accuracy counts both as equally wrong.

**Rare classes become vanishingly small.** Split a fixed data set among more classes and
some will have a handful of examples, or one. Each one-vs-all classifier for a rare class is
then the grossly unbalanced problem from earlier in this section.

## Grouping the classes

Slide 21 offers a structural fix. Group the classes by similarity and build a **taxonomy**:
a tree with broad categories at the top and specific ones beneath.

The slide's example is ImageNet, which places 27 high-level categories (animal, appliance,
food, furniture and so on) above 21,841 subcategories.

Then **classify from the top down**. First decide which broad category the input belongs to.
Then, within that category only, decide which subcategory. Continue until you reach a leaf.

This is what the slide means by reducing the *effective* number of classes. No single
decision chooses among 21,841 options. The first chooses among 27, and each later one chooses
among the children of one node.

It also addresses both extra difficulties. Mistakes made low in the tree are the acceptable
kind, since the broad category is still right. And the upper-level classifiers pool the
examples of all their descendants, so they are not starved of data even when individual
leaves are rare.

## The cost

An error at the top cannot be repaired lower down. If the first classifier says "furniture",
no second-level classifier will ever answer "husky". The taxonomy also has to exist and has
to match what the features can distinguish, which takes knowledge of the domain.

> With `k` classes, chance is `1/k`, errors differ in seriousness, and rare classes run out
> of data. A taxonomy classified from the top down replaces one enormous decision with a
> chain of small ones.
