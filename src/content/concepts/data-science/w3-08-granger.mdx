The previous concept ended with a problem: correlation cannot tell you which way an arrow
points. Time offers a partial answer. Causes come before effects, so if `Y` moves first and
`X` follows, `Y` is a candidate cause.

## The test

The lecture's definition: **`Y` "Granger causes" `X` if there is greater correlation with a
time lag.** In practice you ask whether knowing the recent past of `Y` improves your
prediction of `X`, compared with using the past of `X` alone. If it does, and the reverse is
not true, the evidence points one way.

Example: rainfall this week against river level next week. Rain predicts river level; river
level does not predict rain. That asymmetry is what the test looks for.

## What it does not say

The slide is explicit: it **says nothing about direct versus indirect causes**. Suppose a
storm system `C` first raises rainfall at one station and then, later, another.
Rainfall at the first station will Granger-cause rainfall at the second, without causing it.
The timing is real; the arrow is borrowed from a third factor.

Also note the word *predict*. Granger causality is a statement about forecasting, which is
weaker than the everyday meaning of "causes". It is a stronger clue than a plain correlation,
not a substitute for an experiment.

## A footnote

The slide asks whether his causality test won Granger the Nobel Prize. It did not: Clive
Granger shared the 2003 economics prize for a related idea, cointegration, which handles
series that wander but stay tied together over time.

> Granger causality upgrades "moves together" to "moves first". That is a better clue, and
> still not a proof.
