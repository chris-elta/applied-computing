Two cleaning problems come from how values are *stored*, not from what they mean.

## Numbers

The lecture's example: the **Ariane 5 rocket exploded in 1996 due to a bad conversion from a
64-bit float to a 16-bit integer.** The value did not fit the smaller type, so the number the
software acted on was not the number it measured.

Three rules from the slide:

- **Measurements should be decimal numbers.**
- **Counts should be integers.**
- **Fractional quantities should be decimal**, not (q, r) pairs like (pounds, ounces) or
  (feet, inches).

The first two make sure the type can represent the quantity. The third is for analysis: you
cannot average (5 lb, 8 oz) without first converting it to one number, so convert once, at
the start, to one unit.

## Characters

The slide calls **unifying character code representations a particularly nasty cleaning issue
in textual data.** Two encodings it names:

- **ISO 8859-1**: a single-byte code for ASCII.
- **UTF-8**: a multibyte encoding for all Unicode characters.

Text in one, read as the other, comes out as question marks or nonsense. The slide's
illustration shows Chinese, French, Thai, Japanese, German, Russian and Arabic lines intact in
one column and as rows of "?" in the other. The Simpsons joke on the slide, "I will study
encodings and properly use UTF-8," is the practical advice: **pick UTF-8, and state the
encoding whenever you read or write a file.**

> The same bytes can mean different things. Decide what each column's type and each file's
> encoding is, and convert at the door.
