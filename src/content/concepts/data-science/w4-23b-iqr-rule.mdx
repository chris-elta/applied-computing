Nine deliveries took these times, in minutes, already sorted:

| 12 | 14 | 15 | 15 | 17 | 18 | 20 | 21 | 48 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |

Is the 48 an outlier? The previous concept suggested a z-score test: flag anything more than
`k` standard deviations from the mean. Try it with `k = 3`. The mean is `180 / 9 = 20` and the
standard deviation is about 10.3, so the 48 has `z = (48 − 20) / 10.3 ≈ 2.7`. Not flagged.

The trouble is that the 48 helped set the yardstick it is measured with. Remove it, and the
other eight have mean 16.5 and standard deviation about 2.9. One extreme value dragged the mean
up and nearly quadrupled `σ`, so it hid itself. This is called **masking**.

## Measure spread with the middle of the data

The **1.5 × IQR rule** avoids this by building its yardstick from values the outlier cannot
reach.

1. **Find the quartiles.** `Q1` is the value a quarter of the way up the sorted data, `Q3`
   three quarters. With nine values, using the convention NumPy and pandas use by default,
   `Q1` is the 3rd value and `Q3` the 7th: `Q1 = 15`, `Q3 = 20`. Software offers several
   conventions, and they can disagree slightly on small datasets; say which you used.
2. **Take the interquartile range.** `IQR = Q3 − Q1 = 20 − 15 = 5`, the width of the middle
   half.
3. **Set the fences** one and a half IQRs beyond each quartile:

```
lower fence = Q1 − 1.5 × IQR = 15 − 7.5 = 7.5
upper fence = Q3 + 1.5 × IQR = 20 + 7.5 = 27.5
```

4. **Flag anything outside a fence.** 48 is above 27.5, so it is flagged. Nothing falls below
   7.5.

Change the 48 to 480 and the fences do not move. The quartiles depend on the order of the
values, not on how far out the extreme one sits. That is what **robust** means.

## Reading the result

- **Check each end separately.** The fences are not symmetric about the median when the data
  is skewed, so one tail can have outliers and the other none.
- **The rule draws the box plot.** The box runs from `Q1` to `Q3`, the whiskers stop at the
  furthest values inside the fences, and flagged points are drawn as dots beyond them.
- **1.5 is a convention, not a law.** For normal data the fences sit about 2.7 standard
  deviations from the mean and flag about 0.7% of points. A wider `3 × IQR` fence is
  sometimes used for "far out" values.
- **A flag is a question, not a verdict.** As in the previous concept, find out *why* a point is
  outside the fence before deciding whether to fix it, keep it or delete it. In a skewed
  variable such as income, many real values will be flagged.

> Judge a point against a yardstick it cannot bend. The quartiles ignore how extreme the
> extremes are, which is why fences built from them catch outliers that z-scores miss.
