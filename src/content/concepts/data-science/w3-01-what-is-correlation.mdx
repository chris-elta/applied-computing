Suppose you know a person's height and nothing else. Can you say something useful about
their weight? Taller people tend to weigh more, so yes, a little. That is the whole idea of
correlation.

## The definition

The lecture puts it this way: two factors are **correlated when the value of `x` has some
predictive power on the value of `y`**. The **correlation coefficient** measures the degree
to which `Y` is a function of `X`, and, symmetrically, `X` of `Y`. Nothing in the definition
prefers one variable over the other.

The coefficient runs on a fixed scale:

| `r` | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | fully correlated: knowing `x` fixes `y` exactly, and they rise together |
| 0 | uncorrelated: knowing `x` gives no linear help with `y` |
| −1 | anti-correlated: knowing `x` fixes `y` exactly, and one falls as the other rises |

Two habits to build early:

- **The sign is direction, the size is strength.** Both `+0.9` and `−0.9` are strong. They
  differ only in which way the cloud of points tilts.
- **Zero means no *linear* relationship.** Points arranged in a perfect U-shape can have
  `r = 0`, because the rise on one side cancels the fall on the other. The next concepts show
  why.

## Why bother

A single number lets you compare relationships that live in different units: height against
weight, study hours against grades. You can rank a hundred candidate features by how much
each tells you about the thing you want to predict, before building any model.

> Correlation asks one question: how much does knowing one value help you guess the other?
> The answer is a number from −1 to 1.
