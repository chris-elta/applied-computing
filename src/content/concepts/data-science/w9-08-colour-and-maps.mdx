Colour is the most tempting channel in a chart and the easiest to get wrong, because people
read some colour properties as *amounts* and others only as *labels*.

## What is seen as ordered

Slide 33 shows three ramps:

| property | perceived as ordered? | use it for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| brightness (light to dark) | yes | amounts |
| saturation (grey to vivid) | yes | amounts |
| hue (red, yellow, green, blue…) | not as much | categories |

Show someone five greys and they can sort them. Show them red, yellow, green, blue and purple
and there is no natural order: hue says *different*, not *more*.

## Rainbows

The rainbow colour map is the classic mistake. Slide 34 says it plainly: "rainbows are
perceptually non-linear". Equal steps in the data do not look like equal steps in colour.
The yellow band looks much brighter than its neighbours, so it reads as a sharp boundary that
is not in the data, while wide stretches of green look uniform.

The better choices:

- **sequential**: one hue getting lighter or darker, for data from low to high;
- **diverging**: "distinct positive/negative colours reflected about a center", for data with
  a meaningful midpoint, such as change from zero.

Slide 35 compares two colourings of the same gene-expression chart: when one category shares
a similar colour with its neighbour, the reader loses the boundary. Pick colours that are
distinct when they must be told apart, and ordered when they encode an amount.

## Maps and cartograms

A map is a chart whose position encodes geography. That raises a problem: area on a map
tracks land, not people. Slide 32 shows the 2012 US election. On the ordinary map, large
sparse states dominate the picture. A **cartogram** "distorts regions to reflect an
underlying variable", here electoral votes, so each state's area matches its weight.

The cartogram is a lie-factor fix: the ordinary map makes area mean something the reader
did not ask about.

> Use brightness or saturation for amounts and hue for categories. Avoid rainbows. Use a
> diverging scale when the data has a centre, and remember that on a map, area means land
> unless you make it mean something else.
