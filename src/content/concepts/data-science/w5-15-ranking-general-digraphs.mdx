The topological sort needed results that never contradict each other. A real season is not
like that. A beats B, B beats C, and then C beats A. Follow the arrows and you return to
where you started. That is a cycle, and with a cycle no line-up puts every arrow pointing
forward. Wherever you place those three teams, at least one result will say the lower team
beat the higher one.

## A new goal

Since perfection is impossible, look for the least bad order. The slide: **for general
directed graphs, we seek the order minimizing the number of "wrong way" edges.** A wrong-way
edge is a result your ranking contradicts, where the lower-ranked team won. Fewer of them
means a ranking that agrees with more of the evidence.

Put another way: find the smallest set of results to disregard so that the remainder is
consistent, then topologically sort what is left.

## The exact answer is too expensive

The slide gives the bad news in one line: **cutting the minimum number of edges to leave a
DAG is NP-complete.** You do not need the formal definition here. The practical meaning is
that no efficient method is known that always finds the best answer. You could try every
possible order, but ten teams already have more than three million orders and the number
explodes from there.

## A heuristic

The slide: **reasonable heuristics start by sorting by the difference between in/out
degree.**

Count each vertex's arrows. With arrows drawn from winner to loser:

- **out-degree** = arrows leaving = wins
- **in-degree** = arrows arriving = losses
- **score** = out-degree − in-degree

| player | wins (out-degree) | losses (in-degree) | score |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | 4 | 1 | +3 |
| B | 3 | 2 | +1 |
| C | 2 | 3 | −1 |
| D | 1 | 4 | −3 |

Sort by score: A, B, C, D. A player who beats many and loses to few goes near the top. That
tends to make most arrows point forward, which was the goal.

## What it gives up

This score is wins minus losses, which is close to the vote counting the lecture warned
about. It treats every opponent as equal, so a schedule full of weak opponents still
inflates a score. The slide says heuristics *start* here. It is a quick, explainable first
ordering, and you can refine it afterwards, for example by swapping neighbours when a swap
reduces the wrong-way count.

You have now seen the same data handled two ways. A DAG gets an exact answer by topological
sort. A graph with cycles gets an approximate answer by a degree count. The first job with
real comparison data is therefore to check for cycles.

> When results contradict each other, no ranking agrees with all of them. Aim for the fewest
> wrong-way results, and accept a good heuristic, because the exact optimum is too costly.
