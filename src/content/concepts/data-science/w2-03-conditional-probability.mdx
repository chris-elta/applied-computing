You roll two dice and are told the first is a 6. How likely is it that the total is 10 or
more? The answer changed the moment you were told something, because you are no longer asking
about all rolls, only those that start with a 6.

## The definition

The **conditional probability** of `A` given `B` is

```
P(A|B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B)
```

Read the fraction as a share. Of everything that can happen when `B` occurs (the bottom),
what portion also has `A` (the top)? Conditioning shrinks the world to the cases where `B`
happened and asks how much of that smaller world is `A`.

The dice example: given a 6 first, the total is at least 10 if the second die is 4, 5 or 6.
That is 3 of the 6 equally likely second rolls, so `P = 3/6 = 0.5`. Without the information,
the probability of a total of 10 or more is only `6/36`.

## When it does nothing

The slide notes that conditional probabilities get interesting **only when events are not
independent**. If they are, `P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B)`, and the definition collapses:

```
P(A|B) = P(A) × P(B) / P(B) = P(A)
```

Learning `B` leaves the probability of `A` where it was. This is the same fact as the
definition of independence, seen from the other side: independent means being told about
`B` is useless.

## Order matters

`P(A|B)` and `P(B|A)` are generally **different numbers**. The chance that a professional
footballer is fit is not the chance that a fit person is a professional footballer. The next
concept, Bayes' theorem, is exactly the rule that converts one into the other.

> Conditioning restricts attention to the cases where you know something happened. The
> bar means "given", and the two orders are different questions.
