The slide's example: **University A ranks 111th of 351 teams, RPI = 39.18.** Those are two
descriptions of one fact. Which one told you something?

For most people, the rank. You do not know whether 39.18 is good, but 111th of 351 places
the team in the top third straight away. Once you have a score you can always sort it into a
ranking, so you have to choose which to show. The slide gives three questions for choosing.

## 1. Will the numbers be presented in isolation?

A score alone needs the reader to know the scale. A rank carries its own context, as long as
you say out of how many. If the audience has never seen the measure before, the rank reads
better.

## 2. What is the distribution of scores?

The slide asks: **how much better is #1 than #2?** A ranking cannot say. First and second
might be separated by a hair or by a wide gap, and both cases appear as "1, 2".

| team | score | rank |
| --- | --- | --- |
| P | 98.0 | 1 |
| Q | 71.2 | 2 |
| R | 71.1 | 3 |
| S | 70.9 | 4 |

The rank column says the teams are evenly spaced. The score column says one team is far
ahead and three are tied for practical purposes. A ranking keeps the order and discards the
size of the gaps.

## 3. Do you care about the middle or the extremes?

**Small changes in score can cause big rank differences.** This happens in the middle of a
distribution, where items bunch together. If dozens of teams sit between 39.0 and 39.5, a
change of 0.2, which may be nothing more than noise, moves a team past many others. At the
extremes items are sparse, and rank is stable.

So rankings are most trustworthy at the top and bottom and least trustworthy in the crowded
middle. A headline such as "the university fell 20 places" may describe a change in score
too small to mean anything.

## Choosing

| situation | prefer |
| --- | --- |
| Audience does not know the scale | Rank, with "of how many" |
| The size of the gap matters | Score |
| Items bunched in the middle | Score, since ranks exaggerate there |
| Only the winner matters | Rank |

Reporting both, as the slide's example does, is often the best answer.

> A rank tells you the order and hides the gaps. A score shows the gaps and needs a scale to
> be read.
