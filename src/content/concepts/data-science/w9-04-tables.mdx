Not every data set wants a chart. The lecture opens its tour of chart types with the one
people forget: the table.

## What a table does better

From slide 16, tables have four advantages over plots:

| advantage | why |
| --- | --- |
| numerical precision | a reader can see 99.6, not "about 100" |
| understandable multivariate display | each column is another dimension, with no encoding to learn |
| heterogeneous data | names, counts, rates and money can sit side by side |
| compactness for few points | a dozen numbers fit in less space than a chart of them |

If the reader needs exact values, or there are only a handful of points, a table is often the
better choice.

## Making a table readable

Slide 17 shows a raw table of countries with area, density, birth rate, population,
mortality and GDP. Every number is there, but nothing invites comparison. Slide 18 lists what
to fix:

- **Order rows to invite comparisons.** Alphabetical order is rarely what the reader wants;
  sort by the column that matters.
- **Order columns** to highlight importance or to put related pairs side by side.
- **Right-justify uniform-precision numbers.** Then digits line up by place value and a
  number's length shows its size.
- **Use emphasis, font or colour** for the few entries that matter.
- **Avoid excessive-length column descriptors.**

Slide 19 applies them. Thousands separators and a fixed number of decimal places make the
population column scannable, and the eye can now see that two countries are an order of
magnitude bigger than the rest without reading a digit.

## The general point

A table is a visualisation too, and the same aesthetic applies: remove what does not help,
arrange what remains so the comparison the reader wants is the easy one. Right-justification
is the table's version of a zero baseline: it makes length mean size.

> Use a table when precision matters or there are few values. Then sort it, align it, and fix
> its precision so the comparison the reader wants needs no effort.
