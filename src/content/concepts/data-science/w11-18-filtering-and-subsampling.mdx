The last concept said a simple model cannot use a billion records. Slides 8 and 9 turn that
into advice: with big data, one of your most useful options is to **throw much of it away**.
There are two different reasons to discard a record.

## Filtering: removing what you do not want

Slide 8 calls this an important benefit of big data: you can discard much of it to make
analysis cleaner.

The slide's example: English accounts for only 34% of all tweets on Twitter, but you can
exclude the rest and still have enough for meaningful analysis. If your method understands
English text, tweets in other languages are noise to it. With a small data set, dropping 66%
would be ruinous. With 500 million tweets a day, the remaining third is about 170 million a
day.

Filtering is a decision about what is relevant, and the slide says it **requires
application-specific knowledge**. Someone has to know that language matters for this
analysis, that machine-generated accounts should go, that a particular field is unreliable
before a certain date. A filter changes which population your conclusions describe.
Excluding non-English tweets means your findings are about English-language tweeters, and
you should say so.

## Subsampling: keeping less of what is good

Subsampling is different. The records are all relevant, and you keep a fraction anyway.
Slide 9 gives three reasons it can pay.

| reason | why it helps |
| --- | --- |
| Cleanly separate training, testing and evaluation data | with data to spare, you can set aside distinct portions that never mix |
| Simple, robust models have few parameters | big data is overkill for them, so a sample trains the same model in a fraction of the time |
| Spreadsheet-sized data sets are fast and easy to explore | you can look at the rows, plot them and try an idea in seconds |

The third is easy to undervalue. Exploration depends on quick feedback: try something,
look, try something else. If every question takes an hour on a cluster you will ask few
questions. A sample that fits in a spreadsheet lets you ask hundreds, and once you know which
are worth asking you can run those on the full data.

## The catch

Both operations assume that what you keep still looks like what you had, or like what you
meant to study. A filter can quietly remove a group you needed. A subsample can
over-represent one corner of the data. How you choose the records matters as much as how
many you keep, and the next three concepts are about how to choose.

> With big data you can afford to discard. Filter out what is irrelevant, using knowledge
> of the application. Subsample what is good, to separate your data cleanly and work
> quickly.
