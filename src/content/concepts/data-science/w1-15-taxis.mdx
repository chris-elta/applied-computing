The last dataset is **NYC taxi cab data**. It gives the **driver or owner, pickup and dropoff
location, and fare** for every taxi trip taken. It was obtained from New York City through a
Freedom of Information Act request.

That last detail is worth noticing. Interesting data is often not sitting in a public
download; somebody had to ask for it. The lecture earlier called such datasets a **scarce
resource** that takes hard work and imagination to obtain.

## The lecturer's questions

- How much do drivers make each night?
- How far do they travel?
- How much slower is traffic during rush hour?
- Where are people travelling to and from at different times of the day?
- Do faster drivers get tipped better?
- Where should drivers go to pick up their next fare?

## Sorting them by what is needed

| Question | Straight from the record? |
| --- | --- |
| Where do people travel, by time of day | Yes — locations and times |
| How much drivers make each night | Nearly — sum the fares by driver and night, though costs are not there |
| How far they travel | Needs distance worked out from locations |
| Traffic slower at rush hour | Inferred from trip length against time taken |
| Faster drivers tipped better | Needs a measure of "faster" and needs tips recorded |
| Where to pick up next | A recommendation — needs a model built on the rest |

The bottom of the list is where data science begins: the record does not contain the answer,
and you must construct it, then decide how far to trust what you built.

> Some questions are lookups. The interesting ones are built out of the data, and the
> building is where you can go wrong.
