The model outputs a probability. To act on it you need a yes or a no, so pick a cut-off:
predict `y = 1` when `hθ(x) ≥ 0.5` and `y = 0` otherwise. Slide 9 asks what that rule means
in terms of the inputs.

## From a probability to a sign

Look at the sigmoid curve. `g(z) ≥ 0.5` happens exactly when `z ≥ 0`: the curve crosses 0.5
at `z = 0` and only goes up from there. Since `z = θᵀx`:

| the model predicts | when | which means |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `y = 1` | `hθ(x) ≥ 0.5` | `θᵀx ≥ 0` |
| `y = 0` | `hθ(x) < 0.5` | `θᵀx < 0` |

The exponential has dropped out. To classify a point you compute the weighted sum and check
its sign.

## The example on slide 10

Two features, so `hθ(x) = g(θ₀ + θ₁x₁ + θ₂x₂)`. Suppose training has produced `θ₀ = −3`,
`θ₁ = 1`, `θ₂ = 1`. Then the model predicts `y = 1` if

```
−3 + x₁ + x₂ ≥ 0      that is,      x₁ + x₂ ≥ 3
```

The equation `x₁ + x₂ = 3` is a straight line through `(3, 0)` and `(0, 3)`. Every point on
or above it is predicted positive, and every point below it negative. On the slide the red
crosses sit above the line and the blue circles below.

That line is the **decision boundary**: the set of points where `θᵀx = 0`, so `hθ(x)` is
exactly 0.5 and the model is undecided.

Check two points:

| point | `−3 + x₁ + x₂` | prediction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `(1, 1)` | −1 | `y = 0` |
| `(2, 2)` | 1 | `y = 1` |

## What the boundary belongs to

The boundary is a property of the **parameters**, not of the data. The training set is used
to choose θ. Once θ is chosen, you could delete every training example and the line would
stay where it is.

Distance from the boundary still carries information. A point far on the positive side has a
large `θᵀx` and a probability near 1. A point just across it has a probability near 0.5. The
yes-or-no answer hides that difference, so keep the probability when confidence matters.

> Thresholding the sigmoid at 0.5 is the same as checking the sign of `θᵀx`. The points
> where `θᵀx = 0` form the decision boundary, and it is fixed by θ alone.
