Two further biases from the same slide. These live in the people doing the analysis rather
than in the sampling.

## Confirmation bias

**People stop sampling or analysing when the results confirm expectation.** The mechanism is
a stopping rule. If a surprising result prompts another look and an expected one does not,
errors that happen to agree with you are never found.

The previous section's PubMed example shows the defence. The authors stated what the plot
should look like first, so a mismatch was visible. A result that matched without checking
could still have been an artifact.

## Dunning-Kruger effect

**Poor performers overestimate their level of performance.** The slide's plot has competence
group on the horizontal axis and score on the vertical, with two lines: actual and perceived.
At low competence the perceived line sits well above the actual one. They converge as
competence rises.

For data work the practical reading is about *you*. When you are new to a field, your
confidence in an analysis is least reliable, because you do not yet know what can go wrong.
Get a second reader, and be more suspicious when the work seems easy.

| Bias | Where it acts | Defence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Confirmation | When you stop looking | Check expected results as hard as surprising ones |
| Dunning-Kruger | In your estimate of your own skill | Outside review |

> Your own certainty is not evidence. Build checks that do not depend on how sure you feel.
