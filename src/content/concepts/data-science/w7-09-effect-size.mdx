The speaker notes ask a question meant to sound silly: **who has more fingers, men or women?**
Almost everyone has ten, but not quite everyone, so the two groups' averages will differ by a
sliver. With millions of records, even that sliver could come out highly significant. But the notes add the
question that matters: *is the difference large enough that I should care about it?*

## Two different questions

The slide states it directly: **for large enough sample sizes, extremely small differences can
register as highly significant.** Significance measures *confidence that there is a
difference*, not the **effect size**, the importance or magnitude of that difference.

You saw why in the t-test. Its denominator shrinks as the samples grow. Hold the gap between
two means fixed, collect more data, and `t` keeps rising until any gap, however small, clears
the bar.

| question | answered by |
| --- | --- |
| Is there a difference at all? | significance (p-value) |
| How big is the difference? | effect size |

## Measuring effect size

Slide 20 gives three measures:

| measure | small | medium | large |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cohen's `d` | > 0.2 | > 0.5 | > 0.8 |
| overlap between the two distributions | ≈ 85% | ≈ 67% | ≈ 53% |
| Pearson correlation coefficient | 0.2 | 0.5 | 0.8 |

**Cohen's d** is the easiest to read. From the slide, `d = |μ − μ′| / σ`: it measures the gap between means in units of standard
deviation, so it does not depend on the sample size or the units. From the notes, `d = 0.8`
means the means differ by almost a full standard deviation, a large effect that you can see by
eye in the data, as with men's and women's heights.

The **overlap** row says the same thing as a picture. At a small effect the two bells mostly
sit on top of each other. At a large effect they are visibly separate, but still about half
overlapping.

The correlation row uses the lecturer's thresholds. Other sources often use lower cut-offs for
correlations specifically, around 0.1, 0.3 and 0.5. Either way, the scale runs from small to
large and is judged on its own, not by the p-value.

## A worked contrast

Two teaching methods average 82 and 80, with standard deviation 10.

- `d = (82 − 80) / 10 = 0.2`, a small effect.
- With 10,000 students per group, Welch's t is `2 / √(100/10000 + 100/10000) = 2 / 0.141 ≈ 14`.
  That is enormously significant.

Both statements are true together. The difference is certainly real, and it is small. Whether
it is worth retraining every teacher is a judgement about cost, and effect size is the input to
that judgement that a p-value cannot give.

> Significance tells you a difference exists; effect size tells you how big it is. Large samples
> make everything significant, so report both.
