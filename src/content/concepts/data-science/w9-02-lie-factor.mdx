A chart makes a visual claim: *this one is that much bigger than that one*. The **lie factor**
measures whether the claim matches the data.

```
lie factor = size of effect in graphic / size of effect in data
```

A value of 1 is honest. The lecture names three ways to drift away from it.

## 1 · Dimensionality

Slide 5 shows pictograms: a doctor whose height tracks a number, and oil barrels sized by
price. The trouble is that a reader perceives *area* or *volume*, not height. If a number
doubles and you double the barrel's height, its width doubles too, so its area goes up four
times and its volume eight times.

> Fixing a two- or three-dimensional representation by a single parameter yields a lie,
> because area or volume increase non-proportionally to length.

Use a bar, which only varies in one dimension, or scale the area, not the length.

## 2 · Scale distortion

Slide 6 shows a news graphic of the top tax rate rising from 35% to 39.6%. With its axis
starting at 34%, the second bar towers over the first. Redrawn from zero, the two bars are
close. The slide's rules:

- **always start bar graphs at zero**, because a bar's length is the value;
- **always label your axes**;
- **use continuous scales**, linear or clearly labelled (a log scale is fine if it says so).

## 3 · Aspect ratio

Slide 7 plots one series three times. Stretched tall and narrow, a dip looks like a cliff;
squashed wide and short, it looks like nothing. The steepness of a slope is a property of the
chart's shape, not the data. The lecture's guidance is to aim for lines near **45°**, or a
golden-ratio frame, which is the shape at which slopes are easiest to compare.

## The common thread

Each case is a choice the reader cannot see. They assume a bar starts at zero, that a
barrel's size tracks the number, that a steep line is a big change. When those assumptions
fail, the chart lies even though every label on it is correct.

> Readers judge by length, area and slope. A chart is honest when those match the data, which
> means zero baselines for bars, one-dimensional scaling, and an aspect ratio that does not
> invent cliffs.
