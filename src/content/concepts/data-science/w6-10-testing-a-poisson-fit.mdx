A distribution with the right mean can still be the wrong distribution. The lecture tests
the Poisson on two real quantities, both with known averages, and gets one near-success and
one clear warning.

## Kids per family

The slide asks: **the average U.S. family has 2.2 kids, but how are they distributed?** Its
argument for a Poisson: **if families repeatedly decide whether to have any more children
with fixed probability `p`**, the count follows a Poisson.

With `μ = 2.2`:

| children | `P(X = x)` | share of families |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | 0.11 | 11% |
| 1 | 0.24 | 24% |
| 2 | 0.26 | 26% |
| 3 | 0.19 | 19% |
| 4 | 0.10 | 10% |
| 5+ | ≈ 0.07 | about 7% |

You can check the first row by hand: `e⁻²·² ≈ 0.111`. The slide's chart draws these
predictions as a line, with real data as dots. The overall shape is right: a peak at 2 and a
quick fall after it. The clearest miss is one child. The Poisson predicts about 24% of
families, and the data shows about half that. Families planning a sibling for their first
child are not tossing a coin after each birth, so "fixed probability" is only an
approximation.

## Human lifespans

The other slide: **from an average lifespan of 80, the Poisson distribution looks kind of
reasonable.** A Poisson with `μ = 80` is a neat bell around 80.

Then the question that undoes it: **but is the probability of dying each day constant over
all ages?**

It is not. The slide's lower chart shows the real distribution of ages at death: lopsided,
with a long tail to the left, a peak in the 80s, and a small spike at age 1 from infant
deaths. The Poisson cannot produce that shape.
Its spread is `√80 ≈ 9` years, so it predicts that hardly anyone dies before 60. In reality,
risk is low for decades and then climbs steeply with age, and that is a different model.

The same objection applied to the geometric lightbulb model earlier in this section. Both
assume the chance of the event never changes. For bulbs that is plausible. For people it is
not.

## How to judge a fit

Both examples follow the same steps:

1. **Write down the mechanism the distribution assumes.** For the Poisson: independent
   events at a constant rate.
2. **Ask if the real process works that way.** Constant risk of death at every age? No.
3. **Compare the predicted shape with data,** not only the mean. Any model can match a
   mean, because the mean is what you plug in.

> Matching the mean is free; matching the shape is the test. Check whether the
> distribution's assumption, a constant rate for the Poisson, describes how the data was
> actually produced.
