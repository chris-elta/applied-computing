Plot US city populations from largest to smallest on ordinary axes. New York sits in the
top-left corner and the rest fall into a flat line along the bottom. The slide notes that
**the biggest values are out of scale on linear plots.** You can see that the data is
skewed, but not what shape it has.

Put both axes on a log scale and the same data becomes a nearly straight line. As the slide
puts it, **straight lines on log-log plots say power law.**

## Why straight

Take logs of both sides of the power law:

```
P(x)      = c · x⁻ᵃ
log P(x)  = log c − a · log x
```

That is the equation of a line, `y = intercept + slope × x`, where

| line | power law |
| --- | --- |
| horizontal axis | `log x` |
| vertical axis | `log P(x)` |
| slope | `−a` |
| intercept | `log c` |

The log turns the power into a multiplication, which is the Week 3 idea that logs turn
products into sums. So the plot is a test and a measuring tool at once. If the points fall on
a line the data is power-law-like, and the line's slope gives the exponent.

A normal distribution fails this test badly. On log-log axes it curves steeply downward,
because its probabilities collapse faster than any power once you are a few `σ` out.

## They are everywhere

The slide lists a few of the many quantities that follow power laws:

- internet sites with `x` incoming links
- frequency of earthquakes at `x` on the Richter scale
- words used with a relative frequency of `x`
- wars which kill `x` people

The grid of twelve charts on the slide shows each data set as an approximate straight line
on **log value, log frequency** plots. They are not perfectly straight. Several bend at the
ends, where data runs thin. The pattern is clear even so.

## A caution

A straight-ish line on a log-log plot is evidence, not proof. Log axes compress a lot of
variation, and several other skewed distributions can look nearly straight over a limited
range. This is the warning from the start of the week again: a similar shape does not prove a
distribution. But a log-log plot is the quickest first check, and a line that is clearly
curved rules a power law out.

> To check for a power law, plot log frequency against log value. A straight line suggests
> one, and its slope is `−a`.
