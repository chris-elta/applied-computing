The third philosophy on slide 56 is a forecaster's, taken from Nate Silver's *The Signal and
the Noise*. Slide 60 lists four principles.

## 1 · Think probabilistically

A model should output a **probability distribution**, not a single number. The lecture
notes' example: instead of "the movie will gross $97 million", say
`P(gross is 90–100M) = 0.45`.

The point forecast pretends to certainty it does not have. The distribution says how
confident you are, and lets a decision-maker plan for the bad case as well as the likely one.

## 2 · Change your forecast in response to new information

A model is **live** if it continually updates its predictions as new information arrives
(slide 61). Three questions test a live model:

- Does the forecast ultimately converge on the right answer?
- Does it display past forecasts, so users can judge its consistency?
- Does the model retrain on fresher data?

Slide 62 shows a 2016 US presidential forecast: the probability of each candidate winning,
plotted day by day, moving as polls arrived.

## 3 · Look for consensus

Slide 63 asks: are there competing forecasts to compare with, such as prediction markets?
What do your **baseline models** say? Do you have several models that use different
approaches? If your model disagrees with all of them, that needs explaining before you
trust it. **Boosting**, a machine-learning technique, explicitly combines an ensemble of
classifiers for the same reason.

## 4 · Employ Bayesian reasoning

Bayes' theorem, from week 2, is the formal way to update a belief on new evidence:

```
P(A | B) = P(B | A) × P(A) / P(B)
```

`P(A)` is the **prior**: what you believed before. `P(B | A)` is how likely the new evidence
is if `A` is true. The result, `P(A | B)`, is the **posterior**: your updated belief. The
lecture notes' example is updating the probability a candidate will win when a new poll
comes in. Principles 1 and 2 are this rule applied habitually.

## A failure: Google Flu Trends

Slide 64: Google Flu Trends predicted flu outbreaks from the frequency of illness-related
searches. It worked, until Google added search suggestions. Suggestions changed what people
typed, so searches no longer tracked illness, and the model failed. It had a correlation and
no theory of why it held, and nothing checking it against what actually happened.

> Forecast in probabilities, update as evidence arrives, compare with other models, and keep
> checking against reality. A correlation that worked yesterday is not guaranteed tomorrow.
