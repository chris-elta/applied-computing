A baseball player gets a hit 30% of the time. In a game they come to bat 5 times. How likely
are exactly 2 hits?

Each at-bat is a yes-or-no **trial**. The slide sets out what a binomial experiment needs:
**`n` identical, independent trials, which have two possible outcomes, with probabilities
`p` and `1−p`**, like heads or tails. Here `n = 5` and `p = 0.3`.

## Building the formula

Take one particular way of getting 2 hits: hit, hit, out, out, out. Because the at-bats are
independent, multiply:

```
0.3 × 0.3 × 0.7 × 0.7 × 0.7 = 0.3² × 0.7³ = 0.03087
```

Any other order with 2 hits, such as out, hit, out, out, hit, has the same probability,
because it multiplies the same numbers in a different order. So the answer is that
probability times **the number of orders**. Choosing which 2 of the 5 at-bats are the hits
can be done in 5 choose 2 = 10 ways.

```
P{X = 2} = 10 × 0.03087 ≈ 0.309
```

That is the whole formula from the slide:

```
P{X = x} = (n choose x) · pˣ · (1 − p)ⁿ⁻ˣ
```

The slide's table names each piece:

| piece | meaning |
| --- | --- |
| `(n choose x)` | number of ways to choose `x` successes out of `n` trials |
| `pˣ` | probability of getting the `x` successes |
| `(1 − p)ⁿ⁻ˣ` | probability of getting the remaining `n − x` failures |

## The batting average example

The slide's blue line: **the observed season batting averages of a `p = 0.300` hitter were
drawn from a binomial distribution.** The player's true ability is fixed at 0.300, but in a
season of a few hundred at-bats the number of hits varies with luck. One season they bat
.320, the next .285. Neither number is the player's real ability. Both are draws from the
binomial with that `p`.

## The assumptions do the work

Both conditions matter. If the trials are **not independent**, as when a hitter in a slump
keeps missing, multiplying the probabilities is no longer allowed. If they are **not
identical**, as with a different pitcher each time, there is no single `p`. The formula is
exact only when the setup really matches it. That is the warning from the previous concept
applied to a particular distribution.

> Binomial: count the successes in `n` independent, identical yes-or-no trials. Multiply
> the probability of one such sequence by the number of orders it can come in.
