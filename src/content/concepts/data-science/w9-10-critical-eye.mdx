The last stretch of the visualisation lecture is a gallery of failures. Each one breaks a
principle from earlier in the week.

## Power laws need log plots

Slide 42 is a student plot of word frequency against rank. On ordinary axes, the most common
words make a spike at the left and every other word is squashed against zero. Nearly all the
data is invisible.

Slide 43 fixes it in two steps:

1. **log the y-axis** and the frequencies spread out into a visible curve;
2. **log both axes** and the curve becomes a straight line.

That straight line is how you recognise a power law, which week 6 introduced. A log-log plot
"can be even more revealing" because it turns the defining property of the distribution into
the simplest shape there is.

## The anti-rules

Slide 44 states the rules for a terrible professional visualisation, ironically:

- display as little information as possible;
- obscure your data with chartjunk, like pseudo-3D and excess colour;
- use poorly chosen scales.

The examples that follow (from wtfviz.net) map onto the week's principles:

| slide | failure | principle broken |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 45 | a scatter with a 1.4% correlation presented as if it showed a relationship | the data must support the story |
| 46 | twenty bars, each a different colour, with a legend to match | uninformative colouring is chartjunk |
| 47 | circles sized with no regard to the percentages printed in them | lie factor, dimensionality |
| 48 | elaborate decorated charts | chartjunk |
| 49 | missing or misleading labels | clear labelling |
| 50 | far too many details for one figure | data-ink and focus |
| 51 | sizes and ordering that imply a ranking not in the data | readers assume order and size mean something |

## Keep a critical eye

Slide 52 repeats Tufte's four principles. They work in both directions: for designing your
own charts, and for reading other people's. When a chart surprises you, ask whether the
surprise is in the data or in the drawing: check the baseline, the scales, the area and the
labels before believing it.

> Most bad charts break one of four rules: too little data ink, a lie factor far from 1,
> chartjunk, or poor scales and labels. For skewed data, the right scale is often a log scale.
