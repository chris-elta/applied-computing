The mean says where the data is centred. **Standard deviation** (SD) says how spread out it
is around that mean.

## How it is built

Take each value's distance from the mean, square it so that distances above and below do not
cancel, average those squares, and take the square root:

```
σ = sqrt( average of (x − mean)² )
```

The average of the squared distances is the **variance**, `σ²`. The standard deviation is
its square root.

One correction to the slide, which says variance is "the square root of the standard
deviation". It is the other way round: **the standard deviation is the square root of the
variance**, and the variance is the square of the standard deviation. The square root brings
the result back into the units of the data, which is why SD is the number you quote.

## A small example

Values 2, 4, 4, 4, 5, 5, 7, 9 have mean 5. The squared distances are 9, 1, 1, 1, 0, 0, 4, 16,
which sum to 32. Dividing by `n = 8` gives variance 4 and SD 2. A typical value lies about 2
from the mean.

## Divide by n or n − 1?

The slide asks a question that puzzles most people the first time. The rule:

- The **population** SD divides by `n`: you have every value.
- The **sample** SD divides by `n − 1`: you have only some, and want to estimate the whole.

The reason is that a sample tends to sit closer to its own mean than to the true mean, so
dividing by `n` understates the spread. Using `n − 1` corrects for it. But for large `n`,
`n ≈ n − 1`, and the choice **does not really matter**.

> SD is the typical distance from the mean, in the data's own units. Variance is that
> distance squared.
