A stock doubles, then halves. You are back where you started. Yet the usual way of stating
the changes makes them look unequal.

## The problem with ratios

Take `new_price / old_price`. Going from 100 to 200, the ratio is 2. Going from 200 to 100,
it is 0.5. Expressed as percentages:

| Move | Ratio | Percentage change |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 100 → 200 | 2 | +100% |
| 200 → 100 | 0.5 | −50% |

The lecture puts it as: 200/100 is far above baseline, while 100/200 is only half way below,
despite being similar changes. Increases stretch upward without limit, while decreases are
squeezed into the range 0 to 1. Averaging or plotting such numbers gives a lopsided picture.

## Take the log

Now compute `log₂` of each ratio:

```
log₂(200/100) = log₂(2)   =  1
log₂(100/200) = log₂(0.5) = −1
```

The slide's point: taking the log **yields equal displacement, 1.0 and −1.0**. Doubling and
halving are mirror images, as they should be, and no change (a ratio of 1) sits at 0.

## Why this matters for plots

**Always plot logarithms of ratios**, as the slide insists. On a plot of raw ratios, a
tenfold rise takes up nine units of vertical space while a tenfold fall takes under one, so
the decreases become a pile-up near zero. On a log scale a tenfold rise and a tenfold fall
sit equally far above and below zero, so both are readable.

Averages behave better too. If a quantity doubles and then halves, the average ratio is 1.25,
which suggests growth that never happened. The average of the log ratios is 0, which is right.

The choice of base is cosmetic: base 2 counts doublings, base 10 counts orders of magnitude.

> When comparing before and after by a ratio, take the log. Growth and decline then become
> the same size in opposite directions.
