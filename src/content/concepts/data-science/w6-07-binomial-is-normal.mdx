The binomial formula gives exact probabilities, but it gets clumsy. The chance of 60 or more
heads in 100 flips needs 41 terms, each with a large "choose" coefficient. The central limit
theorem gives a shortcut.

## A count is a sum

The slide's argument: **the number of heads in `n` tosses is the sum of `n` independent
samples of a 0-1 random variable with mean `p` and `σ = √(p(1−p))`.**

Write each flip as 1 for heads and 0 for tails. Then

```
heads = flip₁ + flip₂ + … + flipₙ
```

Each flip is a tiny distribution of its own: two values, with mean `p` and standard
deviation `√(p(1−p))`. It looks nothing like a bell. But the flips are independent, and
the central limit theorem says sums and averages of independent values come out normal.
So, as the slide says, **the average number of heads is approximated by the normal
distribution.**

## Matching the parameters

Use the binomial's own mean and standard deviation:

| | formula | 100 fair flips |
| --- | --- | --- |
| mean | `np` | 50 |
| standard deviation | `√(np(1−p))` | 5 |

The slide's right-hand chart plots exactly this: `Bin(100, 0.5)` bars under a
`Normal(50, 25)` curve. The two are hard to tell apart. Its last line is **head count
Z-scores are normal**. Convert a count to a Z-score and the 68–95–99.7 rule applies.

## Using it

60 heads in 100 flips: `z = (60 − 50)/5 = 2`. About 2.5% of the time. That is a borderline
result: suspicious, but it will happen about once in every 40 honest experiments.

55 heads: `z = 1`, which happens about 16% of the time and is nothing to worry about.

## When it is poor

The approximation needs the bell to have room on both sides. For the coin, `np = 50` sits
far from 0 and from 100, so it works well. For the lightbulbs (`n = 1000`, `p = 0.001`)
the mean is 1 and the distribution is the half-bell from earlier this week, jammed against
zero. A normal centred on 1 with σ about 1 would put real probability on negative failure
counts. That situation, many trials and a rare event, is what the Poisson distribution is
for.

> A binomial count is a sum of independent 0-1 trials, so it is close to normal with mean
> `np` and spread `√(np(1−p))`, provided the mean is not crowded against 0 or `n`.
