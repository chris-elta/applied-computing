It is natural to assume that more data means better answers. Slide 5 challenges that with one
sentence: massive data sets are the result of **opportunity instead of design**.

A designed data set starts with a question. Someone decides who to measure, how and when,
then collects accordingly. A massive data set usually exists because a system was running
and recorded what passed through it. Nobody chose it to answer your question. The slide
lists four problems that follow.

## Unrepresentative participation

The people in the data are whoever used the service. Users of a social network are not a
cross-section of the population: they differ in age, country, income and how much they like
to post. This is **bias**, and it does not shrink with size. A billion records from the wrong
population answer a question about that population very precisely.

## Spam and machine-generated content

A large share of activity on any open platform is not human. Bots, advertising and automated
reposts appear in the logs looking like everything else. Count "posts mentioning a product"
and you may be counting one marketing script.

## Power laws mean redundancy

Activity is very unevenly spread. A few items (the most popular accounts, words, pages)
account for most of the records, and most items appear only a handful of times. So a huge
data set contains the same common things millions of times over and still has almost nothing
on the rare things. The size is real but much of it is repetition.

## Temporal bias

Data collected over time reflects the system at the time: its features, users and fashions.
The slide's example is **Google Flu Trends**, which estimated flu activity from what people
searched for. The model was built on the search behaviour of one period. Search behaviour
changed, and the estimates stopped matching reality even though the volume of data was
never in question.

## What to take from this

| problem | what it damages |
| --- | --- |
| unrepresentative participation | who the conclusions apply to |
| spam and machine content | whether records mean what they seem to |
| power-law redundancy | how much information the volume really holds |
| temporal bias | whether yesterday's pattern holds today |

All four are failures of **veracity**, the fourth V. None is fixed by collecting more of the
same. This is week 1's instruction to ask where the data came from, applied at a scale where
it is tempting to skip.

> Big data is found, not designed. Its size reduces random error only; bias, spam,
> redundancy and drift remain however large it grows.
