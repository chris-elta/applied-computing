A weather station logs the temperature every hour. Two things go wrong in one year:

- at **2 pm and 3 pm on 3 March** the logger skips two readings;
- from **1 to 21 July** the station is offline for repairs.

Both are missing data, and the previous concept's methods look as if they apply. They do not
apply well here. Mean imputation fills every July hour with the *annual* average, about 11°,
in the middle of summer. And methods that treat rows as independent records miss what a
time series has that a table of people does not: **neighbouring values are related.** The
autocorrelation concept in week 3 measured that: today's temperature predicts tomorrow's, and
14 July predicts next year's 14 July. Use those two relationships, and match the method to the
size of the gap.

## Short gaps: interpolate from neighbours

When a few points are missing, the readings either side pin them down closely. Draw a straight
line between them. If the known values are `x(a)` at time `a` and `x(b)` at time `b`, then for
any `t` between them:

```
x̂(t) = x(a) + (t − a) / (b − a) · (x(b) − x(a))
```

With 10° at 1 pm and 16° at 4 pm, the line rises 2° an hour, so `x̂(2 pm) = 12°` and
`x̂(3 pm) = 14°`. Over two hours the error is small, because temperature does not jump.

Interpolation assumes the series **moves smoothly**. Some series move in **steps**: a price
that changes when someone edits it, a stock count that changes at a delivery. For those,
**forward fill** is the honest choice: the value stays as it was until something changes it.

In pandas, `s.interpolate(method="time")` interpolates using the timestamps, and `s.ffill()`
forward-fills. The default `s.interpolate()` assumes the rows are evenly spaced, which is wrong
if the timestamps are not.

## Long gaps: borrow from the cycle

Across three weeks, a straight line from the 30 June reading to the 22 July reading is a smooth
ramp. It erases 21 day-night swings, and any heatwave in between. The neighbours are too far
away to say anything about the middle.

The series still has information about July, in earlier years. Build a **seasonal profile**:
for each missing hour, take the average for the same date and hour across previous years. That
keeps the shape of a typical July, including the daily cycle. Two refinements help:

- **Adjust the level.** If this June ran 1.5° warmer than past Junes, add 1.5° to the profile,
  so the fill joins the real readings at both edges.
- **Use a neighbouring series** if one exists. A station 10 km away usually tracks this one
  closely. Its July, shifted by the usual difference between the two, carries this year's
  actual weather.

| gap | method | keeps | loses |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| a few points | linear interpolation | the local trend | nothing much |
| a few points, step-like series | forward fill | the current level | the moment of any change |
| days or weeks | seasonal profile, level-adjusted | the cycle | what was unusual about this year |
| days or weeks, neighbour available | neighbouring series, shifted | this year's weather | local differences |

A seasonal fill gives *typical* weather. It cannot recover a heatwave that only the missing
weeks saw. So **record which values were imputed**, in a separate column, and leave them out of
any analysis that is about the extremes.

> In a time series the gap's neighbours are evidence. Use the nearby points when the gap is
> short, the cycle when it is long, and always keep track of which values you made up.
