You step on a scale and it says 68 kg. Is that a lot? You cannot say without knowing the
person's height: 68 kg is light for someone two metres tall and heavy for a child. Two
measurements are needed, and people want one number. That is the job of a scoring function.

## The formula

**BMI is a score designed to capture whether your weight is under control.**

```
BMI = mass / height²        (mass in kg, height in metres)
```

The slide works one case: `68.0 / 1.727² = 68.0 / 2.98 ≈ 22.8`.

The single number is then read against fixed bands.

| BMI | band |
| --- | --- |
| below 18.5 | underweight |
| 18.5 to 25 | normal |
| 25 to 30 | overweight |
| over 30 | obese |

So 22.8 is in the normal band.

## Why divide by height squared

Dividing by plain height would not be enough. A taller person is also wider and deeper, so
their mass grows faster than their height does. The slide says **mass should scale with the
square (or cube) of height.** Dividing by `height²` is the designer's choice for cancelling
that growth, so that tall and short people can be compared on one scale. The words "or cube"
show that the exponent is a decision, not a law of nature.

## Where it works and where it does not

The slide gives the case for BMI in one line: it **is easy to interpret, and correlates with
body fat.** Two numbers anyone can measure, one division, four bands.

Then it shows the limit. The two plots put each athlete at their height and weight, coloured
by band. Basketball players are tall and mostly land in normal or overweight. Football
players are shorter and heavier, and many land in overweight or obese. They are not carrying
excess fat. They carry a lot of muscle, and the formula sees only mass and height.

BMI is not body fat. It is a cheap quantity that goes up and down with body fat in most
people. For a group unlike most people, the link breaks, and the score keeps reporting
numbers as confidently as before. The lecture names this kind of stand-in a *proxy* later
this week.

> A score answers the question its formula asks, which may not be the question you care
> about. Check the population before you trust the bands.
