The first practice dataset is **Baseball-Reference.com**, a *statistical record of play*:
summary statistics for each year's batting, pitching and fielding record, with teams and
awards.

For each dataset the exercise is to ask who, what, where, when and why. Here the *who* is
players and teams, the *what* is season statistics, and the *when* is every year the record
goes back.

## Questions about baseball

The lecturer's list is about the game itself:

- How do we best measure an individual player's skill, value or performance?
- How fairly do trades between teams work out?
- What is the trajectory of a player's performance as they mature and age?
- To what extent does batting performance correlate with the position played?

## Questions about people

The same data supports questions that are not about baseball at all:

- Do left-handed people have shorter lifespans than right-handers?
- How often do people return to where they were born?
- Do player salaries reflect past, present or future performance?
- Are heights and weights increasing in the population?

## What to notice

A single dataset yields two kinds of question. The first kind is answered by the data you
have. The second uses the data as a **stand-in for a wider population**, and that stretch
needs care: professional ballplayers are not a random sample of people.

That is the sort of doubt the previous concepts on wisdom and curiosity were preparing you
for.

> A dataset's subject is the thing it describes. Its uses go well beyond that, but each
> step away raises a question about who is missing.
