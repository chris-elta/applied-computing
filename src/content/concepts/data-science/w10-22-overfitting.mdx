Week 9 introduced the bias-variance trade-off in words. Slide 38 draws it for linear regression.

## Three fits

The same house-price data, fitted three ways:

| hypothesis | fit | week 9's name |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `θ₀ + θ₁x` | a straight line that misses the curve the data clearly follows | underfit, high bias |
| `θ₀ + θ₁x + θ₂x²` | a gentle curve through the middle of the data | just right |
| `θ₀ + θ₁x + θ₂x² + θ₃x³ + θ₄x⁴` | a wiggly curve through every point | overfit, high variance |

The definition, from slide 38:

> **Overfitting**: if we have too many features, the learned hypothesis may fit the training set
> very well (J(θ) ≈ 0), but fail to generalise to new examples (predict prices on new examples).

The quartic has a near-zero cost. It is also obviously wrong: between the points it dips and
swerves in ways no housing market does. Low training cost is not the goal; predicting new houses
is.

## Why features cause it

Slide 39 lists the features you might throw at the problem: size, bedrooms, floors, age, average
neighbourhood income, kitchen size, and so on up to `x₁₀₀`. With one sample of houses, a hundred
parameters can fit almost anything, including the noise. With the polynomial features from earlier
in the week, each added power gives the curve one more bend.

The more features compared with training examples, the worse it gets. When features outnumber
examples, the normal-equation concept showed that `XᵀX` is not even invertible.

## Two remedies

Slide 40:

1. **Reduce the number of features.**
   - Manually select which features to keep.
   - Use a model-selection algorithm (later in the course).
2. **Regularisation.**
   - Keep all the features, but **reduce the magnitude** of the parameters θⱼ.
   - Works well when there are many features, each of which contributes a bit to predicting y.

Option 1 throws information away: if kitchen size really does help a little, dropping it loses
that. Option 2 keeps every feature but makes the model pay for leaning on any of them heavily. It
is Occam's razor, from week 9, written into the cost function, and it is the subject of the next
two concepts.

Either way, you can only tell whether you have overfitted by testing on data the model has not
seen: week 9's out-of-sample rule.

> A model overfits when it fits the training data almost perfectly and new data badly, usually
> because it has too many features or too much flexibility. Remove features, or keep them and shrink
> their parameters: regularisation.
