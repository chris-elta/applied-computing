IQ scores are built to average 100 with a standard deviation of 15. How rare is an IQ of
130? If IQ follows a normal distribution, the answer comes from one subtraction and one
division.

## The bell curve

The slide's opening line: **the bell-shaped distribution of height, IQ, etc.** It is
**completely parameterized by mean and standard deviation.** Once you know `μ` and `σ`
there is nothing else to choose. Every normal distribution is the same curve, shifted and
stretched.

The curve itself:

```
f(x; μ, σ²) = 1 / (√(2π) σ) · e^(−(x − μ)² / 2σ²)
```

You will rarely compute this by hand, but its parts are readable:

- `(x − μ)²` is the squared distance from the mean. The minus sign in the exponent means the
  density falls off fast as you move away, which gives the bell its shape.
- Dividing by `2σ²` measures that distance in units of `σ`. A wider bell falls off more
  slowly.
- `1/(√(2π) σ)` is a scaling constant that makes the total area exactly 1.

`f` is a **probability density**, not a probability. For a continuous variable the
probability of hitting exactly 130.000… is zero. The density says how crowded the values are
near 130, and probabilities come from areas under the curve.

## Z-scores and the rule of thumb

Because every normal is the same shape, you only need one table, measured in standard
deviations. Convert to a **Z-score**:

```
z = (x − μ) / σ
```

The slide notes that **tight bounds on probability follow for Z-scores** from normal
variables:

| region | approx. share of data |
| --- | --- |
| `μ ± 1σ` | 68% |
| `μ ± 2σ` | 95% |
| `μ ± 3σ` | 99.7% |

Compare Week 2's Chebyshev inequality, which guaranteed only 75% within 2σ for *any*
distribution. Knowing the distribution is normal buys a much tighter bound.

## The IQ example

For IQ 130: `z = (130 − 100)/15 = 2`. 95% of people are within 2σ, so 5% are outside, and by
symmetry half of that is in each tail. That is the slide's conclusion: **about 2.5% of people
have IQs above 130.**

The same reasoning gives an IQ above 115 (`z = 1`) as about 16%: 68% inside, 32% outside,
half of it above.

## A reasonable start

The slide adds a caution in red: **not all bell-shaped distributions are normal, but it is
generally a reasonable start.** The 68–95–99.7 rule is only as accurate as that assumption.
The next concept shows where it breaks.
