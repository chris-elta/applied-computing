With no right answer to test against, how do you decide whether a score is any good? You
cannot compute its error. What you can do is check its behaviour against a short list. The
slide gives five properties.

| property | what to check | example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Easily computable** | Can it be worked out from data people actually have? | BMI needs a scale, a tape measure and one division |
| **Easily understandable** | Can a user say what a higher value means? | "Higher BMI means heavier for your height" |
| **Monotonic interpretation of variables** | When one input goes up, does the score move in one consistent, sensible direction? | More mass at the same height always raises BMI |
| **Produces satisfying results on outliers** | Do extreme cases come out where common sense puts them? | The best and worst items you know should land near the top and bottom |
| **Uses systematically normalized variables** | Are the inputs on comparable scales before being combined? | z-scores, or everything rescaled to 0–1 |

## Monotonicity

Suppose a grading formula could give you a lower mark for answering one more question
correctly. You would call that a bug without needing to know the right grade. Monotonicity
turns that instinct into a test: fix all inputs but one, slide that one up, and watch the
score. If it reverses direction, either the formula is wrong or it is making a claim that
needs to be defended in the open.

## Outliers

You may not know the right score for a typical item, but you usually have firm opinions
about a few extreme ones. A ranking of basketball players that does not place the players
everyone agrees were great near the top has a problem. This is the nearest thing to
validation a score gets: you check the cases you are sure about. The football players in the
BMI plots are a case of this test failing.

## Normalization

This connects to last week. Add "years of experience" (0 to 40) to "satisfaction rating" (1
to 5) and the first variable decides almost everything, because its numbers are bigger.
Nobody chose that. Converting each variable to a z-score first puts them on the same
footing, and then any weights you apply mean what you intend. The lecture's default recipe
for a score is a linear combination of normalized values.

## What the list is for

None of the five says the score is *correct*. They say it is usable, explainable and free of
obvious faults. A score can pass all five and still stand for the wrong thing. That question
needs the idea of a gold standard, in the next section.

> You cannot prove a score right. You can check that it is computable, explainable,
> monotonic, sensible at the extremes and built from normalized inputs.
