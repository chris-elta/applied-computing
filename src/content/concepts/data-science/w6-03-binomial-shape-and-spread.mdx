Flip a fair coin 200 times, then do it again, and again. The head counts bunch around 100
and rarely stray below 80 or above 120. Now test 1,000 lightbulbs that each burn out with
probability 0.001. Most batches have 0 or 1 failures and a few have 3 or 4. Both are
binomial, but they look very different.

## Shape

The slide's description: **discrete, but bell (or half-bell) shaped.**

- *Discrete* because the count is a whole number. There is no 100.5 heads.
- **Coin flips** (`p = 0.5`, `n = 200`): a symmetric bell centred on 100.
- **Lightbulb burnouts** (`p = 0.001`, `n = 1000`): the bell is centred on 1 and squashed
  against zero, since there cannot be −1 failures. Only the right half shows.

As the slide notes, **the distribution is a function of `n` and `p`**. Those two numbers
determine everything else.

## Centre and spread

The centre is `np`. The slide notes that **the mode, mean and median are all `np`** when
that is a whole number. 200 flips centre on 100; 1,000 bulbs on 1.

The spread has a closed form:

```
σ = √(np(1 − p))
```

| case | `np(1−p)` | `σ` |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 200 coin flips | `200 × 0.5 × 0.5 = 50` | about 7.1 |
| 1,000 bulbs | `1000 × 0.001 × 0.999 ≈ 1` | about 1 |

Two things to notice. `p(1−p)` is largest at `p = 0.5`, so fair coins are as spread out as
binomials get for a given `n`. And `σ` grows with `√n`, not `n`: four times the flips gives
twice the spread.

## Exactly half is unlikely

The slide's last point is surprising: **the probability of getting exactly `n/2` heads is
`O(1/√n)`, so it is actually quite low.**

Why: the counts spread over a band about `σ` wide on each side of the centre, and `σ` grows
like `√n`. Roughly `√n` values share most of the probability between them, so each one
gets something like `1/√n`. For 200 flips the exact figure is about 0.056. The single most
likely outcome happens less than one time in seventeen. At 20,000 flips it is about 0.006.

This matters when you read data. A result that is "exactly as expected" is itself rare, and
small deviations from `np` are what chance normally produces. They are not evidence that
anything is wrong.

> A binomial count centres on `np` and wanders by about `√(np(1−p))`. The most likely
> single value is still unlikely, because the probability is spread over a band that grows
> with `√n`.
