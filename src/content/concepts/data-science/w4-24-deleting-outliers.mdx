Once you have found an outlier, the tempting move is to delete it and refit. The lecture gives
both sides.

## When it helps

**Deleting outliers prior to fitting can yield better models, e.g. if these points correspond
to measurement error.** A reading that is wrong at the source adds noise and can drag a fit
toward it. Removing it, with evidence that it is wrong, moves the model nearer the truth.

## When it hurts

**It can also yield worse models, e.g. if you are simply deleting points which are not
explained by your simple model.**

Suppose the data follows a gentle curve and you fit a straight line. A few points sit far from
the line. If you delete them, the line fits the rest well, and you have not improved the
model: you have cut the data down until it agreed with your assumption. The outliers were the
curve's evidence.

| | Reason for deleting | Verdict |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Good | The measurement is known or shown to be wrong | Remove or fix it, and record why |
| Bad | The model cannot explain it | Keep it, and question the model |

## The test to apply

Ask what you know about the *point* that has nothing to do with the *model*. If the answer is
"the sensor was faulty that day", that is evidence. If it is "the line misses it", that is a
sign about the line.

> Delete a point because of what you know about how it was measured, never because of how
> badly your model fits it.
