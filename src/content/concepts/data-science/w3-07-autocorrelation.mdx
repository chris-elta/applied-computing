Time-series data is the awkward case for everything so far. Yesterday's sales are not an
independent draw from the same distribution as today's. The data has memory, and cycles.

## Cycles

The lecture's example: sales in different businesses may have **7-day, 30-day, 365-day and
even four-year (`4 × 365`)** cycles. A café is busy at weekends, a shop at month end, a
florist in February, an Olympic-year supplier every fourth year. If you ignore a cycle, you
misread it: a January dip may be the season, not a problem.

## Correlate a series with itself

To find a cycle, correlate the series with a shifted copy of itself. **Autocorrelation at
lag `k`** is the correlation between `S[t]` and `S[t+k]` over all valid `t`
(`0 < t < n − k`). If the series repeats every `k` steps, this is unexpectedly large.

| Lag | Autocorrelation | Reading |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | near 0 | today does not predict tomorrow |
| 7 | high | today predicts the same weekday next week |
| 14, 21 | high, fading | multiples of the cycle also match |

## The autocorrelation function

Plot the autocorrelation against `k` and you get the **autocorrelation function** (the
chart on slide 15). Peaks mark cycle lengths. One lag costs `O(n)` to compute, since you
walk the series once. Naively, all `n` lags would cost `O(n²)`, but the **Fast Fourier
Transform** yields the full set in `O(n log n)`. For a series of a million points that is
the difference between hours and seconds.

## Two cautions

- A series that trends upward correlates with itself at every lag, drowning out cycles.
  Remove the trend first.
- A peak is a pattern, not a cause. That distinction is the subject of the next concept.

> Autocorrelation asks when a series resembles its own past. Peaks in the answer reveal the
> rhythm of the data.
