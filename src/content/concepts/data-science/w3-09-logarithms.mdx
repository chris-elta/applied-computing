You already met logarithms in algorithms courses, where they count how many times something
can be halved. Here they earn a place for different reasons.

## What a logarithm is

The logarithm is the **inverse of the exponential**. If `b^x = y`, then `log_b(y) = x`. It
answers "what power?":

| Statement | Reading |
| --- | --- |
| `2³ = 8` | so `log₂(8) = 3` |
| `10⁴ = 10,000` | so `log₁₀(10,000) = 4` |

Logs squash huge ranges. Going from 1 to a billion is only nine steps on a base-10 log
scale.

## Sums of logs instead of products

The property that matters most in data work is `log(ab) = log a + log b`. The lecture
applies it to probabilities: **summing logs of probabilities is more numerically stable than
multiplying them.**

Suppose a model scores a document by multiplying the probability of each of its 1,000 words,
each around 0.01. The true answer is about `10⁻²⁰⁰⁰`. A computer's ordinary floating-point
number bottoms out near `10⁻³⁰⁸`, so the product **underflows to zero**, and every document
looks equally impossible.

Sum the logs instead and each word contributes about −2, giving a total of −2000. Comparing
documents by that sum ranks them correctly. And since the log is increasing, whichever
document has the larger probability also has the larger log-probability.

## Where this is going

The two concepts after this one use the logarithm for a different purpose: making *ratios*
and *skewed data* behave. Same function, different reasons.

> A logarithm turns products into sums. Wherever numbers multiply to something absurdly small
> or large, work in logs.
