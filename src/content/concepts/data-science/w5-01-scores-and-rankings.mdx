Think about how your grade in a course gets made. There are lab marks, quiz marks, an exam
mark, perhaps participation. Somebody decides the labs are worth 30% and the exam 40%, adds
it up, and you become one number. Then the class list is sorted by that number.

Those are the two objects this week is about.

| | what it is | example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Scoring function** | A measure that reduces multi-dimensional data to a single value, to highlight some particular property | Your percentage in a course |
| **Ranking** | An ordering of items, usually made by sorting scores | Your position in the class |

The slide shows a wall of logos: college rankings, FIFA world rankings, tennis rankings.
Every one of them is a formula somebody chose, followed by a sort.

## What grading shows about scores

The lecture uses grades because you already know them from the inside. It points out three
things about grading systems.

- **Degrees of arbitrariness.** Each teacher differs. One drops the lowest quiz, another
  does not. Neither is wrong.
- **Lack of validation data.** There is no *right* grade. Nobody can look up what you
  deserved and compare it with what the formula produced.
- **General robustness.** Students tend to get similar grades in all their classes anyway.
  Different formulas, written by different people, mostly agree about who is doing well.

The third point is what keeps the first two from being fatal. If scores were arbitrary and
also disagreed wildly, they would be noise. Because sensible formulas tend to agree, a
score that is not provably right can still be useful.

## Scores and statistics

The slide ends with: **calling scores *statistics* lends them more dignity.** A batting
average, a GPA and a credit score are all computed the same way a grade is: someone chose
inputs and weights. The name does not change what the number is. When you meet a statistic,
ask what was combined, with what weights, and who decided.

The rest of the week follows from the missing right answer. With no answer to check against,
you cannot test a score the way you test a prediction, so you need other ways to tell a good
one from a bad one.

> A score is a designed number, not a measured one. Someone chose what goes in and how much
> each part counts.
