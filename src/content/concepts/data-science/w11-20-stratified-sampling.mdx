Random sampling is unbiased on average. Any one random sample can still be unlucky. Slide 13
gives the remedy: to ensure no group is statistically over- or under-sampled, **explicitly
sample proportionally from each group**.

## How it works

Divide the population into groups, called **strata**. Then sample within each stratum
separately, taking the same fraction from each. The slide's picture shows a population
sorted into strata and a sample drawn from every one.

Suppose a population of 10,000 is 90% urban and 10% rural, and you want 200 people, a 2%
sample.

| stratum | population | stratified sample (2% of each) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| urban | 9,000 | 180 |
| rural | 1,000 | 20 |

A simple random sample of 200 would contain 20 rural people on average. In practice it might
contain 15, or 25. A stratified sample contains exactly 20, every time.

## Why that matters

Slide 14 gives the reasoning.

**When small groups have distinct opinions or attributes, sample variance can dominate
signal.** Say 80% of rural people support a policy and 30% of urban people do. The true
overall figure is `0.9 × 30% + 0.1 × 80% = 35%`. If chance gives you 25 rural people in
place of 20, your estimate rises through nothing but luck in who was drawn. A small and
distinctive group makes the whole estimate jumpy. Fixing the group sizes removes that source
of noise.

**Bias comes from sample over- or under-representation.** If a group's share of the sample
differs from its share of the population, the result leans toward or away from that group.
Stratifying sets every share to the correct value by construction.

## The requirement

The slide states the problem: **you must identify group members prior to sampling**. To take
2% of rural people you need to know who is rural before you draw anyone. That information is
often missing or unreliable. You also have to decide which grouping matters, and stratifying
on one attribute does nothing for an attribute you did not think of.

## When there are no predefined groups

The slide's last point: **cluster sampling** is an unsupervised variant with no predefined
groups. If nobody has labelled the records, a clustering algorithm can discover groups of
similar records from the data, and those discovered groups can then be sampled from.
"Unsupervised" has the meaning it had in week 10: the groups are found, not given.

> Stratified sampling takes the same fraction from every group, so each group's share is
> exactly right and not right only on average. It needs the groups to be known before you
> sample.
