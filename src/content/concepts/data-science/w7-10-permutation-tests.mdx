The t-test and the KS test come from formulas, with assumptions attached. The t-test leans on
normality. Both need the right table, the right number of tails and the right degrees of
freedom. The slides call these *subtleties*. **Permutation tests** skip the formulas and let the
computer simulate what randomness looks like. Slide 22 calls this a *more computationally
idiot-proof* way to establish significance.

## The idea

From slide 23: **if your hypothesis is true, then randomly shuffled data sets should not look
like real data.**

Take two groups with mean scores 75 and 80, as in the notes. The null hypothesis says the group
makes no difference. If that is true, the labels "A" and "B" are arbitrary. You could shuffle
them, reassigning each score to a group at random, and the data would look much the same.

So do exactly that:

1. Compute the statistic on the real data. Here, the difference in means is 5.
2. Pool all the values. Shuffle the group labels randomly, keeping the group sizes.
3. Compute the same statistic on the shuffled data.
4. Repeat steps 2 and 3 many times, at least 1,000.
5. See where the real statistic falls among the shuffled ones.

If shuffled data often produces a gap of 5 or more, the real gap is unremarkable. If it almost
never does, the labels matter.

## The heights picture

Slide 24 shows heights as bars, coloured and sorted by length, with men and women in two blocks.
In the real data (left) each block is a smooth staircase, and the two blocks differ clearly in
their lengths. In the two shuffled versions (centre and right), each block gets a random mix of
heights. The blocks now look alike, and the difference between them shrinks.

The real data looks nothing like its shuffles, and that is the evidence that gender is
related to height.

## Why use it

Slide 22 lists the advantages:

| advantage | because |
| --- | --- |
| no assumption of normality or equal variance | the null distribution is built from your own data |
| works for almost any statistic | mean, median, correlation, accuracy: compute it on shuffled data the same way |
| easy to implement | a loop, a shuffle and a count |
| intuitive | you can see how rare the real result is |

The slide's title says *bootstrapping*, and the notes separate the two related ideas.
Bootstrapping resamples *with replacement* from your sample, to see how much a statistic
varies. A permutation test *shuffles labels*, to test the null hypothesis directly. Both use the
computer in place of a formula.

You choose the statistic. Slide 23 says you need a statistic you believe is interesting, such
as a correlation, an error or a size. The test then says how unusual its real value is.

> Shuffling the labels creates worlds where the null hypothesis is true. If the real data stands
> out from those worlds, the labels carry information.
