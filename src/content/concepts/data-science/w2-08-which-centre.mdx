A summary of data hides something. The question is whether it hides the thing you care
about. The lecture gives three measures of centre and a table for choosing between them.

## The mean

Add the values and divide by how many there are. It uses all the data and is easy to compute.
The slide's caveat: the mean is meaningful for **symmetric distributions without outliers**.

## The median

The middle value once the data is sorted. It ignores how extreme the extremes are, which
protects it from outliers and makes it good for skewed data. The price is that it ignores
most of the exact values.

## The geometric mean

The `n`th root of the product of `n` values. It is always at most the arithmetic mean, and
more sensitive to values near zero. It suits **ratios**: 1/2 and 2/1 should average to 1, and
the geometric mean gives exactly `√(0.5 × 2) = 1`, where the arithmetic mean gives 1.25.

It cannot be used with negative values.

## Two worked cases

**Salaries: 50k, 50k, 60k, 70k, 5M.** The mean is 5,230k ÷ 5 ≈ 1.05M. No one earns anything
like that; four of five earn under 71k. The median is 60k, a much better "typical" salary.
An outlier controls the mean and barely touches the median.

**Growth: +50%, then −50%.** Think of them as multipliers, 1.5 and 0.5. Start with 100: you
reach 150, then 75. The arithmetic mean of the percentages is 0%, which says nothing changed,
yet you lost a quarter. The geometric mean of the multipliers is `√0.75 ≈ 0.866`, about
−13% per year, and `0.866² = 0.75` reproduces what happened.

## Choosing

| Measure | Use it when | Avoid it when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mean | Data is roughly symmetric, no outliers | Skewed data or outliers |
| Median | Skewed data, outliers | You need every value to count |
| Geometric mean | Ratios, percentages, growth rates | Negative values |

## The misconception

"The average" is not one thing. A report that says "average salary" without saying which is
either careless or selective, and the difference can be a factor of twenty.

The measures also connect to spread. In the next concept the standard deviation is built
around the mean, so it inherits the same sensitivity to outliers.

> Ask what the data looks like before picking the average. Skew and outliers favour the
> median; growth favours the geometric mean.
