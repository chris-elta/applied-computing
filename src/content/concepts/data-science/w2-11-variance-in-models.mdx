The signal-versus-noise problem is not just about baseball. It applies to the models you will
build.

The lecture says you will typically develop **several models for each challenge**, from very
simple to complex. Then you compare their scores. The trouble is what fills those scores.

## Where the differences come from

Some of the difference in performance is explained by simple variance:

- which training and evaluation pairs were selected,
- how well the parameters were optimised,
- and similar accidents of the process.

None of these reflects the quality of the underlying approach. Re-run with a different split
and the ranking can change.

## A small improvement may be noise

A model that is **0.5% better in accuracy** might just be noise. You would not celebrate a
hitter going from 0.300 to 0.302; the same scepticism applies.

## What to do

In such cases, the slide's advice is to choose the **simpler model**. It is:

- easier to interpret,
- faster to run,
- at less risk of **overfitting**.

Choosing simple when scores are indistinguishable is not a compromise: you get the benefits
of simplicity at no measured cost. The complex model has to earn its extra machinery with a
gain that is clearly bigger than the noise.

To know whether a gain is bigger than noise, you need to *measure* the noise — for example by
repeating the evaluation, which the next concept covers.

> A gap between two scores is a claim only if it beats the noise. If it does not, take the
> simpler model.
