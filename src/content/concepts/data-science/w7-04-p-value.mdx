The null hypothesis says nothing is going on. The **p-value** measures how badly the data
fits that claim.

## The definition

From the slide:

> The p-value is the probability of getting a result as extreme as, or more extreme than, the
> one observed, **if the null hypothesis were true.**

Read it in three parts:

| part | meaning |
| --- | --- |
| *if `H₀` were true* | imagine a world where there is no effect |
| *as extreme or more* | count every result at least as far from "no difference" as yours |
| *probability* | how often that world produces such results |

In symbols, `p = Pr(result at least this extreme | H₀)`.

## A worked feel for it

Suppose a coin is fair; that is `H₀`. You flip it 10 times and get 9 heads. Results at least
this extreme, in either direction, are 9 or 10 heads, or 9 or 10 tails. Each of the `2¹⁰ = 1024`
sequences of flips is equally likely, and 22 of them give such a result (1 + 10 for heads, the
same for tails). So `p = 22/1024 ≈ 0.021`. A fair coin does this about 2% of the time.

That is surprising, but not impossible. The p-value does not say the coin is biased. It says
how unusual this result would be if it were not.

## Reading it

The slides give the conventions:

| p-value | conventional reading |
| --- | --- |
| `p < 0.05` | statistically significant |
| `p < 0.01` | highly significant |

**Smaller p-values are stronger evidence against `H₀`.** The cut-off, written `α` (alpha), is
chosen before you look at the data. 0.05 means accepting that one time in twenty, a world with
no effect will still produce a "significant" result. That fact drives the Bonferroni correction
later in the week.

## The two classic misreadings

**It is not the probability that `H₀` is true.** `p = 0.03` does not mean a 3% chance of no
effect. The p-value is a probability of *data* given a hypothesis. The probability of a
hypothesis given data is a different quantity, and slide 21 points to Bayes' theorem as the way
to convert one into the other. The permutation-test caveat at the end of this week returns to
this distinction, because it is the most common mistake in reading significance.

**It is not the size of the effect.** A p-value says how *surprising* a difference is, not how
*large*. With enough data, a trivial difference gives a tiny p-value. The effect-size concept
takes this up.

> A p-value is how often a world with no effect would produce data at least this extreme. Small
> means surprising. It is not the chance the null is true, and not the size of the effect.
