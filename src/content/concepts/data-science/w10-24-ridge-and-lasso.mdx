The regularised cost from the previous concept has a name. Slide 46 names it and its main
alternative.

## Coefficients as importance

Slide 46 starts with a condition:

> When variables have mean zero, a coefficient's magnitude is a measure of its value to the
> objective function.

If features are in wildly different units, a coefficient's size says nothing: price per square foot
is small because square feet are many. After standardising every feature (Z-scores, or the mean
normalisation from the feature-scaling concept), a large |θⱼ| really does mean feature j matters a
lot. That is what makes penalising sizes fair: every coefficient is measured on the same scale.

## Two penalties

| method | penalty | in words |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **ridge regression** | `λ Σ θⱼ²` | the sum of **squared** coefficients: the L₂ penalty |
| **LASSO** | `λ Σ abs(θⱼ)` | the sum of **absolute values**: the L₁ penalty |

Ridge is exactly the regularised cost from the previous concept. Week 9's Occam's razor concept
mentioned both as methods that "employ penalty functions to minimise features".

They behave differently in one important way. Squaring makes a tiny coefficient cost almost nothing,
so ridge shrinks every coefficient but rarely to exactly zero. The absolute value keeps charging
small coefficients at full rate, so LASSO tends to push the least useful ones **all the way to zero**,
dropping those features from the model. LASSO does feature selection as part of fitting.

## Correlated variables

Slide 47's example shows what ridge buys you. `v₁` and `v₂` are two **highly correlated** Z-score
variables: nearly the same number every time. Two models:

```
A:  Y = 200,001·v₁ − 200,000·v₂
B:  Y = v₁
```

While `v₁ ≈ v₂`, model A gives about `200,001·v₁ − 200,000·v₁ = v₁`. The two models make almost the
same predictions, and A may even fit the training data slightly better: its enormous coefficients
let it chase the tiny, noisy differences between v₁ and v₂.

Now compare their ridge penalties:

| model | Σ θⱼ² |
| --- | --- |
| A | 200,001² + 200,000² ≈ 8 × 10¹⁰ |
| B | 1² = 1 |

Ridge prefers B by a vast margin. That is the right answer:

> The second is better even if it fits slightly less well on the training data.

Rewrite model A as `Y = v₁ + 200,000·(v₁ − v₂)`. On new data where v₁ and v₂ differ by even 0.01,
its prediction shifts by about 2,000. Model B ignores v₂, so the gap does not move it at all. A's training-set advantage was noise, and its coefficients would make it
unstable on anything new. This is high variance from week 9, and also the redundant-features problem
from the normal-equation concept, in a softer form: when features nearly duplicate each other, many
very different θs fit almost equally, and ridge picks the small, stable one.

> Ridge penalises squared coefficients and shrinks them all; LASSO penalises absolute values and sets
> some to exactly zero. Both need standardised features, and both prefer a simple model to a
> slightly better training fit, which is what protects against correlated features and overfitting.
