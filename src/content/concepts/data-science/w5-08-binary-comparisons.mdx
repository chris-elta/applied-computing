The scores so far this week had a common shape: take some measurements of an item, normalize
them, and combine them with weights. The slide says so: **linear combinations of normalized
values generally yield reasonable scores.** For much of data science that recipe is enough.

Some things come with no measurements, though. A chess player has no height, weight or
speed that makes them good at chess. What you have is a record of who they beat and who beat
them. To rank chess players you have to work from those results.

## The raw material

**Rankings are often formed by analyzing series of binary comparisons.** A binary comparison
is one judgement between two items:

- Team A beats team B.
- An expert votes for A instead of B.
- A student chooses university A over B.

Each one is a small piece of evidence about the order. The slides write it `A > B`, which
you read as "A is preferred to B".

## Counting wins

The simplest thing to do is count wins and sort by the count. The slide warns against it in
red: **vote counts fail to pick the best when different teams face different levels of
competition.**

| team | wins | opponents |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A | 3 | all top teams |
| B | 5 | all weak teams |

Counting says B is better. Most people would say A is. A count treats every win as the same
size, and wins are not the same size: beating a strong opponent is better evidence than
beating a weak one. If every team played every other team equally often the counts would be
fair. Real schedules, expert panels and search results are rarely that tidy.

Every technique in the rest of the week is a way of making a comparison count for more or
less depending on who it involved.

## Four techniques

| technique | core idea | example use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Elo** | Pairwise competition and probability updates | Sports, games |
| **Merging ranks** | Combining multiple ranking lists | Voting, meta-search |
| **Graph orderings** | Use directional relationships | Tournaments, citations |
| **PageRank** | Importance through network links | Web search, influence detection |

They take different inputs. Elo takes a stream of match results over time. Merging takes
several complete rankings and produces one. Graph orderings and PageRank take the whole web
of who-beat-whom, or who-links-to-whom, at once. Part of the skill is seeing which shape
your data has.

> A list of who beat whom is enough to build a ranking, provided you account for the
> strength of the opponents and do not simply count wins.
