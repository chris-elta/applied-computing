> A broken clock is right twice a day.

Before you can say a model is good, you need to know what *no skill* looks like. A
**baseline** is the simplest reasonable model for the problem. From slide 66:

> Only after you decisively beat your baselines can your models be deemed effective.

## Representative baselines

From slide 67, with the lecture notes' examples:

| baseline | example |
| --- | --- |
| uniform or random selection among labels | guess "spam" or "not spam" with equal chance, or weighted by how common each is |
| the most common label in the training data | always predict "not fraud" |
| the best single-variable model | predict a movie's gross from its budget alone |
| same label as the previous point in time | tomorrow's price is today's |
| rule-of-thumb heuristics | flag an email as spam if it contains "free money" |

## Why baselines matter

A number on its own means nothing. "92% accuracy" sounds strong until you learn that 92% of
the cases are negative, so always saying "no" also scores 92%. The next section makes this
precise, but the baseline already exposes it.

Baselines also earn their place in three other ways:

- they tell you whether a problem is **hard**: if the simple heuristic scores 98%, there is
  little room for a complicated model;
- they catch **bugs**: a sophisticated model that loses to "predict the average" is
  probably broken;
- they are part of **consensus**: comparing with other forecasts was one of Silver's
  principles in the previous concept.

## Fair, not stupid

> Baseline models must be fair: they should be simple but not stupid.

Beating a random guess at house prices proves nothing. Beating "price = average price per
square foot × size" proves something. Choose the strongest simple model, because that is the
one a sceptic would propose.

> Report every model next to the best simple model for the problem. If it does not beat that
> decisively, it has not shown any skill.
