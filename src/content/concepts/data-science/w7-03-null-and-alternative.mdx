A new teaching method is tried on a class, and the average score rises from 75 to 80. The
slide asks: **is it really better, or did we just get lucky with this sample?**

Real data always contains random variation. Teach the *same* way to two different classes
and their averages will still differ. So a difference alone proves nothing. We need a
structured way to decide whether a difference is bigger than chance would usually produce.
That structure is **hypothesis testing**.

## Two hypotheses

Every test starts by writing down two competing claims about the populations, not about the
samples.

| | name | claim | example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `H₀` | null hypothesis | there is **no** real effect; any difference is chance | `μ₁ = μ₂` |
| `H₁` | alternative hypothesis | there **is** a real effect or difference | `μ₁ ≠ μ₂` |

Here `μ₁` and `μ₂` are the true average scores under each teaching method: what you would
get if you could teach every possible student both ways. You never see them. You see two
sample averages, 75 and 80, which wobble around the true values.

## Why the null comes first

The null is the hypothesis you can calculate with. If nothing is going on, you know what the
world should look like: the two samples come from the same population, and you can work out
how often samples that size differ by 5 points just by chance. The alternative, "there is
some difference", does not say how large, so it gives you nothing to calculate.

So the logic runs backwards from what you might expect:

1. **Assume `H₀`.** Suppose the method makes no difference.
2. **Ask how surprising the data would be** under that assumption.
3. **If very surprising, reject `H₀`** in favour of `H₁`.
4. **Otherwise, fail to reject `H₀`.**

## What you can and cannot conclude

The slide is careful here: **we never prove `H₁`. We find evidence strong enough that `H₀` is
unlikely.** The key idea from slide 7 is the same: we can never be 100% certain, but we can
measure how unlikely such a result would be if nothing had changed.

The same caution applies in the other direction. Failing to reject `H₀` does not prove there
is no effect. It means this data was not enough to show one. A real but small improvement,
tested on ten students, will often fail to reach significance.

The number that measures "how surprising" is the p-value, in the next concept.

> State the null: nothing is going on. Then ask how unlikely your data would be if that were
> true. You can reject the null, but you never prove either hypothesis.
