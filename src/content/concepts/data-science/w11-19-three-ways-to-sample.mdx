You have decided to keep a fraction of the data. Which records? Slides 10 to 12 give three
methods. Each fixes a weakness of the one before.

## Truncation: the first n

Take the first `n` records and stop. It is simple and anyone repeating it gets the same
records. The weakness is that **record order often has meaning**. Slide 10 lists three ways.

| if the file is ordered by | the first `n` records are | bias |
| --- | --- | --- |
| time | the oldest | temporal: you analyse only old data |
| name | the A's | lexicographic: more Arabic names, fewer Chinese |
| ID number | the lowest IDs | numerical: IDs can encode meaning, as social security numbers do |

The lexicographic example shows how quietly this happens. Surnames beginning with A are not a
random slice of people; some languages contribute many of them and others few. Nobody
intended a sample skewed by origin, and the alphabet produced one.

## Random sampling: each with probability p

Go through the records and keep each one independently with probability `p`. Position in the
file no longer matters, so there are no explicit biases. Slide 11 lists the costs.

- **Statistical discrepancies.** Chance does not spread evenly. Some regions of the data
  will be over-sampled and others under-sampled, and the sample size itself is only
  approximately `p` times the total.
- **Not reproducible** without the seed and the random generator. Someone re-running your
  analysis gets different records unless they have both.
- **Multiple samples will not be disjoint.** Draw two random samples and some records land
  in both. That matters if one was meant for training and the other for testing.

## Uniform sampling: every m-th record

Keep the records whose position is congruent to `i mod m`. With `m = 100` and `i = 7` that
is records 7, 107, 207 and so on: one in every hundred, evenly spaced through the file.

Slide 12 says this balances many concerns:

- you obtain an **exact number** of records;
- it is **quick and reproducible**, with no seed to keep;
- it **ensures disjoint samples**: choose a different `i` and you get entirely different
  records.

The slide gives a real use. Twitter governed its API services this way, offering streams of
different sizes (spritzer, garden hose, fire hose) as different fractions of the full feed.

The one thing to watch is a pattern in the file whose period matches `m`. If records
alternate weekday, weekend in blocks of the same length as your step, every `m`-th record
could come from one kind of day.

## Side by side

| | truncation | random | uniform |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| exact sample size | yes | no | yes |
| reproducible | yes | only with seed and generator | yes |
| disjoint samples | yes | no | yes |
| free of order bias | no | yes | yes, barring a matching period |

> Taking the first `n` records inherits whatever the ordering means. Random sampling
> removes that bias at the cost of exactness and reproducibility. Taking every `m`-th record
> gives exact, repeatable, non-overlapping samples.
