Here is a trap for anyone who has just learned the mean. Two products have the same average
lifetime, so surely they are equivalent?

The lecture's example uses **printer cartridges**, with two lifetime distributions drawn side
by side, both centred on 3000. One is a bell curve: most cartridges last close to 3000,
but a fair number fall short or run long. The other is a single spike at 3000: every cartridge
lasts almost exactly that long. The mean is the same; the behaviour is not.

## What the slide says

Two statements, and they pull in opposite directions:

- **Distributions with the same mean can look very different.**
- **Together, the mean and standard deviation fairly well characterise any distribution.**

The first is the warning: one number is not enough. The second is the reassurance: two
numbers are much better.

## Why two are enough

The mean says where the distribution sits, and the standard deviation says how wide it is.
For a cartridge buyer, that translates into two questions: how long will it last on average,
and how much will it vary around that? A buyer planning stock cares about the second at
least as much as the first.

The claim is "fairly well", not "completely". There are shapes two numbers cannot capture,
such as a distribution with two separate peaks. That is why you should still plot the data
where you can.

## The next question

If mean and standard deviation are close to a full description, can we say something precise
about how the mass is spread from them alone? The final concept gives a guarantee that holds
for any distribution.

> A mean tells you where the distribution sits. It cannot tell you how much to trust that
> as a prediction for one item.
