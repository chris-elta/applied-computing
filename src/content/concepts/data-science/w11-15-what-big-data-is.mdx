Everything in this course so far has assumed the data fits in a table on your laptop. Slide
2 names the other case. The buzzword "Big Data" presumes analysis of truly massive data sets:
all of Twitter or Facebook, the web logs of a major site, the genome sequences of thousands
of people, every image on Instagram.

The slide closes with the theme of the lecture: **working with data generally gets harder
with size.**

## How big

Slide 3 gives 2025 figures, all per day:

| service | per day |
| --- | --- |
| X | 500 million tweets |
| Facebook | 4 petabytes of incoming data (4 million GB) |
| Google | 8.5 billion search queries |
| Instagram | 95 million photos |
| Apple | 250 million app downloads |
| Email | 333 billion messages |

For scale: a laptop with a 1,000 GB disk would hold Facebook's incoming data for about 22
seconds of the day. No single machine holds this, and that is what forces the techniques
later in the lecture.

## Three Vs

Size is one of three axes on slide 4.

| V | the question | at the small end | at the big end |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Volume** | how much? | megabytes | petabytes and beyond |
| **Velocity** | how fast does it arrive? | batch: collected, then analysed | real time: analysed as it arrives |
| **Variety** | how many forms? | tidy tables | text, photos, video, unstructured data |

Data can be "big" on any one of them. A modest stream that needs a response within a second
is a velocity problem. A small archive of mixed scans, emails and spreadsheets is a variety
problem. Each needs different tools, so say which V you mean.

The slide places student work on this picture: projects are typically **batch problems on
megabyte-scale CSV-type data**. That is the small, slow, uniform corner. The habits it builds
(load everything, try an idea, look at the result) are the ones that stop working as you move
outward.

## A fourth V

The slide adds **veracity**: can the data be trusted? Volume, velocity and variety describe
how hard the data is to handle. Veracity describes whether handling it is worth anything.
More data of doubtful accuracy is not better data, and the next concept is about exactly
that.

> "Big" has three axes: volume, velocity and variety. Most coursework sits at the easy
> corner of all three. Veracity, whether the data is true, matters at any size.
