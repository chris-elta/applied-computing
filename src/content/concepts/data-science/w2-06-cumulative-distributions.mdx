You have a pdf. You can also ask a different question: how likely is the variable to be
**at most** some value?

## The cdf

The **cumulative distribution function** (cdf) is the running sum of the pdf:

```
F(x) = P(V ≤ x)
```

For the two dice, add up the bars as you move right: after 2 you have 1/36, after 3 you have
3/36, and so on until you reach 1 at 12. It starts at 0, ends at 1 and never goes down.

The pdf and cdf hold **exactly the same information**. One is the integral of the other, and
the other is its derivative. The slide's phrase is worth keeping: sum to go from pdf to cdf,
difference to come back.

### When the bars are not width 1

For the dice, every bar is one unit wide, so a bar's height *is* its probability and adding
heights works. In general a histogram's height is a **density**: probability per unit of `x`.
The probability in a bar is its area:

```
P(bar) = height × width
F(x)   = sum of (height × width) over every bar up to x
```

For example, bin phone prices into $100-wide bins. If the $300–$400 bar has density 0.002 per
dollar, it holds 0.002 × 100 = 0.2 of the probability. Rebin into $50-wide bins and the
density stays about 0.002, but each bar now holds 0.002 × 50 = 0.1. The density does not
depend on the bin width, which is why you multiply by it. With width 1 the multiplication
changes nothing, and you get the dice rule.

## Same information, different readability

Both contain the full story, but they make different questions easy.

| Question | Easier in |
| --- | --- |
| What is the most likely value? | pdf — look for the peak |
| What fraction lies below a threshold? | cdf — read off the height |
| Is the shape symmetric, skewed, lumpy? | pdf |

## The iPhone example

The slides ask: *Apple iPhone sales have been exploding, right?* The chart shown is
cumulative sales over time, which climbs steeply. It looks explosive.

Then: **how explosive is that growth, really?** The answer is that cumulative charts present
a misleading view of growth rate. A running total always rises, even if you sell the same
number every quarter. What you actually care about is the incremental change, which is the
derivative of this function, and that is hard to see by eye.

To judge growth, plot sales per period instead.

> Choose the plot for the question. A cumulative curve tells you totals to date, not how
> fast things are changing.
