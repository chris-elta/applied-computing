Two students ask the same question. One has 10 data points, one has 100,000. Both compute a
correlation and both want to know whether to believe it. The answer depends on how many
points there are as much as on `r`.

## Significance depends on sample size

Random data is never perfectly uncorrelated. Draw a few points and you will often see an
accidental `r` of 0.3 or 0.4. A significance test asks how often chance alone would produce
a correlation at least this large. Roughly, for the usual 0.05 threshold:

| Sample size `n` | Smallest `r` that counts as significant |
| --- | --- |
| 10 | about 0.63 |
| 100 | about 0.20 |
| 10,000 | about 0.02 |

(These are standard critical values for a two-sided test.) A correlation of 0.19 between video
games and violent behaviour, from the earlier list, is only convincing with a large sample.
The slide's point is: **even small correlations become significant with large-enough sample
sizes.**

## Significant is not important

The slide's second warning: significance testing measures whether there is a *real*
relationship, rather than random chance. It does **not** measure whether the relationship
matters. That second thing is the **effect size**, and `r²` is one way to read it.

So a headline saying "a significant link was found" needs one follow-up question: how big?
A 0.02 correlation can be perfectly real and perfectly useless.

## Why weak signals still matter

The lecture draws an optimistic conclusion. Each single correlation may explain only a small
effect, but **many weak, independent correlations can together have strong predictive
power.** This motivates "big data" models with many parameters: no one feature is
impressive, but a hundred of them, combined, can predict well. Independence is the condition
that matters. Repeating the same weak signal a hundred times adds nothing.

> Ask two questions of every correlation: is it real (significance), and is it big enough to
> matter (effect size)? Large samples make the first easy and leave the second open.
