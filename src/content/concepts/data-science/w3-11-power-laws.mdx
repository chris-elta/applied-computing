Some quantities are not bunched around a typical value. Wealth, follower counts, city
populations and website traffic have a few enormous values and a crowd of small ones. Their
mean is dominated by the giants, and their standard deviation is huge for the same reason.
The tools from last week assume a tamer shape.

## Power laws

A **power-law** distribution is one where values keep occurring far out in the tail. Plotted
directly it is a spike against the left-hand axis with a tail too thin to see.

The lecture's remedy: **taking the logarithm of variables with a power-law distribution
brings them more in line with traditional distributions.** The log turns "how many
times bigger" into "how many steps along".

## The lecturer's example

The slide jokes that the lecturer's wealth is roughly the same number of logs from typical
students as from Bill Gates. Read that with round, illustrative figures: a typical student
has about `10³` dollars and Bill Gates about `10¹¹`. On the log scale those are 3 and 11, and
someone at 7 (about `10⁷` dollars) sits 4 logs from each. On the raw scale that person is
ten thousand times richer than the student and ten thousand times poorer than the billionaire,
which is just what the equal distance on the log scale says.

That is not to say log scales are more true; they answer a different question, one about
*proportions*. For wealth, income and traffic, proportions are usually what you care about.
Doubling matters whether you start from a hundred or a million.

## Why it matters for correlation

This connects back to the start of the week. Pearson's `r` is dragged around by extreme
values, since they dominate the sums of squares. Correlating raw wealth with anything is
mostly correlating the few richest people. Take logs first and the whole population
contributes.

The last slide gives the rule: **take the logarithm of a value before analysis** when your
data follows a power law or is a ratio. It applies to the two cases you have now seen.

> If the data spans orders of magnitude, analyse it on a log scale. Equal ratios become equal
> distances, and the giants stop drowning out everyone else.
