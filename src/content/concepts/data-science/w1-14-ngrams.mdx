The third dataset is **Google Ngrams**. It presents an annual time series of how often every
"popular" word or phrase of **one to five words** occurs in scanned books.

Two facts define what it can and cannot tell you:

- **"Popular"** means it appears more than 40 times in total.
- Google has scanned about **15% of all books ever published**, making the resource quite
  comprehensive but far from complete.

In who/what/where/when/why terms, the *what* is word frequencies, the *when* is by year, and
the *who* is authors and publishers of the books that got scanned, which is not the same as
everyone who speaks the language.

## The lecturer's questions

- How has the amount of **cursing** changed over time?
- What is the **lifespan of fame and technologies**? Is it increasing or decreasing?
- How often do **new words emerge**? Do they stay in common usage?
- What words are **associated with other words** — can you build a language model?

## Reading a chart of counts

These are good questions because a time series makes them visible. Each also needs a check
against the data's biases. A rise in a word's frequency could mean the world changed, or that
more books were printed, or that the library scanned more recent titles.

The fourth question links to what you saw in the music case: a **language model** is the same
kind of thing MeLoDy builds on, trained on text instead of audio.

> Counts in a corpus describe the corpus. Whether they describe the world depends on how the
> corpus was assembled.
