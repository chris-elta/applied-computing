Logistic regression as described so far assumes a tidy problem: two classes of similar size.
Slide 8 lists four ways real problems are less tidy. The first is class size, and the other
three (multi-class, hierarchical classification and partition functions) follow in the next
concepts.

## What imbalance does

Slide 9 asks you to consider an extreme case: 1 positive example and 1,000,000 negative ones.

You might expect the separating line to sit at the midpoint between the lone positive and the
cluster of negatives. It does not. The cost `J(θ)` is an average over all examples, so a
million of its terms come from negatives and one from the positive. The cheapest way to lower
that average is to make every negative very confidently negative, which means pushing the
line **as far from the big cluster as possible**. The single positive has almost no say.

The resulting model can score 99.9999% accuracy by predicting "negative" for everything, and
it is useless for the purpose it was built for. Week 9's warning about accuracy on unbalanced
classes applies here to training as well as to evaluation.

The slide's advice is blunt: **use equal numbers of positive and negative examples.**

## Four ways to get there

From slide 10:

| approach | how | cost |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Find more of the minority class | collect or label more rare cases | effort, sometimes impossible |
| Discard from the bigger class | keep a sample of the majority | throws away real data |
| Weigh the minority class more heavily | make each rare example count more in the cost | risk of overfitting |
| Replicate the smaller class | copy rare examples, ideally with random perturbation | risk of overfitting if copies are exact |

The first is the only one that adds information. Fifty fraud cases copied a thousand times
are still fifty cases, and a model can memorise their quirks. That is why the slide attaches
"beware of overfitting" to weighting and "ideally with random perturbation" to replication:
small random changes to each copy stop the model from fixing on fifty exact points.

Discarding looks wasteful, yet with a million negatives it is often the right call. A simple
model does not need all of them, a point the lectures on scale return to.

> A cost that sums over examples is ruled by the larger class. Balance the classes before
> training, and prefer real minority examples to copies.
