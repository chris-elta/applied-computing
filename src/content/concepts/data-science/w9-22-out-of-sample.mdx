> A joke is not funny the second time, because you already know the punchline.

Slide 86 calls this **the veil of ignorance**. A model tested on data it was trained on
already knows the answers. Good performance there is "very suspect, because models can easily
be overfit", which is the variance problem from the bias-variance concept.

## Out-of-sample predictions

From slide 80:

> The best way to assess models involves out-of-sample predictions: results on data you never
> saw (or even better, did not exist) when you built the model.

"Did not exist" is the gold standard. A model of election results is best tested on the next
election; a stock model on next month's prices. Then there is no way for the future to have
leaked into the building of the model.

## Three-way split

When you cannot wait for the future, partition the data you have. The slide's proportions:

| part | share | used for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| training | 60% | fitting the model's parameters |
| testing | 20% | comparing models and tuning settings |
| evaluation | 20% | one final, honest measurement |

The lecture notes use different names for the last two: testing to tune parameters,
validation to measure final performance. Many textbooks swap "test" and "validation". The
names vary; the roles do not. One part fits, one chooses, one judges.

The essential rule is the slide's condition:

> This works only if you never open the evaluation data until the end.

## Why choosing counts as fitting

The trap is subtle. You never trained on the evaluation set, so it seems safe to look. But
suppose you try ten models and keep the one with the best evaluation score. Some of that
score is luck: that model happened to suit those particular cases. By picking it, you have
fitted to the evaluation set, just one level up. Its score is now optimistic, like the joke
heard twice.

That is why there are three parts and not two. The testing set absorbs all the choosing, and
the evaluation set stays unseen.

## Sins in evaluation

Slide 81 adds that summary statistics can hide mistakes that no split protects against:

- **Did I mix training and evaluation data?** A duplicated record, or a time series split at
  random so the future leaks into the past, does this silently.
- **Do I have bugs in my implementation?**

A suspiciously good score is more often one of these than a breakthrough. The next concept is
about catching them by looking at the errors themselves.

> Judge a model only on data it never saw, and never use that data to make choices. If you
> must choose, choose on a separate test set, and open the final evaluation set once.
