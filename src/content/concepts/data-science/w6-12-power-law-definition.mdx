The slide on wealth lists a ladder of fortunes:

| people | wealth each |
| --- | --- |
| 1 (Bill Gates) | $80 billion |
| 5 | $40 billion |
| 25 | $20 billion |
| 125 | $10 billion |
| 625 | $5 billion |

Every step down halves the fortune and multiplies the number of people by 5. The slide's
summary: **as you multiply the value by `x`, you divide the number of people by `y`.** That
fixed trade between size and count is the defining property of a power law.

## The definition

From the slide, for a power-law distributed variable `X`:

```
P(X = x) = c x⁻ᵃ
```

Two numbers:

- **`a`**, the exponent, does all the work.
- **`c`** is, in the slide's words, **unimportant: for a given `a`, this constant ensures the
  probability sums to 1.** It is bookkeeping, not a choice.

## What doubling does

Compare a value `x` with one twice as big:

```
P(2x) / P(x) = c(2x)⁻ᵃ / c x⁻ᵃ = 2⁻ᵃ
```

The `c`s cancel, and so does `x`. **Doubling `x` reduces the probability by a factor of
`2ᵃ`**, no matter where you start: from 10 to 20, or from a million to two million. The slide
says **larger values keep getting rarer at a steady rate.**

| `a` | doubling `x` divides the probability by |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | 2 |
| 2 | 4 |
| 3 | 8 |

## Reading the exponent off the wealth ladder

Halving the fortune multiplies the count by 5, so `2ᵃ = 5` and `a = log₂ 5 ≈ 2.3`.

## Why this is different from a normal

A normal distribution has a scale built in: `σ`. Each extra standard deviation makes values
dramatically rarer. Going from 2σ to 4σ cuts the probability of being that far out by a factor of
several hundred. A power
law has no such scale. Going from $5 billion to $10 billion costs the same factor of 5 as
going from $40 billion to $80 billion. This is why the tail never runs out, and why the
richest person or biggest city is never as far-fetched as a normal model says.

> A power law trades size for count at a fixed rate: double the value and the probability
> falls by `2ᵃ`, wherever you are on the scale. The exponent `a` is the whole story.
