The charts so far compare categories or relate two variables. These three show the
**distribution** of one continuous variable: where its values fall.

## Histogram or CDF

Slide 27 draws one data set both ways, with the rule:

> Histograms are better for displaying peaks, CDFs for showing tails.

| chart | what you read off it | weakness |
| --- | --- | --- |
| histogram | where the data is most common, how many peaks, rough shape | tails are bars too short to see |
| CDF | any percentile: read across from 0.9 to find the 90th | peaks become a steep section, easy to miss |

A heavy tail barely registers on a histogram. On a CDF it is the slow final climb toward 1,
and you can read off exactly what fraction lies beyond any value.

## Bin size matters

A histogram depends on a choice the data does not make: how wide the bins are. Slide 28
draws the same values with 5, 10 and 100 bins, and then the same at a smaller sample size.

- **Too few bins** smooth away real structure: a two-peaked distribution can look like one.
- **Too many bins** for the amount of data, and each bin holds a handful of points; the chart
  becomes noise, with spikes and gaps that are just sampling luck.

The right number grows with the amount of data. Try a few and look for the shape that stays
stable. A CDF has no bins, which is one more reason to check it alongside the histogram.

## Box and whisker plots

A box plot "concisely shows the range and quartiles" of a distribution (slide 29):

- the **box** runs from the first quartile to the third, so it holds the middle half;
- the **line** inside it is the median;
- the **whiskers** reach toward the extremes, with outliers often drawn as separate points.

Its strength is compactness. Slide 29 places one box per hour of the day side by side, so
you can compare twenty-four distributions at once, which twenty-four histograms would not
allow. Its weakness is the same compactness: two peaks inside the box are invisible.

> To show one distribution, use a histogram for peaks and a CDF for tails, and try several bin
> sizes. To compare many distributions side by side, use box plots.
