Everything you observe in data is a mixture of two things: a **signal**, the real effect you
are after, and **noise**, the variation that would occur anyway. The lecture's point is that
noise is usually much larger than people expect.

> It is hard to measure "signal to noise" ratio, because much of what you see is just
> variance.

## Stock market investors

Suppose you want to rank investors by skill. You look at their returns and sort them. But
performance has high variance, so it is difficult to determine **how much of the observed
difference is due to skill versus luck**. The best return this year may belong to somebody
who was merely lucky, and the worst to somebody who was merely unlucky.

## The 0.300 hitter

A batting average of 0.300 means a 30% success rate. A 0.300 hitter over 500 at-bats is
regarded as consistent. But the slide shows simulations of a hitter whose *true* ability is
exactly 0.300:

| Season result | Chance for a real 0.300 hitter |
| --- | --- |
| 0.275 or below | 11% |
| 0.325 or above | 11% |

So one season in nine, that hitter looks like a 0.275 player, and one in nine like a 0.325
player, and nothing about their ability has changed. The slide's question is the right one:
*good or bad season, or lucky or unlucky?*

You can see the size of the effect from the mathematics. For 500 attempts at a 30% rate, the
standard deviation of the observed average is about 0.02, and 0.275 is roughly 1.2
deviations below 0.300. That is well within ordinary wobble.

## The misconception

We read results as if each number were the true value. It is not: it is **one draw** from a
distribution. Before explaining a difference, ask how big a difference chance alone could
produce, using the standard deviation from the previous concept as the yardstick.

This matters directly in the next concept, where the numbers being compared are the scores of
your own models.

> A difference means something only when it is large compared with the variation you would
> see anyway.
