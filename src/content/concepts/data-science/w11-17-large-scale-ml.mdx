If you have a billion records, the obvious thing to do is train on all of them. Slide 6
explains why that has historically not worked well, in three points.

## Few parameters, nothing to gain

A model's parameters are what it learns. A linear regression on five features has six
numbers to choose. A few thousand examples pin those down closely, and the next billion only
tighten estimates that were already tight. The slide: models with few parameters cannot
really benefit from large numbers of examples.

To gain from more data a model needs room to learn something more, which means more
parameters.

## The running time must be near linear

Week 10's normal equation was slow once the number of features grew. The same arithmetic
applies to the number of records `n`.

| running time | 1,000 records | 1 billion records |
| --- | --- | --- |
| linear in `n` | 1,000 steps | 1 billion steps: seconds |
| `n²` | 1 million steps | 10¹⁸ steps: decades |

Any method that compares each record with every other is off the table. On big data the
slide's rule holds: algorithmic complexity must be near linear.

## Big matrices had better be sparse

A table of 100 million users by 1 million products has 10¹⁴ cells, far beyond any memory.
It is workable only because nearly every cell is empty, since each user has touched a few
products. A **sparse** matrix stores the non-empty cells only. If the matrix were dense, it
could not even be written down.

## The exception

The slide ends: huge foundation neural network models are the exception here. They have
billions of parameters, so they do keep improving with more data, and they are trained with
methods whose cost is linear in the data. They meet the first two conditions by design.

## Many small models

Slide 7 gives another way to spend big data. Big data on all your customers translates to
**modest data on each of many individuals**.

A retailer with 500 million purchase records and 10 million customers has, on average, 50
purchases per person. That is a small data set, 10 million times over. **Personalisation**
means training a small model for each individual, and the slide notes it is only possible
with big data: you need the enormous total to have even a little on each person.

So the same data supports two different uses. One huge model can describe everyone, or many
small models can each describe someone. A simple model fitted to everybody uses neither
well.

> Data only helps a model with room to learn from it. On big data, favour algorithms that
> are near linear, representations that are sparse, and either a very large model or very
> many small ones.
