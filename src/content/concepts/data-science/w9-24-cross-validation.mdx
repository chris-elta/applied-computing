The previous concepts assume you have enough data to lock 20% away. Often you do not.
Slide 87 offers **cross-validation**.

## k-fold cross-validation

> Train on (k − 1)/k of the data, evaluate on the rest, then repeat, and average.

Concretely, with `k = 5` and 500 examples:

1. cut the data into 5 **folds** of 100;
2. train on folds 2–5 (400 examples), evaluate on fold 1;
3. train on folds 1 and 3–5, evaluate on fold 2;
4. …and so on, until each fold has been held out once;
5. average the 5 scores.

Every example is used for evaluation exactly once and for training `k − 1` times. No data is
wasted, and no model is ever scored on data it trained on.

Slide 87 points out the bonus: "you get a variance as to the accuracy of your model!" Five
scores, not one, tell you how much the result depends on which cases happened to be held out.
Accuracy of 0.80 ± 0.01 is a different claim from 0.80 ± 0.15.

The limiting case is **leave-one-out** cross-validation: `k` equals the number of examples, so
each model trains on everything but one case. It uses the data most fully, and costs one model
per example.

## Amplifying small evaluation sets

Slide 88 gives three more tricks for when labelled data is scarce:

| trick | idea |
| --- | --- |
| create negative examples | when positives are rare, a random unlabelled case is very likely negative |
| perturb real examples | add noise to real cases to make similar synthetic ones |
| give partial credit | score by how far a prediction is from the boundary, not just which side |

The lecture notes add the standard tools for **class imbalance**: undersampling the majority
class, oversampling or generating synthetic minority examples (such as SMOTE), and giving
minority examples higher weight.

## Scoring hard problems easier

Slide 89: with many classes, the exact-match rate can be so low that it is discouraging and
misleading, because real improvements do not show. The **top-k success rate** gives credit if
the right label is among the model's first `k` guesses. Choose `k` "so that real improvements
can be recognised": large enough that the rate moves, small enough that it still means
something.

> When data is short, cross-validate: rotate which fold is held out, average the scores, and
> read their spread as a measure of uncertainty. When the task is hard, score it so that
> progress is visible.
