The lecture's example: the number of police active in a precinct correlates strongly with the
local crime rate. Does policing cause crime? Obviously not. Cities send more officers where
crime is already high. The data shows the two moving together; it cannot show which is the
driver, or whether either is.

## Four explanations for A and B correlating

Whenever two things `A` and `B` are correlated, one of these holds:

| Explanation | Example |
| --- | --- |
| `A` causes `B` | smoking raises mortality |
| `B` causes `A` | crime rates cause police numbers to rise |
| `C` causes both | hot weather drives ice-cream sales and swimming |
| Spurious correlation | butter production in Bangladesh tracks a stock index |

The last row is the slide's joke. Search through enough pairs of unrelated series and some
will line up by luck. A short series with a shared trend is especially easy to match.

## What to do about it

Correlation is still valuable: you can use it to predict without knowing why. Trouble comes
when you act on it. If you want to *change* `B` by changing `A`, you need a causal claim,
and the data alone cannot provide it.

Habits to build:

- **Name the reverse arrow and the third factor** before believing a causal story. Both are
  usually easy to find.
- **Be suspicious of sifting.** The more pairs you test, the more spurious ones appear.
- **Look for interventions.** Randomised experiments, where you set `A` yourself, break
  the link to any hidden `C`.

The lecture ends the slide with a dry remark: social scientists are very good at not
ascribing causality to correlations. It is a discipline worth copying. The next concepts look
at one attempt to use timing to say more.

> A correlation is evidence that something links two variables. It is silent on what.
