Permutation tests need random shuffles. Simulations need random samples from a given
distribution. Slide 30 asks for an algorithm to draw **uniformly random points from a circle**,
and warns that the problem is *more subtle than it looks*.

## The tempting answer

Every point in a circle has a radius `r` and an angle `a`. So pick `r` uniformly between 0 and
the radius, pick `a` uniformly around the circle, and plot the point.

The slide's picture shows the result: **too many points near the centre.**

The reason is in red on the slide: **the inner half circle is smaller than the outer half!**
Take a circle of radius 1. Half the draws have `r < 0.5`, so half the points land in the inner
disc of radius 0.5. But that disc's area is `π × 0.5² = π/4`, a quarter of the whole circle's
`π`. Half the points crowd into a quarter of the area, so the centre is twice as dense as it
should be, and the edge too sparse.

Uniform in `r` is not uniform in area. There is more room far from the centre.

## The fix: rejection sampling

From the slide: **independently sampling `x` and `y` gives points uniform in the box, so
discarding those outside the circle leaves a uniform distribution.**

1. Draw `x` and `y` uniformly between −1 and 1. The point is uniform in the square.
2. If `x² + y² > 1`, throw it away and try again.
3. Otherwise, keep it.

Every patch of the square is equally likely, so every patch of the circle inside it is too. The
cost is the discards. The circle takes `π/4 ≈ 79%` of the square, so about one draw in five is
wasted.

## Any distribution in one dimension

For a single variable there is a general method. From slide 32: **convert the distribution to
its cumulative distribution (CDF). Selecting a probability `p` in `[0, 1]` then maps to a value
in the CDF.**

1. Draw `p` uniformly between 0 and 1.
2. Find the value `x` where the CDF reaches `p`: `F(x) = p`.
3. Return `x`.

For a discrete example, take outcomes A, B and C with probabilities 0.5, 0.3 and 0.2. The CDF
rises in steps:

| outcome | probability | CDF after it | `p` that lands here |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | 0.5 | 0.5 | 0 to 0.5 |
| B | 0.3 | 0.8 | 0.5 to 0.8 |
| C | 0.2 | 1.0 | 0.8 to 1 |

Each outcome gets a band of `p` exactly as wide as its probability, so a uniform `p` picks it
with exactly that probability. For the normal curve on the slide, `p = 0.5` maps to the mean,
and `p` near 0 or 1 maps far out into the tails. The steep middle of the CDF takes up a wide
range of `p`, which is why values near the mean come up most often.

> Choose randomness in a space where uniform really means uniform: the square, not the radius;
> the CDF's probability, not the variable itself.
