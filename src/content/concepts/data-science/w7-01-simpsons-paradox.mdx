A survey of high-school students plots hours spent studying for an exam against the final
grade. The cloud of points slopes clearly downwards, with `r = −0.798`. The slide asks
*what's going on here?* Does studying make you do worse?

## The hidden variable

Colour the same points by **course** and the picture changes. Each course forms its own
small cloud, and **inside every course the trend runs upwards**: more study, higher grade.

The downward slope comes from how the courses sit relative to each other:

| course (from the plot) | typical hours | typical grade |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Physical Education | few | high |
| Art, Social Studies | middle | middle |
| English, Algebra | many | lower |

Hard courses demand more study *and* produce lower grades. Pool them with easy courses and
the course difference swamps the study effect inside each one. The overall correlation is
real arithmetic, but it answers a question nobody asked.

## The definition

From the slide: **Simpson's paradox** is when *an association between two variables in a
population emerges, disappears or reverses when the population is divided into subgroups*.

It is named after Edward Simpson, who described it in 1951. It is called a paradox because
it seems impossible that every part points one way and the whole points the other. It is not
a contradiction, though. The overall figure is an average of the groups, weighted by how
many points each group has, and changing the weights can move an average anywhere.

## What it means for analysis

- **A pooled correlation can mislead.** Before you trust a trend, ask whether a third
  variable splits the data into groups that differ on both axes.
- **The split is not always the right answer either.** Which view is correct depends on the
  question. To ask whether *studying helps a given student in a given course*, compare within
  courses. To describe the school as a whole, the pooled figure is a fair summary.
- **This is a confounder at work,** the same idea as week 3's warning that correlation is not
  causation. Course difficulty drives both study time and grade.

This is why the week opens here. Before asking whether a pattern is *significant*, you have to
ask whether it is the pattern you think it is.

> An association in the whole can vanish or reverse in every part. Look for the variable that
> splits the data before you interpret a trend.
