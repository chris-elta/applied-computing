Women's heights are roughly normal. Men's heights are roughly normal. Measure everyone in a
city and plot the combined histogram, and you get a bell — and that bell is **not** normal.

## Two ways a bell can fool you

The slide gives two cases.

**Fat tails.** **Not all bell-shaped distributions are normal (e.g. stock returns are log
normal with fat tails).** Daily stock returns look like a bell, but very large moves, crashes
in particular, happen far more often than a normal distribution allows. A normal model
might say a move of 5σ happens once in thousands of years. Markets produce them every
decade or two. Anyone using the 68–95–99.7 rule on returns underestimates risk at exactly
the moments it matters.

**Mixtures.** **Mixtures of normal distributions are not normal, like full population
heights.** The slide's chart shows women's heights peaking near 162 cm and men's near
176 cm. The combined population curve (the blue line) is wider and flatter at the top than
either. If the two groups were further apart it would have two humps.

## Why mixtures are everywhere

The slide's closing point: **statistical tests exist to establish whether data is drawn from
a normal distribution, but populations are generally mixtures of multiple distributions:
height, weight, IQ.**

Most populations you measure are made of subgroups: ages, sexes, regions, customer types.
Each might be close to normal on its own. Pooled together they are not, and the summary
statistics of the pool can describe nobody. A mean height of 169 cm for the city describes
neither group's typical member.

## What to do about it

- **Plot before you assume.** A histogram shows two humps or a long tail immediately.
- **Split by known groups** and check each one. If the groups look normal and the whole does
  not, you have found a mixture.
- **Test, if it matters.** Formal tests of normality exist. They answer the question the
  shape alone cannot.

This is the general warning from the start of the week in its most common form. The normal
is a reasonable start, as the previous concept says, but only a start.

> A bell shape is not proof of normality. Look for heavy tails and hidden subgroups before
> trusting the normal's tidy percentages.
