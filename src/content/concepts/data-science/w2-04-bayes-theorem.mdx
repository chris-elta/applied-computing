The previous concept ended on a warning: `P(A|B)` and `P(B|A)` are different questions.
Bayes' theorem is how you get from one to the other.

## The theorem

The slide states it as a way to **update our likelihood estimate of `A` in response to
knowledge of `B`**:

```
P(A|B) = P(B|A) × P(A) / P(B)
```

You start with a belief, `P(A)`, the **prior**. Evidence `B` arrives. The theorem gives the
revised belief, `P(A|B)`, the **posterior**.

## Why it works

The proof slide is short, and it is worth following because it reveals there is nothing
mysterious. The definition of conditional probability gives two statements about the same
joint event:

```
P(A ∩ B) = P(A|B) × P(B)
P(A ∩ B) = P(B|A) × P(A)
```

Both equal the same thing, so set them equal and divide by `P(B)`. The theorem is the
definition of conditioning, written twice.

## A worked example

A condition affects 1% of people. A test catches it in 90% of those who have it, but wrongly
flags 9% of those who do not. You test positive. What now?

Picture 10,000 people:

| Group | People | Test positive |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Have it (1%) | 100 | 90 |
| Do not (99%) | 9,900 | 891 |

That is 981 positives, of whom 90 are real. So `P(condition | positive) = 90/981 ≈ 9%`.
The formula agrees: `0.9 × 0.01 / 0.0981 ≈ 0.092`.

## The misconception

The intuition that a positive means a 90% chance is very strong, and wrong. It confuses
`P(positive | condition)`, which the test's makers report, with `P(condition | positive)`,
which is what *you* need.

They differ because of the **prior**. When the condition is rare, even a good test produces
more false alarms than true ones, since there are so many more people who do not have it.
The base rate is not a detail; it dominates the answer.

The same trap appears wherever you evaluate a detector on rare events — fraud, faults,
attacks. Conditional probability told you the two directions differ; Bayes tells you by how
much.

> To update a belief you need the evidence and where you started. Forgetting the prior is
> the classic error.
