Here is the recipe, built from the picture. Draw the two means as a horizontal and a vertical
line through the scatter plot. That splits the plane into four quadrants.

- Points in the **upper-right and lower-left** quadrants lie above both means or below both.
  They agree, and vote for a positive relationship.
- Points in the **other two** quadrants disagree, and vote for a negative one.

Turn each vote into a number by multiplying the point's two distances from the means, `(Xᵢ − X̄)`
times `(Yᵢ − Ȳ)`. Agreement gives a positive product, disagreement a negative one, and points
far from the centre vote louder. Add the votes up.

## The formula

```
        Σ (Xᵢ − X̄)(Yᵢ − Ȳ)
r = ─────────────────────────────────
    √Σ(Xᵢ − X̄)² · √Σ(Yᵢ − Ȳ)²
```

The numerator is the **covariance** (up to a constant). As the slide notes, it determines
the **sign but not the scale**: measure height in millimetres rather than metres and it grows
a thousandfold without the relationship changing. The denominator divides out the spread of
each variable (the two square roots are, up to a constant, the standard deviations from last
week), so that `r` always lands between −1 and 1 whatever the units.

## Worked examples

The steps are always the same: find both means, take each point's two distances from them,
multiply them pairwise, then divide by the two spreads.

### A positive correlation

Five students: hours studied `X` and quiz score `Y`.

| `X` | `Y` | `X − X̄` | `Y − Ȳ` | product | `(X − X̄)²` | `(Y − Ȳ)²` |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 2 | −2 | −2 | 4 | 4 | 4 |
| 2 | 4 | −1 | 0 | 0 | 1 | 0 |
| 3 | 5 | 0 | 1 | 0 | 0 | 1 |
| 4 | 4 | 1 | 0 | 0 | 1 | 0 |
| 5 | 5 | 2 | 1 | 2 | 4 | 1 |
| **Sum** | | | | **6** | **10** | **6** |

The means are `X̄ = 3` and `Ȳ = 4`. Then

```
r = 6 / (√10 · √6) = 6 / √60 ≈ 6 / 7.746 ≈ 0.77
```

The two extreme points (1, 2) and (5, 5) supply most of the numerator, because they sit
farthest from the means. The middle points, close to a mean in one coordinate, barely vote.

### A negative correlation

Four people: evening screen time in hours `X` and a sleep-quality score `Y`.

| `X` | `Y` | `X − X̄` | `Y − Ȳ` | product | `(X − X̄)²` | `(Y − Ȳ)²` |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 8 | −1.5 | 3 | −4.5 | 2.25 | 9 |
| 2 | 6 | −0.5 | 1 | −0.5 | 0.25 | 1 |
| 3 | 5 | 0.5 | 0 | 0 | 0.25 | 0 |
| 4 | 1 | 1.5 | −4 | −6 | 2.25 | 16 |
| **Sum** | | | | **−11** | **5** | **26** |

With `X̄ = 2.5` and `Ȳ = 5`:

```
r = −11 / (√5 · √26) = −11 / √130 ≈ −11 / 11.40 ≈ −0.96
```

Every point lies in the upper-left or lower-right quadrant, so every product is negative
or zero, and the line fits closely.

### Perfect pattern, zero correlation

Take `X = −2, −1, 0, 1, 2` and `Y = X²`, so `Y = 4, 1, 0, 1, 4`. The relationship is exact,
yet `X̄ = 0`, `Ȳ = 2`, and the products `(X − X̄)(Y − Ȳ)` are

```
(−2)(2) + (−1)(−1) + (0)(−2) + (1)(−1) + (2)(2) = −4 + 1 + 0 − 1 + 4 = 0
```

so `r = 0`. The left half of the parabola votes negative and the right half votes positive,
and they cancel. Pearson looks only for a straight line, and `Y` depends on `X` completely
here without one.

### Changing units

Multiply every `X` in the first example by 1000 (a change of units). The numerator becomes 6000 and `√Σ(X − X̄)²` becomes `1000·√10`, so

```
r = 6000 / (1000 · √10 · √6) ≈ 0.77
```

The covariance grew a thousandfold. `r` did not move.

## What real values look like

The lecture lists correlations from real studies:

| Pair | `r` |
| --- | --- |
| SAT scores and freshman GPA | 0.47 |
| SAT scores and economic status | 0.42 |
| Income and coronary disease | −0.717 |
| Smoking and mortality rate | 0.716 |
| Video games and violent behaviour | 0.19 |

Notice how rarely anything reaches 0.9. In the social and medical sciences, values around
0.4 to 0.7 are typical of relationships people treat as real, and 0.19 is small. What those
numbers *mean* is the subject of the next concept.

## The limit

Pearson's `r` measures how well the points fit a **straight line**. A relationship that is
perfectly regular but curved can score low, and one extreme outlier can drag `r` a long way.
Later this week you meet a rank-based alternative for exactly those cases.

> Pearson correlation is the average agreement of two variables about their means, scaled
> so the units cancel.
