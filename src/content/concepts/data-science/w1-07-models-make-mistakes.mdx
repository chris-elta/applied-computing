During a live-streamed match involving Inverness Caledonian Thistle in Scotland, an
AI-powered camera designed to track the ball repeatedly focused on the linesman's bald head
instead of the ball.

It is a funny clip, and it also states something structural about ML systems.

## Mistakes you cannot list in advance

Traditional software fails in ways you can usually trace to a line of code. A model fails in
ways that are **often unexpected**, and they are **hard to foresee and capture because there
is no spec**. Nobody had "the ball may look like a head" on a list.

## What "correct" means

That raises a question the slides pose outright: *what does it mean to be correct?* For a
model you cannot check every case against a rule. You can only evaluate whether it works
**well enough, on average, on some test data**.

Two words in that sentence do the work:

- **Enough** — the standard is acceptable, not perfect.
- **On average** — some individual predictions will be wrong, and the score does not say
  which.

## Living with it

The next slide states the consequence: the system must be able to **tolerate some incorrect
predictions**, and be aware of how it might **influence the world**. Two slides on
*complexity* follow as pictures; they make the point visually that a real system has many
more moving parts than the model alone.

So the design question changes. It is no longer "how do I make it never wrong?" but "what
happens when it is wrong, and how bad is that?"

> An average score tells you how often you will be wrong. It does not tell you how badly, or
> where.
