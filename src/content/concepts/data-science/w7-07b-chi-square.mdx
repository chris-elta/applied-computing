Simpson's paradox opened this week with students who study gym very differently from algebra.
That raises a question the slide puts as **do categories really matter?** Does knowing which
course a student is in, or which group a person belongs to, change the distribution of what
you are measuring?

The t-test and the KS test compare numbers. When both variables are **categories** (sex and
party, course and pass/fail, device and whether someone bought), the tool is the
**chi-square test**. From the slide, it establishes whether *different population labels exhibit
the same distribution*.

## Step 1 · Build a contingency table

A **contingency table** counts how many observations fall in each combination of categories. The
slide's example asks whether party preference differs by sex:

| | Democrats | Republicans | total |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| male | 23 | 31 | 54 |
| female | 28 | 22 | 50 |
| total | 51 | 53 | 104 |

Men lean Republican and women lean Democrat. Is that more than chance?

## Step 2 · Compute the test statistic

The null hypothesis is that sex and party are unrelated. If so, each row should split the same
way as the whole table: 51 of 104 people, about 49%, are Democrats, so about 49% of the men should
be too. That gives each cell an **expected count**:

```
E = row total × column total / grand total
```

Then compare observed with expected, cell by cell:

```
χ² = Σ (Observed − Expected)² / Expected
```

| cell | `O` | `E` | `(O − E)² / E` |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| male, Democrat | 23 | `54 × 51 / 104 ≈ 26.48` | 0.458 |
| male, Republican | 31 | `54 × 53 / 104 ≈ 27.52` | 0.440 |
| female, Democrat | 28 | `50 × 51 / 104 ≈ 24.52` | 0.494 |
| female, Republican | 22 | `50 × 53 / 104 ≈ 25.48` | 0.475 |
| **sum** | | | **`χ² ≈ 1.87`** |

Squaring stops positive and negative gaps from cancelling. Dividing by `E` judges each gap
relative to the size of the cell, so being 3 off matters more in a cell of 10 than in a cell of
1,000.

## Step 3 · Evaluate against the distribution

Under the null hypothesis, `χ²` follows a **chi-square distribution**. That is the skewed curve on
the slides, which starts at 0 and has a long right tail. Its shape depends on the degrees of
freedom, `(rows − 1) × (columns − 1)`, which is 1 here.

The critical value for `df = 1` at `α = 0.05` is 3.84. `1.87 < 3.84`, so **the difference is not
significant**. A sample of 104 people splitting this way is quite consistent with sex and party
being unrelated.

## Where it goes wrong

The third slide lists four issues:

| issue | what to watch |
| --- | --- |
| **degrees of freedom** | More categories means more cells, and a larger `χ²` by chance alone. Use the table row for your `df`. |
| **counts, not fractions** | "100%" is easy to get from one person. Run the test on raw counts, and the slides ask for more than 10 in each cell. |
| **significance level** | Is 0.05 the right threshold? With many tables, Bonferroni applies. |
| **sampling** | The test assumes random, independent observations. One person surveyed twice, or a whole household counted separately, breaks it. |

> The chi-square test asks whether category labels change a distribution. Build the counts,
> compare each with what independence predicts, and sum the squared surprises.
