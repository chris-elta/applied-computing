If noise hides differences, the natural response is to reduce it. The lecture lists four
methods.

## The four methods

**1. Choose the lower-variance option.** The slide's illustration: walking to class is slower
than taking the bus, but the **arrival times have lower variance**. If being on time matters
more than being fast, the slower option is better. Optimising the average is not always the
goal.

**2. Repeat the experiment.** Repeating an experiment multiple times reduces variance.
**k-fold cross-validation** is the standard example: instead of trusting one train/test
split, you evaluate on `k` different ones and average.

**3. Sample properly.** Proper random and deterministic sampling means that the data you
evaluate on is chosen by a fair, reproducible procedure, not by convenience. Deterministic
means you can repeat it exactly.

**4. Eliminate outliers, if justifiable.** Extreme values inflate variance. Removing them
reduces it, but only if there is a good reason. Deleting inconvenient points because they
spoil the result would be dishonest.

## A worked idea

Say one test split gives 82% and another gives 78%. If you report the first, you say 82; if
the second, 78. Averaging five splits might give 80% with the runs ranging over a few points.
Now the estimate does not depend on a lucky draw, and you can see the spread too.

## Choosing among them

The methods target different sources of noise: the choice of process (1), the choice of data
split (2 and 3), and extreme values (4). In practice you combine them, and the fourth carries
a duty to explain why the points were dropped.

> You cannot remove noise from the world, but you can make your measurement of it steadier.
