A single score says how good a model is. It does not say *why*, or where it fails, or whether
the score is even real. Slide 81 put it this way: formal evaluation metrics reduce models to a
few summary statistics, and many problems can be hidden by statistics.

## Build an evaluation environment

Slide 82 sketches an **evaluation environment**: a harness that runs any model against the
test data and produces a standard report. Building it once, before the models, means every
model is judged the same way and comparing them is honest.

Slide 83 lists what a good one produces:

| output | why |
| --- | --- |
| **error distributions**, not only right or wrong | how close was each prediction? |
| a report with multiple plots, generated automatically | so you actually read it, every time |
| relevant summary statistics | to gauge quality at a glance |

## Error histograms

Slide 84 returns to dating documents by decade, the example from the confusion-matrix
concept. It plots a histogram of each model's error, the predicted decade minus the true one:

- **a random model** produces a wide, flat histogram: its errors are spread across two
  centuries;
- **a naive Bayes model** produces a tall peak at zero, with errors falling off quickly on
  either side.

The histogram shows what a single accuracy figure cannot: that the second model is usually
within a decade or two when it is wrong. The lecture notes add that the shape matters too. An
**asymmetric** error distribution, consistently predicting too early, say, points to
**systematic bias** or imbalance in the data.

## Stratify the results

Slide 85 shows a results table that breaks performance down by **topic** and by
**difficulty**, measured as document length. The lecture notes call this stratifying the
errors "by class, document length, or context" to identify weak spots.

A model with a good average can be excellent on long documents and hopeless on short ones.
That is not visible in the average, and it is exactly what you need to know: either to
improve the model where it is weak, or to tell users where not to trust it.

## Catching the sins

This is also how the "sins in evaluation" from the previous concept get caught. A bug or a
leak tends to produce errors that *look wrong*: a spike of perfect predictions on one topic,
or a distribution with a strange shape. You only see that if the report shows distributions,
not just averages.

> Build the evaluation harness before the models. Have it report how far off each prediction
> was, plot the distribution of errors, and break results down by kind of case, because
> averages hide both weaknesses and bugs.
