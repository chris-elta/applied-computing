Computers find patterns in data quickly. Run enough comparisons over a large table and
something will always stand out. The slides put the hard question plainly: **when is a pattern
significant?**

## Two questions about every pattern

The slides contrast two professions:

| who | asks | the worry |
| --- | --- | --- |
| statisticians | is this observation **significant**? | it might be chance |
| data miners | is this observation **interesting**? | it might not matter |

Both questions matter, and they are different. A pattern can be interesting and not
significant: a striking trend in a sample too small to rule out luck. A pattern can be
significant and not interesting: a real difference too tiny to act on. This week covers both,
starting with significance and returning to *how big* in the effect-size concept.

Strong correlations in big data sets can look *obviously* significant. The slides warn that
the effects are often more subtle than that. Simpson's paradox, in the previous concept, is one
way an obvious-looking pattern turns out to be something else.

## A classic hard case

Drug trials are where this question carries the most weight. From the slide:

| | cured | patients | cure rate |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| drug A | 19 | 34 | 56% |
| drug B | 14 | 21 | 67% |

**Is B better than A?** B's rate is eleven points higher. But these are small groups. If both
drugs worked equally well, how often would two groups this size differ by eleven points
anyway? With a few dozen patients each, quite often. One or two patients either way changes
the rates noticeably.

The slide notes that approval of a new drug rests on this kind of analysis and can add or
subtract billions from a drug company's value. The answer has to come from a method, not from
whether the number looks big.

## What the rest of the week supplies

That method is **hypothesis testing**: assume nothing is going on, then ask how surprising the
data would be. The following concepts build it up:

1. state a *null hypothesis* that the difference is chance;
2. compute a *p-value*: how unlikely a result this extreme is under that hypothesis;
3. choose a test that fits the data, such as a t-test, a KS test or a permutation test;
4. separately, measure how *large* the effect is.

> Finding a pattern is the easy part. Before acting on it, ask two things: could chance alone
> produce it, and is it big enough to matter?
