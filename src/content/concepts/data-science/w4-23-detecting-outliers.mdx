The slide opens with a story: **the largest reported dinosaur vertebra is 50% larger than all
the others, presumably a data error.** One number far from the rest is a reason to look, not
yet a reason to act.

## Finding them

- **Look critically at the maximum and minimum values for all variables.** A height of 7,000 cm
  or an age of −3 shows up immediately.
- **Normally distributed data should not have large outliers, `k σ` from the mean.** For
  large `k`, a point is so improbable under the normal assumption that a mistake is the more
  likely explanation. This is where the z-scores from earlier come in: a z-score of 8 is an
  alarm.

## When you cannot just look

**Visually, it is easy to detect outliers, but only in low dimensional spaces.** With many
variables you cannot plot the data. The slide's answer: **think of it as an unsupervised
learning problem, like clustering.** Group the points, and **points far from their cluster
centre are good candidates for outliers.**

## Detecting is not deleting

The slide's red line is: **fix why you have an outlier. Don't just delete.** An outlier is a
symptom. It may come from a unit mix-up, a placeholder like 9999, a real rare event or a
different population. Finding out which tells you whether to fix it, flag it or keep it. The
next concept covers when deletion helps and when it hurts.

> An outlier is a question: why is this value here? Answer it before you decide what to do
> with the value.
