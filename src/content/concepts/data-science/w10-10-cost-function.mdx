Slide 17 draws three lines with different `θ₀` and `θ₁`. Which fits best? We need a number that
measures fit, and then we pick the parameters that make it smallest.

## The idea

From slide 18:

> Choose θ₀, θ₁ so that hθ(x) is close to y for our training examples (x, y).

"Close" needs a definition. For each training example, the error is the prediction minus the
truth, `hθ(x⁽ⁱ⁾) − y⁽ⁱ⁾`. Squaring it makes every error positive and punishes big misses most.
Averaging over the training set gives one number.

## The cost function

```
J(θ₀, θ₁) = (1 / 2m) Σᵢ₌₁ᵐ (hθ(x⁽ⁱ⁾) − y⁽ⁱ⁾)²
```

and the goal is

```
minimize J(θ₀, θ₁)  over θ₀, θ₁
```

Read it from the inside out:

| piece | meaning |
| --- | --- |
| `hθ(x⁽ⁱ⁾) − y⁽ⁱ⁾` | how far off the line is on example i |
| `( … )²` | squared, so the sign goes and big misses dominate |
| `Σᵢ₌₁ᵐ` | added up over every example |
| `1 / 2m` | averaged, and halved |

This is called the **squared error function**, or **mean squared error**: the same MSE as in
week 9's numeric-error concept, apart from the extra half.

## Why the ½?

The course notes explain: the mean is halved "as a convenience for the computation of gradient
descent". When you differentiate the square, a factor of 2 comes down, and the ½ cancels it.
Halving does not change *which* θ is best, because the minimum of `J` is in the same place
whether or not you halve it.

## A worked example

The notes' data and guess from the previous concept: points (0, 4), (1, 7), (2, 7), (3, 8), and
`hθ(x) = 2 + 2x`.

| x | y | hθ(x) | error | squared |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | 4 | 2 | −2 | 4 |
| 1 | 7 | 4 | −3 | 9 |
| 2 | 7 | 6 | −1 | 1 |
| 3 | 8 | 8 | 0 | 0 |
| | | | **sum** | **14** |

With `m = 4`: `J = 14 / (2 × 4) = 1.75`.

Change `θ₀` or `θ₁` and `J` changes. If a line passed through every point, every error would be
zero and `J` would be 0, its smallest possible value.

## The shift in viewpoint

`hθ(x)` is a function of `x`, for fixed parameters: give it a house, get a price. `J(θ₀, θ₁)` is
a function of the **parameters**, for fixed data: give it a line, get a score. Learning happens in
the second picture. The next two concepts draw it.

> The cost J(θ₀, θ₁) is half the mean squared error of the line's predictions on the training
> set. Learning means finding the θ₀ and θ₁ that make it smallest.
