Borda's method on the last slide gave first place 1 point, second place 2 and so on. That
looks like the only natural choice. It is one of many, and it carries an assumption.

## What linear weights say

With scores 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, moving up one place is always worth exactly one point. The slide
states the assumption: **linear position weights make sense when we have equal confidence
across all positions.** The gap between 1st and 2nd is treated as just as real, and just as
well judged, as the gap between 3rd and 4th.

## Are you that confident?

Rank ten films you have seen. Your favourite and your least favourite come to mind
immediately. Is the film you put 5th really better than the one you put 6th? Ask yourself
tomorrow and you may swap them.

The slide says the same: **we presumably trust our distinctions among the best/worst more
than the middle elements.** Voters are fairly reliable at the ends and unreliable in the
middle. Linear weights ignore that. A coin-flip between 5th and 6th moves the total by a
full point, exactly as much as a firm judgement between 1st and 2nd.

## The alternative: weights from a normal curve

The slide suggests **normally distributed weights**, and the figure shows how to build them.
It is a bell curve cut by vertical lines into slices of equal area. Look at where the lines
fall on the horizontal axis.

- In the **middle** the curve is tall, so the lines are packed close together.
- In the **tails** the curve is low, so the lines are far apart.

Use the positions of those lines as the scores for ranks 1, 2, 3 and so on. The middle ranks
get scores that are almost equal, and the top and bottom ranks get scores that are well
separated.

| | step between neighbouring ranks |
| --- | --- |
| Linear weights | The same everywhere |
| Normal weights | Large at the top and bottom, small in the middle |

Swapping two middle items now barely changes a total, and swapping the top two changes it a
lot. The weights carry the confidence.

There is a second way to see it, using week 2. If the underlying quality of the items is
roughly bell-shaped, most items really are close together in the middle, and only a few
stand apart at the ends. Linear scores turn ranks back into evenly spaced qualities. Normal
weights turn them back into something shaped like the qualities probably were.

## Which to use

Neither is correct in general. Linear weights are easier to explain and suit cases where
each position reflects an objective measurement. Normal weights suit subjective rankings of
many items. As with the transcript score, the choice of weights is a claim about your data,
and you should be able to state it.

> Weights encode how much you trust each distinction. If the middle of a ranking is close to
> guesswork, give it small differences.
