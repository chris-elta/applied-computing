Computer scientists do not **naturally appreciate data**. To a programmer it is stuff to run
through a program, and the usual way to test an algorithm is to run it on "random data".

The lecture argues that this habit is the opposite of what data science needs, because
interesting datasets are a **scarce resource** that takes hard work and imagination to obtain.

## Two worldviews

The slides set out the contrast in two parts.

| Scientists | Computer scientists |
| --- | --- |
| Strive to understand a complicated, messy natural world | Build their own clean, organised virtual worlds |
| Nothing is ever completely true or false | Everything is either true or false |
| Data-driven | Algorithm-driven |
| Obsessed with *discovering* things | *Invent* rather than discover |
| Comfortable that data has errors | Not comfortable with errors |

## What this means for you

If your training is in computing, the right-hand column is your default. It is a good default
for writing programs: a sorting routine is correct or it is not.

It becomes a liability when the object of study is the world. A claim about customers, a
football league or taxi journeys is not true or false in the way a theorem is. It is better
or worse supported, and the data behind it will have gaps and mistakes.

So the shift the lecture asks for is not to abandon rigour but to change what rigour looks
like: work out how much you can trust the data, and say how sure you are.

> In mathematics you prove. With data you weigh evidence, and you expect the evidence to be
> imperfect.
