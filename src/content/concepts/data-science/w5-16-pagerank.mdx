A tourist is wandering around Wikipedia. They start on a random page, click a random link on
it, then a random link on the page they land on, and keep going for a very long time. Where
are they likely to be?

They are probably not on an obscure page that nothing links to. They are probably on a page
that many paths lead to. The slide defines PageRank this way: **PageRank(v,G) corresponds to
the probability that a random walk on G ends up at vertex v.** A page is important if a
random walker is often found there.

## The formula

```
PR_j(v) = Σ over links (u,v) of   PR_{j−1}(u) / out-degree(u)
```

In words: to get page `v`'s new score, look at every page `u` that links to `v`. Each such
page passes on its own previous score, divided by the number of links it has. Add up what
arrives.

| symbol | meaning |
| --- | --- |
| `v` | The page we are computing PageRank for |
| `(u,v) ∈ E` | An edge from page `u` to page `v` |
| `PR_{j−1}(u)` | PageRank of `u` from the previous iteration |
| `out-degree(u)` | Number of outgoing links from `u` |

The division comes from the walker. From page `u` they pick one of its links at random. If
`u` has four links, a quarter of the time they come to `v`. So `u` sends a quarter of its
score along each link.

## It is circular, and that is fine

A page's score depends on the scores of the pages linking to it, which depend on the scores
of the pages linking to *them*. The slide calls it a **recursive formula** that **defines an
iterative algorithm, which quickly converges in practice.**

1. **Start** by giving all pages an equal score, such as `1/N` for `N` pages.
2. **In each iteration**, update each page's score using the formula. Pages receive
   fractional contributions from the pages that link to them.
3. **Repeat** until the scores stop changing much. The algorithm has then converged.
4. **After convergence**, the scores are the steady-state probabilities of the random walk.

You begin with a guess everyone knows is wrong, that all pages are equal, and each pass
makes it less wrong.

## Why this beats counting links

Counting incoming links is vote counting again. PageRank weights each link by the score of
the page it comes from. One link from a highly ranked page can outweigh fifty links from
pages nobody visits. Elo handled the same problem for match results.

## All roads lead to Rome

The slide's title continues: **"…then Rome must be an important place."** It then shows the
top Wikipedia pages by PageRank.

- **Over all pages:** Napoleon, George W. Bush, Carl Linnaeus, Jesus, Barack Obama,
  Aristotle and so on.
- **Over links between people only:** George W. Bush, Bill Clinton, William Shakespeare,
  Ronald Reagan and so on.

The lists differ because the graphs differ. Carl Linnaeus, who named a great many species,
is linked from a huge number of species pages. Restrict the graph to people and those links
vanish, and so does he.

The slide calls PageRank **a good feature to measure centrality or importance of
vertices.** Note the word *feature*. PageRank measures how connected a page is in one
particular graph. It does not measure fame, merit or truth. It is a proxy, and which graph
you feed it changes the answer.

> A page is important if important pages link to it. Start with equal scores and keep
> passing them along the links until they settle.
