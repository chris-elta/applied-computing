Logistic regression answers yes or no. Many questions have more than two answers. Slides 13
and 14 give examples: is a movie a comedy, drama, action or documentary? Should an email go
in Work, Friends, Family or Hobby? Is the patient not ill, or has a cold, or flu? Is the
weather sunny, cloudy, rain or snow?

Slide 15 draws the difference. Binary data has two kinds of point. Multi-class data has
three or more, here triangles, squares and crosses.

## Reusing the binary classifier

You do not need a new algorithm. Slide 16 states the idea: build a multi-class classifier out
of several independent binary ones. This is **one-vs-all**, also called one-vs-rest.

With three classes, make three training sets from the same data (slide 17):

| classifier | positive class | negative class |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `hθ⁽¹⁾(x)` | triangles | squares and crosses together |
| `hθ⁽²⁾(x)` | squares | triangles and crosses together |
| `hθ⁽³⁾(x)` | crosses | triangles and squares together |

Each is an ordinary binary problem: "is it this class, or anything else?". Train logistic
regression on each. Classifier `i` then estimates `P(y = i | x; θ)`, and each has its own
decision boundary fencing its class off from the rest.

For `k` classes you train `k` classifiers.

## Predicting

Slide 18 gives the rule. For a new input `x`, run all the classifiers and pick the class
whose classifier gives the highest probability:

```
predicted class = the i that maximises hθ⁽ⁱ⁾(x)
```

Suppose a new point gets:

| classifier | output |
| --- | --- |
| triangles | 0.15 |
| squares | 0.72 |
| crosses | 0.20 |

The prediction is "square". Taking the maximum also settles the awkward regions: a point
that two classifiers both claim, or that none claims with more than 0.5, still gets the label
of the most confident one.

## Two things to notice

The outputs above sum to 1.07, not 1. Each classifier was trained separately and knows
nothing of the others, so nothing makes them add up. That is harmless for choosing a label
and a problem if you need true probabilities. The concept on partition functions deals with
it.

Each binary problem is also unbalanced by construction. With ten equal classes, every
classifier sees one part positive to nine parts negative, so the previous two concepts on
balancing apply.

> One-vs-all turns a `k`-class problem into `k` yes-or-no problems, one per class against
> the rest. Predict the class whose classifier is most confident.
