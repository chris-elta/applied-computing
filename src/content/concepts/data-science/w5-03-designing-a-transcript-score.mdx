The slide sets a task and gives no answer: **propose a statistic to map a student's grade
transcript at BCIT to a single numerical score such that better performance gets a higher
score.**

Try it before reading on. A transcript is a list of courses, each with a grade, a credit
count, a level and a term. What do you do with it?

## There are several reasonable answers

| score | what it rewards | what it costs |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Credit-weighted average (plain GPA) | Doing well overall | Treats every course as equally hard and ignores whether you are improving |
| Difficulty-weighted average | Taking harder, higher-level courses | Someone has to choose the weights, and early-program students are at a disadvantage |
| Recency-weighted average | Improvement | Plays down strong early terms |
| Trimmed average (drop the highest and lowest grades) | Consistency, with one bad day forgiven | Throws data away, and needs enough courses to be fair |
| Average minus a penalty for spread | Being reliably good | Punishes a student for risking a hard elective |
| Score relative to the cohort | Standing among classmates | Needs cohort statistics, and is shaky for small groups |

Every row is defensible, and every row has a cost.

## Same students, different order

Take two students who both average 80%. One took mostly first-year courses and scored
evenly. The other took a heavy third-year load and climbed from 72% to 88%.

- Plain GPA: a tie.
- Difficulty-weighted: the second student is ahead.
- Recency-weighted: the second student is ahead.
- Average minus a spread penalty: the first student is ahead.

Nothing about the students changed between those lines. The formula changed, and the
ranking followed.

## The exercise has no answer key

The question says "better performance" as if everyone agreed what that means. They do not.
Before you can write the formula you have to decide whether you value difficulty,
improvement, consistency or standing. That decision is not a statistical one. It belongs to
whoever will use the score, and for what: a scholarship committee and a co-op employer may
reasonably want different things.

This is the same position BMI was in with its exponent. The choice is hidden inside the
formula, and the output looks equally authoritative whichever way you chose.

So when you design a score, write down what it is meant to reward first. When you read
someone else's, work out from the formula what they decided to reward, and ask who loses
under that choice.

> Choosing a scoring function is choosing what you value. Different reasonable choices
> give different rankings of the same data.
