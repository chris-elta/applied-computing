The average large US city has 165,719 people. The standard deviation is a huge 410,730. Using
the normal distribution, the slide works out that the biggest city ought to be about the size
of Indianapolis, around 780,000 people.

**New York had 8,008,278 people in the 2000 census.**

## How wrong is the normal model?

Put New York on the normal model's scale:

```
z = (8,008,278 − 165,719) / 410,730 ≈ 19
```

The 68–95–99.7 rule stops at 3σ. A 19σ value under a normal distribution is so unlikely
that it would not happen once in the age of the universe. Yet the city exists, and Los
Angeles and Chicago are far beyond 3σ too. The data is not normal. Its upper tail is
enormously heavier.

## Power laws

The slide's definition: power laws are **`p(x) = c x⁻ᵃ`**, for an exponent `a` and a
normalization constant `c`. The key difference from the distributions so far:

> They do not cluster around a mean like a normal distribution, instead having very large
> values rarely but consistently.

| | normal | power law |
| --- | --- | --- |
| typical value | the mean | none, really |
| very large values | effectively never | rare, but always some |
| mean and SD | describe the data well | dragged around by the largest values |

Week 3 met power laws for a different reason, as data to take the log of before analysis.
This week says what the shape is.

## The 80-20 rule

The slide's blue line: power laws **define 80-20 rules: 20% of the X get 80% of the Y.** A few
cities hold a large share of the urban population. A few websites get most of the traffic.
A few customers bring in most of the revenue. The exact split depends on `a`, but the
pattern is the same: the total is dominated by a small number of very large items.

## Where they come from

The slide's explanation: **power laws arise when the rich get richer.** A big city attracts
more jobs, so more people, so more jobs. A popular website is linked to more, so found more,
so linked to more. Growth in proportion to current size compounds differences rather than
averaging them out. That is the opposite of the central limit theorem, where many small
independent effects cancel into a bell.

> If a few values are many standard deviations out and keep turning up, the data is not
> normal. It may be a power law, where there is no typical size and the largest items
> dominate the total.
