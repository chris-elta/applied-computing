It is tempting to hear "data science" and picture someone training a model. The lecture
pushes back on that in the first week.

> Our primary goal here is to learn to think about data: understanding, cleaning,
> visualization — not just building models.

## What is in and what is out

| In the course | Out of the course |
| --- | --- |
| Understanding, cleaning and visualising data | Deep learning |
| Some applied machine learning | Building generative AI models |
| Using generative AI tools, with citation | A course about machine learning as such |

The reasoning is practical. A model can only be as good as the data it was handed and the
question it was asked. Someone who can build any model but cannot tell whether the data is
wrong will produce confident nonsense.

## Where the lecturer's view comes from

The slide on the lecturer's own experience lists the kind of data behind this stance:

- Premier League data, to find exceptional players
- DNS data, to find DNS tunnelling
- DNS data from IoT devices, to find anomalous behaviour
- Community question-answering data
- A startup company focused on CRMs

None of these is "train the biggest model you can". Each starts with a messy source and a
question, and most of the effort goes into working out what the data can and cannot say. The
lecturer states plainly: *this drives what I will teach here.*

## Generative AI

Generative AI is described as "eating the world", including parts of what the lecturer
considers data science. The focus nonetheless stays on how to think correctly about
**mid-scale** data analysis, so that you reach the right understanding on important tasks.
You will discuss and use generative models but not build them.

> Modelling is one step. Deciding what to model, and whether the data can bear it, is the
> job.
