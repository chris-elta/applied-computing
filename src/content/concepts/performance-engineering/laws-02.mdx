Three quantities, one relationship that holds for any queue that isn't growing without
bound:

```
L = λW
```

`L` is the average number of items in the system, `λ` the average arrival rate, and `W`
the average time each item spends inside. Requests in a service, cars on a motorway,
work items on a team — the shape of the system doesn't matter.

## What makes it unusual

Most queueing results demand assumptions: Poisson arrivals, exponential service times,
a particular scheduling discipline. **Little's Law assumes none of that.** It needs only
that the system is stable — that what goes in comes out, and nothing accumulates
forever. That is why it survives contact with real systems where the tidy distributions
never hold.

## Using it in practice

The value is in solving for whichever term you can't measure directly:

- **Sizing a pool** — you know arrival rate and latency, so `L` tells you the
  concurrency you must support. Provision less and the queue grows without limit.
- **Inferring latency** — you can see queue depth and throughput, so `W = L/λ` gives
  the latency you couldn't instrument.
- **Sanity-checking a claim** — if reported throughput, latency and concurrency don't
  multiply out, one of the three is measured wrong.
